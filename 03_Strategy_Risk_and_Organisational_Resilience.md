# 03. Strategy, Risk and Organisational Resilience

**Purpose and reading rule.** This is a director-centred knowledge reference, not a management strategy textbook. A board must decide which objectives are worth pursuing, which risks it will deliberately assume, which losses and harms are unacceptable, what resources can be committed, whether execution is credible, and what new evidence would justify changing course. The integrated governance question is: **Can this entity pursue this strategy, within its lawful authority, mission and risk capacity, without jeopardising the interests and rights it must protect?**

## 03.01 Purpose, mission, economic value, community outcomes and the business model

### 03.01.1 What strategy is ultimately for

**Purpose** identifies the organisation's reason for existing and the bounds of its legitimate activities. **Mission** expresses what the entity intends to do, for whom and to what end; **vision** describes a desired future state; **values** describe acceptable conduct. **Strategy** selects a coherent way to fulfil the purpose. **Business model** is the operating and economic mechanism by which activities convert capabilities and resources into outputs, outcomes, income and cash. **Operating plan** assigns work, milestones and resources. None is interchangeable.

A board can approve an inspirational mission and a financially balanced budget while still having no meaningful strategy. The missing element is a set of choices that explain why the entity can outperform relevant alternatives in creating its intended value, and why other attractive activities are deliberately excluded. AICD teaches that strategy and risk are inseparable and that directors must understand both the value being pursued and the business model intended to generate it. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 11-16, 19-24, 33-35]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 5-6, 9-11]]

For a commercial enterprise, value creation is not necessarily equal to more revenue, market share, assets or accounting profit. The relevant question is whether a strategy can create durable **economic value after funding the cost of capital** and preserving financial resilience. For a charity, the primary end is legitimate charitable purpose and beneficiary outcomes, with solvency and reserves enabling sustained service. For a CATSI corporation, value may include livelihoods, community capability, cultural continuity and self-determination, subject to the corporation's rule book, law and community priorities. For an RNTBC, native title rights and relationships with Country are not economic outputs to be maximised or exchanged through ordinary corporate discretion. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-26]; AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 16-18]]

### 03.01.2 A director's purpose-to-performance chain

The board should be able to reconstruct a causal chain: **legal purpose and affected rights → intended beneficiaries or customers → value proposition/outcomes → strategic choices → distinctive capabilities and relationships → resource commitments → operating model → indicators of success and risk → actual results → adaptation**. Ask what part of this chain is based on demonstrated evidence rather than an assertion. A strategic plan that contains goals and key performance indicators (KPIs) but no explanation of advantage, capabilities or constraints is not yet a strategy.

| Dimension | Commercial illustration | Charity illustration | RNTBC illustration |
|---|---|---|---|
| End sought | Competitive returns and sustainable cash generation | Better educational attendance, retention and outcomes | Effective stewardship of native title rights and holders' decisions; sustaining cultural and economic priorities |
| Means | Target segment, product, process, pricing, distribution and investment | Services, referral partnerships, grants and volunteers | Properly authorised native title agreements, governance processes, cultural knowledge and commercial activity where authorised |
| Success evidence | Unit margin, customer retention, return on invested capital (ROIC), free cash flow and downside | Beneficiary outcomes and equity of access, service reliability, reserves | Rights respected, holder participation and consent where required, benefit delivery and culturally legitimate accountability |
| Boundary | Law, finance, safety, risk appetite | Charitable objects, grants, safeguarding, donor conditions, financial continuity | CATSI Act, rule book, native title legislation and the rights/directions of affected common law holders |

No scorecard should substitute a convenient financial surrogate for outcomes that matter. In particular, a dollar estimate of community benefit is not an entitlement to override decision rights or to treat cultural loss as an offsettable cost.

### 03.01.3 Value creation, value capture and distribution

An enterprise may create customer value through lower prices, but fail to capture sufficient margin to fund its assets; conversely, a project may make money by imposing unacceptable risk on workers, consumers or Country. Directors should separate **value created**, **value captured by the organisation**, **value distributed to stakeholders**, **uncompensated external costs**, and **rights that cannot be bargained away by ordinary company approval**. Legality and authorisation are prior decision gates, not weights in a financial scoring exercise.

**IAE: business-model unit test.** Consider an illustrative product sold at A$110, with A$70 variable cost and A$20 allocated recurring cost: contribution is A$40 and accounting operating profit A$20 per unit before finance and tax. If it needs A$300 million of incremental capital and yields A$18 million of steady after-tax operating profit, apparent unit profitability coexists with a 6% return on the invested capital. At an assumed 9% cost of capital, annual economic profit is A$18m minus A$27m = **negative A$9m**. The numerical illustration is not AICD course data; it shows why accounting and strategic narratives cannot replace capital productivity.

### 03.01.4 Board tests, evidence and red flags

**Request:** a one-page purpose-to-business-model map; major customer or beneficiary needs; sources of funding; historical unit economics; capability map; stakeholder/rights-holder analysis; and how financial, service, safety and legitimacy outcomes are measured. **Challenge:** What must be true for the organisation to remain useful and viable in five years? Which outputs are not outcomes? Whose needs are missing? Which assets or relationships must never be put at risk? **Decide:** whether management's proposed strategic objectives are authorised, sufficiently specific and suitable for further development. **Monitor:** changes in the actual value proposition, beneficiary/customer experience, margin and cash flow, and delivery of intended outcomes. **Red flags:** a vague mission used to justify any expansion; revenue growth presented as value creation; no evidence of outcomes; continuing expenditure because it is funded; or directors taking decisions reserved by law to others.

## 03.02 Strategy as integrated choices and trade-offs

### 03.02.1 Strategy is one connected architecture, not competing departmental 'strategies'

AICD defines contemporary strategy around integrated choices concerning where and how the organisation will compete or operate, the advantage it will sustain, partnerships and ecosystems, adaptability and long-term value. It uses the Lafley–Martin **strategic choice cascade**, which asks about winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, required capabilities and management systems. Strategy is therefore **singular at the entity level**, even though business units legitimately have subordinate approaches and plans. Finance, climate, technology, people and risk initiatives must be mutually consistent and must not individually authorise a different corporate direction. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 19-21, 52-54]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 10, 14]]

A choice is strategic when it excludes credible alternatives, commits scarce resources or changes the enterprise's long-term risk, rights or capabilities. Statements such as 'grow', 'be innovative', 'be sustainable', 'excel at service' and 'be the safest' are aspirations unless they generate distinctive decisions about markets, clients, service models, capital, partners and trade-offs. A director should ask: **What are we choosing not to do? Which resources are deliberately being withdrawn? What hypothesis differentiates this strategy from competitors' plans?**

### 03.02.2 Strategic theory and competing lenses

| Lens and original attribution | Directors use it to ask | What it omits or can distort |
|---|---|---|
| Porter: industry structure, five forces and positioning | Are industry economics attractive? Can we defend relative advantage? | Industry boundaries may be porous; networks, regulation and ecosystems can change quickly. |
| Resource-based view and VRIO (valuable, rare, hard to imitate, organised) | What assets, people, data and capabilities are genuinely distinctive? | Valuable capabilities can become obsolete; internal excellence may be commercially misdirected. |
| Lafley–Martin choice cascade | Do aspiration, chosen field, advantage, capabilities and systems reinforce each other? | Can sound too competitively framed for charities/RNTBCs unless adapted to mission and rights. |
| Ansoff product–market matrix | Are we deepening current offerings, adding products, entering markets or diversifying? | It classifies growth direction, not financial attractiveness or risk capacity. |
| Scenario planning and dynamic capabilities (Teece and others) | Can we sense shifts, act and reconfigure capabilities? | 'Agility' is not a substitute for disciplined commitments and accountable performance. |
| Balanced Scorecard (Kaplan–Norton) | Have strategic assumptions been translated into financial, customer, internal process and learning measures? | Balanced categories can conceal causal weakness or bad incentives. |
| Theory of change / outcomes logic | Why should proposed activities lead to particular social or community outcomes? | Attribution and counterfactuals are hard; outcomes may be non-commensurable. |
| Strategic positioning and activity-system fit | Do complementary activities reinforce differentiation or cost advantage? | A tightly optimised model can become rigid under structural change. |

The AICD course directly introduces positioning, stakeholder alignment, ecosystems, dynamic capabilities, the strategic choice cascade, vision, big hairy audacious goals (BHAGs), scenario thinking and Balanced Scorecard. The comparative diagnostic table, VRIO, Ansoff synthesis and explicit theory-of-change analysis above are **IAE** unless separately described in the source pages; they are not represented as an AICD-prescribed system. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 19-21, 42-58]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 8-13]]

### 03.02.3 An integrated strategic hypothesis

**IAE worked hypothesis:** A regional manufacturer chooses to defend a high-quality niche instead of entering discount mass retail. Its proposition is reliability and traceability, backed by special-purpose machinery and relationships with retailers. Required systems include quality assurance, inventory visibility and disciplined pricing. It abandons low-margin private-label work, releasing working capital. The strategy should generate better cash conversion and a lower product-recall rate; failure signs include a widening price premium without retention, rising waste, and insufficient returns on specialised assets. An expansion into health food would be a **different strategic proposition**, requiring different expertise and controls, even if the same ovens could technically bake the product.

For an NFP, convert this into an explicit causal theory: 'If locally trusted workers deliver evidence-based early support, then fewer clients progress to expensive crisis intervention, provided referrals reach those at greatest need and public services can absorb referrals.' The board must test each italicised assumption using outcomes and beneficiary evidence, not just grant acquittals. For an RNTBC, a development hypothesis starts with **who has the authority to decide** and **whether the proposal is consistent with holder aspirations and Country**, before choice of commercial vehicle.

### 03.02.4 Strategic coherence and anti-strategy tests

Directors should reject a 'portfolio of strategic pillars' if its initiatives compete for the same limited workforce, capital or management attention without priority rules. Test internal consistency across scope, economics, capability, values, risk appetite and incentives. A growth initiative that requires a culture of fast experimentation but uses uncompromising safety-critical product controls may be possible if experiments are ring-fenced; simply urging staff to be 'entrepreneurial and compliant' is not sufficient. Likewise, outsourcing a key operation to save cost may damage the very reliability advantage the strategy claims to defend.

**Evidence:** a one-page strategic-choice cascade, disaggregated portfolio map, synergy/dependency analysis, stated exclusions, mission or community decision authority, competitor counter-move assessment, and a red-team account of how the strategy could fail. **Escalate:** when a new initiative contradicts approved scope, needs risk appetite changes, depends on unavailable resources, or commits the entity irreversibly without fresh board authority.

## 03.03 Board-management roles across the strategy cycle

### 03.03.1 Governance, not delegated strategy authorship

AICD distinguishes **strategic development** (broad insight, foresight, creative challenge and opportunity formulation), **strategic planning** (management translating choices into executable plans) and **the strategic plan** (the proposed direction and commitments presented for board approval). A strategic board participates early and repeatedly, but it normally does not write operational plans or displace the chief executive officer (CEO). The role split depends on size, maturity, urgency and the organisation's constitution and delegations. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 22-32, 38-41, 59-62]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 8-10, 16-19]]

The board's reserved powers and directors' duties must be determined for the actual entity. For ordinary Corporations Act companies, s 198A is a replaceable rule on management by or under direction of directors, subject to constitution and any applicable law; it does **not** imply that every operational choice must be made personally by the board. Relevant care-and-diligence requirements include s 180. Other regimes, including the CATSI Act and incorporated-association statutes, must not be treated as automatically governed by those sections. [Corporations Act 2001 (Cth), ss 180, 198A, official compilation C2026C00432, 19 September 2026; verified 27 October 2026; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 38-46]]

### 03.03.2 Decision-rights table

| Stage | Board must understand, challenge and decide | Management should prepare and implement | Records and monitoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| Context and purpose | Confirm legal objects, legitimate purpose, stakeholder and rights-holder constraints, and the questions deserving board attention | Diagnose customers, beneficiaries, stakeholders, legal environment, resources and performance | Purpose, constitution, decision-rights map, stakeholder evidence |
| Strategic development | Provide insight, foresight, alternative hypotheses and appetite boundaries; make sure dissent surfaces | Organise foresight, research, workshops and choices | Options ledger, assumptions, workshop minutes |
| Planning | Test coherence, finance, capacity, risk and resource trade-offs; resist drafting managerial tasks | Build scenarios, plans, business case, resources, milestones and risk treatments | Strategic plan, three-way forecasts, risk appetite, roadmap |
| Approval | Decide strategy, material capital allocations, prohibited activities and matters reserved; refuse or condition approval where evidence is incomplete | Recommend preferred option with alternatives and advice | Board resolution, decision rationale, assumptions, delegated limits |
| Execution | Hold CEO accountable for integrated results, delegated authorities and control effectiveness | Lead operations, change and staff; enforce controls | Milestone reviews, KPI/KRI/KCI reports, exception logs |
| Review and adaptation | Test strategic hypotheses; decide whether to continue, pivot, pause, sell or close | Investigate deviations, propose remedies and revise plans | Trigger register, revised forecasts, independent assurance |
| Crisis | Preserve governance authority; monitor safety, solvency, law and stakeholder response; act when board-reserved powers arise | Run incident command and continuity response | Situation reports, legal advice, minutes and recovery plan |

### 03.03.3 Board involvement by lifecycle

A fast-growing, founder-led proprietary company may need directors to challenge concentrated founder assumptions and construct a basic finance and control architecture. A mature listed company needs an enterprise-wide portfolio lens, escalation from subsidiaries, clear committees and market disclosure. A small charity may legitimately have a working board that takes on some management tasks, but directors must distinguish which capacity they are acting in; informal actions should not erase conflict management, delegations or collective accountability. A CATSI corporation may have directors who also represent different families or communities, but that does not eliminate legal rule-book and holder-specific authority. A board dealing with acute distress may need more frequent and intrusive information without covertly replacing management. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 27-32, 84-85]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-26]]

### 03.03.4 Challenging management without shadow management

A director may request disaggregated evidence, visit operations, speak with relevant executives under agreed protocols and commission independent advice where permitted. The test is whether the board improves **direction, judgment and oversight**, not whether every director negotiates supplier terms. In the AICD strategic board model, constructive challenge, long-term perspective, strategic conversations and understanding change management are central. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 33-37]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 5, 9]]

**Director prompts:** Is this the first time we are hearing of this opportunity? Did management understand the board's risk preferences before spending on development? Are we only being asked to endorse one proposal? Have contrary views been filtered out? Does our committee structure allow the full board to discharge responsibility? If we approve subject to conditions, who verifies completion before funds are released?

**Decision documentation:** Minute the nature of alternatives, major assumptions, material questions and advice, conflicts and recusals, risk appetite assessment, authority, decision and conditions. Minutes are evidence of the process, not a guarantee that the decision was sound. Cross-reference [03.06](#0306-board-strategy-workshops-options-evaluation-and-decision-quality) and [03.11](#0311-board-management-committees-and-the-three-lines-model).

## 03.04 External environment, internal capabilities, stakeholders and foresight

### 03.04.1 The strategic environment is a changing system

A board should examine macroeconomics, demographics, policy, technology, substitutes, rivalry, suppliers, workforce, physical environment, social licence and capital markets **as interacting drivers**. A single forecast of industry demand is inadequate if supply, affordability, regulation or technology could change the competitive equilibrium. AICD distinguishes scanning the external and internal landscape, broadening information sources, using strategic insight about the present and foresight about possible futures. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 38-51]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 8-13]]

A useful hierarchy is: **facts** (observable contracts and results); **estimates** (inferred demand, costs and capabilities); **assumptions** (variables chosen for analysis); **forecasts** (conditional outcomes); **scenarios** (internally consistent alternative futures); **beliefs** (management's judgment); and **decisions** (commitments despite uncertainty). Directors should not treat a consultant's forecast as a physical fact or an upside scenario as a probability-weighted central case. The sources, dates, units and confidence of all material estimates should be explicit.

### 03.04.2 External analysis and competitive dynamics

For each potential pathway, examine (1) end-user needs and willingness or ability to pay; (2) substitutes and shifting behaviour; (3) pricing power and margin retention; (4) industry capacity and competitor reactions; (5) upstream suppliers and downstream concentration; (6) regulation and licences; (7) ecosystem partners; and (8) cost and access to capital. Ask whether the strategy relies on an industry-wide demand expansion that every rival is also investing to serve. Capacity expansion may depress prices even if the demand forecast proves correct. Likewise, an attractive technology can be technically viable yet commercially displaced by a cheaper or established substitute.

**IAE:** Distinguish absolute market growth from economic rent capture. Hypothetical market demand grows 30% while capacity grows 50%. Even if sales volumes rise, oversupply can lower margins and industry returns. A market-share target has no value unless it improves cash profitability after the incremental capital committed. Test both the 'right market' and 'right to win', plus how structural shifts affect exit value. [Curriculum foundation: AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 42-54]; extension: author analysis.]

### 03.04.3 Internal capability and organisational readiness

An external opportunity is not the same as a right to succeed. Directors need evidence of managerial expertise, production capability, information systems, working capital, safety, digital resilience, relationships, change capacity and staff trust. Assess **current assets**, **capabilities that can realistically be built**, **capabilities available through partners**, and **critical weaknesses that cannot be fixed within the strategic window**. AICD's 'ready, willing and able' evaluation covers human, financial, information and organisational capital, including leadership, alignment, culture and teamwork. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 66-70]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 20-21]]

An established plant may appear to have spare capacity, yet be unsuitable for allergen-separated products. The financial model may include new ingredients but omit segregation, validation, certification, supervision, record retention and insurance. Similarly, a digital project may improve customer access but fail because staff cannot maintain the system or third-party contracts do not allow recovery within the entity's tolerances. The director's task is to identify capability **interdependencies**, not accept a checklist where 80% of boxes are green.

### 03.04.4 Stakeholder maps and multiple decision authorities

A stakeholder register should record affected people and groups, their interests, rights and constraints, not merely their capacity to influence public relations. Investors, clients, creditors, staff, volunteers, regulators, funders, Traditional Owners and communities have different roles. For RNTBCs, common law holders' native title decision rights are **legal authorities**, not preferences to be traded against other stakeholders in a weighted average. For registered charities, the charitable purpose and vulnerable beneficiaries are not subordinated to whichever donor has the greatest funds. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 16-18, 24-26]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-26]; ORIC, *Decision-making in registered native title bodies corporate*, checked 27 October 2026.]

### 03.04.5 Foresight, uncertainty levels and the planning horizon

The AICD appendix explains foresight as considering alternative futures and using leading signposts; it discusses volatility, uncertainty, complexity and ambiguity (VUCA), scenario planning and the limitations of linear forecasts. The director tool on turbulent strategy adds an iterative process: construct extreme but plausible scenarios, preserve hedges and options, run experiments before major commitment, define triggers, and monitor continuously. It cites Courtney's differing levels of uncertainty, under which the appropriate analysis ranges from conventional forecast to multiple paths and genuine ambiguity. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 8-13]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 39-40]]

**IAE foresight exercise:** Identify three *independent drivers*, not three arbitrary revenue-growth rates. For a manufacturer: (a) consumer willingness to pay for its product; (b) cost and availability of inputs; (c) regulation of product claims. Build three internally consistent futures: stable demand and manageable inflation; weak demand and retailers exerting pricing pressure; new regulatory requirements and high assurance costs. Under each, recalculate cash flow, capital needs, downside safety and the decision to expand or wait. An 'extreme but plausible' scenario is neither a mathematical worst case nor the only future worthy of governance attention. If the probability is not defensibly estimable, do not attach a false percentage.

Planning horizons should match the life of irreversible commitments and obligations. A twelve-month budget cannot evaluate a twenty-year asset or a long-term native title agreement. A five-year strategic plan may need rolling review, but it does not remove the need for 20- to 30-year signposts where useful asset life, closure liabilities or intergenerational consequences demand it. The choice between fixed-date and rolling plans is explicit in the curriculum. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 39-41]]

**Request and challenge:** separate the base case from downside and transition paths; obtain a capability gap assessment; ask what observations would disprove the preferred narrative; identify whose views were excluded; test competitors' responses and financing conditions; and insist that critical assumptions each have an owner, indicator and review date. **Escalate:** structural demand or policy changes, key partner failure, lost permit or consent, inability to fill capability gaps, or an unanticipated funding dependency.

## 03.05 Alternative pathways, opportunity cost and reversibility

### 03.05.1 A real choice set

An option paper should never limit the board to 'approve management's preferred growth plan or do nothing'. Alternatives often include sustaining the core business, productivity improvement, adjacent products, acquisitions, joint ventures, licensing, staged experimentation, strategic partnerships, selective disposal, managed contraction or orderly exit. The **counterfactual** is not always zero cost: doing nothing may allow margin erosion, equipment failure or mission decline. Conversely, acquiring assets can create obligations that prevent funding more valuable opportunities.

AICD advocates explicit alternative evaluation, strategic feasibility, sustainability, accountability, organisational readiness and risk alignment, with board approval of material proposals. The strategic planning exercises and slide 'integrity' evaluation emphasise comprehensibility, sustainability, feasibility, appropriateness and accountability. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 63-70, 84-85]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 19-22]]

### 03.05.2 Multi-gate option evaluation

| Gate | Question | Example of an automatic pause or no-go |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Authority and legal compliance | Is this lawful, within objects, permissions, rights and delegations? | Missing licence, consent, necessary native title holder authority or unlawful product claim. |
| 2. Purpose and values | Does it advance legitimate purpose and protect people and Country? | Revenue requires mission drift, serious safeguarding harm or unauthorised cultural impact. |
| 3. Operational feasibility | Are equipment, skills, data, partners and controls credible? | No validated process for critical food safety or system recovery. |
| 4. Financial acceptance | Can funding absorb cost overruns and stress without breaching obligations? | Severe cash gap, covenant breach or economically negative irreversible commitment without defensible strategic rationale. |
| 5. Risk appetite | Are downside and control failures within approved limits? | Fatal or irreversible harm beyond the board's explicit boundaries. |
| 6. Alternative value | Is this better than the realistic opportunity-cost alternatives? | Higher apparent earnings but lower incremental value than refurbishment or debt repayment. |
| 7. Reversibility and learning | Can the entity stage commitment, test assumptions or abandon cheaply? | Full-scale launch before necessary trials, audits or permissions. |
| 8. Execution and accountability | Who owns results, safeguards, review triggers and stop decisions? | Vague deliverables, no credible owners or veto from a required authority. |

A 'high score' in commercial potential cannot compensate for a failed legality or consent gate. A 2% probability of a catastrophic, non-reversible rights violation is not an acceptable 'expected loss' calculation that automatically authorises the action.

### 03.05.3 Opportunity cost is real even when not in the project spreadsheet

**IAE example (all A$ million, present values at a common valuation date, after tax):** The board can commit A$100m to Option A with a discounted present value of benefits of A$118m: **net present value (NPV) +A$18m**. Option B needs the same capital and yields benefits of A$127m: **NPV +A$27m**. Both create positive standalone value if independently estimated, but choosing A forgoes A$9m relative to B. If funding is scarce and projects cannot both proceed, comparison with zero alone is poor capital allocation. The analysis must also consider differential downside, strategic compatibility and any costs of deferral. The AICD curriculum references NPV and financial readiness; this alternative-ranking method is **IAE**, not an AICD calculation. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 63-70]].

### 03.05.4 Option value and learning before irreversible commitments

**IAE:** A **real option** is the value of preserving a right, not an obligation, to invest, expand, defer, contract or abandon after more information emerges. It differs from postponement without purpose. Its value is higher when uncertainty is material, the investment is difficult to reverse, the opportunity will remain available long enough to learn, and the cost of waiting is moderate. It can be destroyed by delay where competitors lock up access, permits expire or benefits diminish quickly.

**Simplified staged investment:** instead of immediately risking A$80m in a new production line, management proposes A$5m for laboratory work, segregated pilot production, certification and market testing. If the pilot demonstrates safety, demand and unit margins, a further A$75m may be considered. The A$5m is an at-risk learning expense; the board is not yet obliged to approve the A$75m. Compare this with full investment including lost early cash flow, competitive pre-emption and pilot quality. The project may still be unsuitable if the pilot cannot detect the hazard or if even the pilot infringes the relevant rights.

### 03.05.5 Reversibility, exit discipline and no-go criteria

Classify decisions by **reversible** (pricing trial), **partly reversible** (hiring and infrastructure), and **effectively irreversible** (large integrated plant, surrender of native title or lasting environmental harm). Use proportionately more evidence, independently tested downside and formal approval before irreversible commitments. Avoid sunk-cost reasoning: money already irretrievably spent is not normally a reason to continue; compare future incremental benefits and harms with future incremental costs and options from today's decision point.

Original no-go criteria should be precise enough to bind behaviour. Examples: failure to secure valid legal authority; inability to demonstrate a safety-critical control; forecast funding below board minimum; credible severe harm beyond approved tolerance; failure of independent technical validation; and unknown liability that could threaten the organisation's continuity. Where the board genuinely approves an exception to its *discretionary* appetite, record who has authority, the reasons, period, compensating controls and subsequent review. Do not imply the board can waive a statutory prohibition or common law holder consent.

**Evidence and escalation:** independent technical review; decision tree or staged-commitment schedule; uncommitted and unavoidable cost exposure; exit costs and asset salvage assumptions; downside liquidity forecasts; rights and consent records; decision log naming owner and kill criteria. Escalate cumulative capital commitments and 'salami-sliced' approvals which evade a delegated threshold.

## 03.06 Board strategy workshops, options evaluation and decision quality

### 03.06.1 Designing a productive board strategy process

AICD distinguishes establishing the strategic process ('plan to plan'), input into strategic development, drafting the plan, approval and subsequent review. Directors are advised to create enough space for thoughtful dialogue and future-oriented questions rather than allowing a detailed operational presentation to consume the session. The CEO organises the process, while the chair ensures appropriately framed issues, participation and challenge. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 38-41, 58-62]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 16-19]]

**Original workshop architecture:** Four weeks beforehand, management circulates a concise factual strategic diagnosis and a list of unresolved choices. One week beforehand, the board receives an options paper with market assumptions, finance, rights, risk and dissent. The first session reaffirms purpose, boundaries and the status quo; the second tests the external environment and strategic alternatives; the third examines readiness, capacity and capital constraints; the fourth agrees which proposals to develop, not an automatic final investment decision. Management subsequently provides a decision paper and, where required, independent due diligence. Timeframes are illustrative process choices, not AICD rules.

### 03.06.2 The minimum decision paper

A credible paper should contain: (1) precisely requested decision and legal authority; (2) why now; (3) purpose and strategic fit; (4) evidence for need; (5) at least two credible alternatives plus a realistic counterfactual; (6) benefits and outcomes; (7) full lifecycle cash and resources; (8) material assumptions and sensitivities; (9) threats, opportunities and control adequacy; (10) risk capacity and appetite test; (11) cross-functional and stakeholder/rights-holder consultation; (12) implementation and stopping criteria; (13) dissent, gaps and independent advice; and (14) proposed resolutions, owners and monitoring. The AICD decision paper and strategic planning tools support these themes; the exact order and format here are original. [AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 34-36, 100-103, 121-123]; AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 63-70, 84-85]].

A paper that says 'management recommends X' before identifying alternatives invites endorsement bias. Ask whether the CEO would recommend the same project if the current team had not developed it, the company did not own the site or project, and capital could be reallocated without loss of face.

### 03.06.3 Decision quality and competing explanations

Decision quality means a sound process under uncertainty, not a guarantee of a favourable outcome. A board can make a defensible decision and still lose money because a low-probability event materialises. A profitable outcome does not retrospectively cure poor governance. To reduce groupthink: appoint a devil's advocate or red team, collect independent forecasts, document disconfirming evidence, ask silent participants first, separate assumptions from data, disclose conflicts and use in-camera sessions when needed. For a culturally diverse board, design a process that provides meaningful participation rather than equating speed or assertiveness with competence. The AICD board-effectiveness curriculum develops these cognitive and group mechanisms in depth; this file applies them to strategic decisions. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 58-60, 84-85]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-32, 64-88]].

**IAE: pre-mortem.** Ask directors to imagine the approved strategy has failed in three years. Each independently identifies the three most credible causes. Group them into faulty premise, execution, financing, partner, regulation, rights, control and culture. Assign to each a potential early-warning indicator and a tested countermeasure. The purpose is to discover failure modes while still able to change the design, not to predict failure.

### 03.06.4 Integrity, decision rights and conditional approvals

AICD introduces an *Integrity Model* and checks whether an organisation is 'ready, willing and able'. These are evaluation aids, not legal safe harbours or mechanical pass/fail scores. A plan may be understandable and plausible yet **financially unacceptable**, **not within powers**, **culturally illegitimate** or **beyond risk appetite**. Approving a 'strategy in principle' should not be confused with approving the full project expenditure, employment obligations, debt guarantees, rights agreements or communications. Record distinct approval gates.

| Possible resolution | When appropriate | Condition and follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Approve strategy but not investment | Direction credible; detailed engineering, consent or funding unfinished | Return for final investment decision with independent evidence and preconditions met. |
| Approve a capped pilot | Learning is valuable; pilot itself is authorised and controlled | Hard expenditure cap, outcome criteria, data owner and time-limited reporting. |
| Defer | Material unknowns can be resolved without intolerable delay | Specify research, independent review, consultation and decision date. |
| Reject | Illegality, rights violation, unmanageable harm or insufficient risk-adjusted value | Record basis, alternative pathway and preservation of scarce resources. |
| Pause or withdraw prior approval | Circumstances, controls or risk capacity materially changed | Standstill, contractual implications, market/disclosure and stakeholder communication. |

### 03.06.5 Evidence, challenge and red flags

**Request:** annotated alternatives, financial model and independent sensitivities, risk assessment including aggregate exposure, legal and rights advice, readiness and culture assessment, information security and implementation assumptions, named owners, and the proposed board minute. **Challenge:** Which uncertainties would cause us to reverse today's decision? Has risk committee input been sought before launch? What advice was rejected? What does the proposal imply we cannot finance? Is a new way of operating inconsistent with current safety culture? **Red flags:** strategy day devolves into sales presentations; a single preferred option; all positive forecasts; a 'green' heat map with unknown controls; a resolution drafted before challenge; important dissent never minuted; or contractual implementation commencing before formal authority.

## 03.07 Capital allocation, financial capacity and stewardship

### 03.07.1 Strategy is tested through scarce resources

Capital allocation converts strategic intent into irreversible or difficult-to-reverse commitments. The director's obligation is not to maximise an annual expenditure budget or approve the most attractive presentation. It is to ensure the entity chooses the best feasible combination of investments, maintenance, debt obligations, liquidity reserves, people and mission delivery. **Growth consumes capital before it may create value.** Expansion can be a failure even if it increases output, earnings or social visibility when it earns too little on the resources employed, undermines cash resilience or diverts resources from a more valuable and authorised alternative.

The AICD curriculum recognises the strategic importance of financial capital, implementation costs, net present value, funding availability and maintaining a viable business model. The director-tools risk questions explicitly connect the future viability of markets, returns above cost of capital, free cash flow, banking covenants, debt refinancing and finance access. Formal economic-profit decomposition and portfolio optimisation below are **independent analytical extensions**. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 20-21, 63-70, 84-85]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 89-90]; AICD[FIN, 2025/26 V1, pp. 11-22, 80-89]].

### 03.07.2 The basic economics of return and cash

**IAE definitions:**

- **ROIC** = net operating profit after tax (NOPAT) / average invested operating capital, with numerator and denominator consistently defined. This measures operating returns, not a total shareholder return or an internal rate of return. Treatment of leases, goodwill, surplus cash and acquisitions matters.
- **Weighted average cost of capital (WACC)** = an estimate of the required return for the project's or business's relevant market-risk profile, with after-tax nominal cash flows discounted using a consistent after-tax nominal rate. A group's average WACC is not necessarily the correct rate for a riskier project or country.
- **Economic profit**, simplified = NOPAT − WACC × invested capital. Positive accounting earnings can coexist with negative economic profit.
- **NPV** = sum of forecast unlevered after-tax free cash flows discounted at the appropriate rate, less the initial and subsequent incremental investments. In standard cases, a positive NPV indicates estimated value creation, assuming correct baseline, cash flow, risk, tax and terminal-value treatment.
- **Free cash flow** is cash remaining after operating cash requirements and investment needed to sustain and grow the business, subject to a clearly stated convention. It is not the same as earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA).
- **Liquidity** is the ability to meet obligations when due, not simply the accounting profit for the year. Unused committed facilities, restrictions, covenants, counterparties, timing and trapped cash all matter.

**IAE audited arithmetic:** a fictional A$200m operating investment produces annual NOPAT of A$14m on a stable A$200m invested-capital base. ROIC is **7.0%**. At an assumed WACC of **9.0%**, the annual capital charge is A$18m and economic profit is **negative A$4m**. This is an annual economic-profit illustration, not a project NPV. A one-year ROIC below WACC does not automatically mean an investment has negative lifetime NPV, because future cash flows, ramp-up and capital recovery could matter. Equally, high current ROIC can conceal the need for expensive replacement expenditure.

### 03.07.3 Affordability is not value, and value is not affordability

Four tests must be passed separately: (1) **legal, safety and rights acceptability**; (2) **strategic and mission fit**; (3) **financial value or justified mission efficiency**; and (4) **liquidity and risk-capacity affordability** under stress. A positive-NPV mine, factory or expansion can still bankrupt an overleveraged owner during construction. A cash-rich company can also destroy value by funding negative-NPV projects. Neither higher leverage nor higher hurdle rates fixes a safety-critical control weakness.

**IAE hypothetical liquidity bridge (all A$m, next 12 months):** unrestricted cash 80; additional genuinely committed undrawn facilities 40; minimum operational liquidity required 25; unavoidable debt service 20; committed baseline capital expenditure 30. A simple gross liquidity headroom estimate is **80 + 40 − 25 − 20 − 30 = 45**. This is not automatically a safe loss-absorption capacity: trading cash inflows/outflows, covenant tests, debt maturities beyond 12 months, margin calls, facility conditions, restricted cash, tax and coincident shocks must be assessed. If a proposed project exposes the group to a **A$50m** adverse cash call before financing can be raised, management cannot present it as affordable merely because the business has A$80m of cash. Sources are synthetic; calculation is original.

### 03.07.4 Portfolio rather than project decisions

A board should examine the combined effect of initiatives on capital, management capacity, risk correlations and balance-sheet resilience. Five individually affordable projects can jointly create excessive concentration, credit exposure, implementation bottlenecks or refinancing risk. Common drivers include commodity prices, interest rates, government budgets, technology vendors, one regional workforce and the same major customers. Projects also compete for scarce strategic attention. A portfolio should be evaluated under consistent financial scenarios, horizons and constraints rather than comparing unrelated, management-selected upside cases.

**IAE portfolio construction sequence:** rank opportunities by incremental value or mission outcome after risk and cost; impose hard legality/rights and safety gates; model aggregate 12–24-month cash stress and long-term returns; identify shared drivers; reserve sufficient liquidity and capital for non-discretionary obligations; fund highest-value and resilient opportunities within constraints; preserve options and regularly reallocate as evidence changes. Where the organisation's assets have long lives, monitor whether the future replacement and closure obligations are funded, not just short-term cash generation.

| Allocation decision | Questions directors should ask | Indicators and red flags |
|---|---|---|
| Maintain and renew core assets | What must be spent to operate safely and keep current services viable? | Maintenance deferral, asset failures, increasing rework, underfunded renewal backlog. |
| Fund growth | What incremental return/outcome and downside are plausible? | Revenue growth with falling free cash flow and increasing capital intensity. |
| Hold liquidity | What plausible stress must be survived without loss of control or services? | Uncommitted finance assumed available during systemic shocks. |
| Reduce debt | What flexibility and financing cost are recovered? | Covenant headroom too thin; refinance coincides with construction needs. |
| Acquire or divest | What is the full price, integration risk, disposal tax and lost strategic option? | Synergy unsupported, stranded liabilities, misleading comparisons with sunk costs. |
| Return surplus to owners | What is genuinely surplus after committed obligations and resilience? | Distributions financed with debt while essential capital is deferred. |
| Fund NFP reserves and services | What service continuity and restricted-fund obligations govern choices? | Surplus treated as failure, or donor restrictions ignored for liquidity. |
| RNTBC agreement benefits | Who has authority over funds, restrictions, distribution and rights? | Monetisation assumed to legitimise rights decisions or unrestricted use of holder-specific funds. |

### 03.07.5 Mission-specific and legal stewardship

A charity should seek value for money, effectiveness, equity and financial sustainability. Donated labour is not costless if burnout undermines service continuity; a restricted grant is not fungible with unrestricted operating cash. An unlisted family company may accept longer payback or different dividend preferences than a listed company, but directors must still act properly and understand creditor exposure. An RNTBC must distinguish corporate operating revenue, trust funds, agreement proceeds, benefits directed to particular holders and non-financial cultural interests; ordinary corporate discretion does not necessarily authorise reallocating these resources. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-26]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 58-59, 89-90]; see 03.17.]

**Escalation:** material shortfall to the capital-reinvestment plan, negative forecast cash in a stress case, impending covenant breach, dependence on an unapproved grant, any evidence of unlawful funding use, or a commitment large enough to change the approved risk profile. Solvency law and financial reporting are covered fully in the planned companion [File 04](04_Financial_Literacy_Performance_and_Solvency.md) and [File 05](05_Financial_Reporting_Audit_and_Assurance.md). Those links are *planned corpus targets, not verified existing files at this document's production date*.

## 03.08 Definitions and types of risk and uncertainty

### 03.08.1 Risk is the effect of uncertainty on objectives

The ISO 31000 family defines risk in relation to the *effect of uncertainty on objectives*. Risk therefore includes favourable and unfavourable departures from intended outcomes. It is not synonymous with an adverse incident, volatility, a threat list or a compliance exception. AICD contrasts economic, regulatory, social and other perspectives on risk and repeatedly connects risk to the strategic choices that create exposure. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 87-91]; ISO 31000:2018, *Risk management — Guidelines*, ISO publication page checked 27 October 2026.]

**Core distinctions:** **objective** = desired result; **risk source** = circumstance or element giving rise to uncertainty; **hazard** = possible source of harm, notably in safety; **event** = something that occurs or changes; **consequence** = effect on the objective; **likelihood** = chance or frequency, which may be qualitative or quantitative; **exposure** = persons, assets, rights or outcomes susceptible to the risk; **vulnerability** = susceptibility to harm conditional on exposure; **control** = measure that modifies risk; **control weakness** = a design, operation or assurance gap; **residual risk** = risk remaining after relevant controls; **uncertainty** = incompleteness of knowledge about events, probabilities, outcomes or underlying causal structure. Terminology should be documented consistently, recognising that some risk disciplines use a narrower definition of 'risk'.

### 03.08.2 Risk taxonomy and the illusion of independent buckets

| Type | Example of source and objective at stake | Director's immediate question |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic/business model | Customer demand shifts to substitutes | Is the profit/mission model becoming obsolete? |
| Financial/treasury | Commodity price and interest rate fall or rise, debt refinancing | What happens simultaneously to cash, covenants and funding? |
| Operational and safety | Equipment failure, food contamination, injury | Are critical controls designed, working and tested? |
| Legal/compliance | Breach of privacy, worker obligations, licence | Is exposure legally acceptable? What must be reported or stopped? |
| Technology and cyber | Ransomware disrupts manufacturing and records | How quickly can critical operations and evidence be restored? |
| People and culture | Incentives discourage reporting or drive unsafe speed | Does bad news reach the board? |
| Reputation, stakeholder and legitimacy | Service or trust collapse | Who is harmed and is confidence recoverable? |
| Climate, nature, environmental | Flooding, biodiversity loss, permit constraints | What are physical, transition and dependency exposures? |
| Political and geopolitical | Sanctions, sovereign decisions, supply interruption | What contractual, safety and liquidity consequences follow? |
| Rights, Country and cultural governance | Native title decision lacking affected holder consent | Who has authority? Are Country and decision rights protected? |

Risks are not independent because their labels differ. A cyber event can cause operational downtime, unsafe product, breach of data duties, litigation and financial distress. A climate event can impair suppliers, worker safety and mission delivery. Boards need **cause–event–consequence chains** and aggregation that captures interaction. A 'reputation risk' entry is often a consequence of a deeper failure rather than a controllable cause. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 105-107, 125-126, 130-136]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 1-7]].

### 03.08.3 Risk, uncertainty, ambiguity and opportunity

**Risk with estimable frequency**: failure rates can be informed by historical data if the technology and operating regime are stable. **Deep uncertainty**: a novel technology's long-term demand and regulatory regime may not permit defensible probability estimation. **Ambiguity**: actors may disagree about what constitutes success or harm. **Systemic exposure**: multiple entities fail under a shared shock. **Opportunity**: a favourable deviation may justify investing or adapting, but never remove the obligation to protect against unacceptable downside.

The distinction matters for methods. Frequency/severity models may suit insured incidents; scenario and robustness analysis may suit major shifts; consultation and legal rights analysis are required for legitimate decisions about Country. Assigning a single probability to an uncertain socio-political future can create apparent precision without better judgment.

### 03.08.4 Risk expression and ownership

Write risk statements as **because of [source], [event] may occur, causing [consequence on a named objective]**, with specific time horizon, scope and affected parties. Poor: 'Cyber: high'; better: 'If the shared production database is compromised and backups are unusable, the plant may be unable to verify allergen segregation, risking unsafe product release and lost records over weeks.' This description identifies controls and measurable thresholds.

The owner must be a person able to act, not merely a category or committee. A risk owner is accountable for identifying, assessing and treating a risk and reporting outside appetite; control owners execute particular safeguards. Ultimate governance oversight remains with the board. The risk register should record risk, owners, inherent assessment, controls, residual assessment, assumptions, appetite, metrics, actions and assurance. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 111-125, 130-137]].

## 03.09 Risk capacity, appetite, tolerance, limits and prohibitions

### 03.09.1 Five concepts that must not be conflated

**Risk capacity** is the entity's actual ability to absorb risk without compromising its sustainability or required objectives, determined by finance, operational capability, law and obligations. **Risk appetite** is the board's deliberate willingness to take or retain risk in pursuit of the purpose, within capacity and legal limits. **Risk tolerance** is the approved permitted variation or maximum exposure for an identified objective or risk category. **Risk limit** is a specific numerical or categorical control boundary delegated to a team, product or activity. **Prohibition** is an act that may not proceed because the law, decision rights or board policy forbids it. A board-approved 'zero tolerance' statement cannot make every chance of adverse outcome literally zero; it should communicate non-negotiable conduct and the safeguards required.

AICD itself cautions that literature uses 'tolerance' differently. Its teaching generally expresses tolerances as quantitative applications of appetite. APRA's CPS 220 explicitly uses 'risk tolerance' in association with **maximum risk limits**; that usage must be respected for regulated institutions. The defensible practice is to define the terms in the organisation's policy and maintain a clearly connected hierarchy. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 99-104, 107-110, 121-124]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 29-36]; APRA CPS 220, *Risk Management*, current standard page checked 27 October 2026.]

### 03.09.2 A numeric appetite ladder, with matching horizons

**IAE illustrative treasury ladder (all A$m of additional adverse cash use over the next 12 months):**

| Level | Value or rule | Governing implication |
|---|---:|---|
| Estimated financial capacity buffer | 45 | Conditional maximum extra shock absorbable under specified 12-month forecasts; re-estimate when cash, debt or commitments change. |
| Board appetite | 25 | Board chooses to expose **no more than 25** of this buffer to discretionary aggregated stress losses. |
| Management target | ≤18 | Ordinary planning remains well inside appetite, preserving early-warning headroom. |
| Amber early-warning threshold | >18 | Chief financial officer and chief risk officer review within 5 business days; controls and forecasts refreshed. |
| Red tolerance/enterprise limit | >25 | Immediate board chair and risk-committee escalation; no discretionary new commitment until authorised corrective decision. |
| Capacity distress indicator | >45 | Threatens estimated buffer: activate financing/distress response, not a routine request to widen appetite. |
| Prohibited | Unauthorised native title agreement; unlawful conduct; deliberate bypass of life-critical controls | No amount of potential financial gain overrides legal/rights or safety-critical boundaries. |

The example is a **stock of potential cash use within a 12-month horizon**. It cannot be combined without adjustment with annual expected loss, 10-year NPV, accounting impairment or an ordinal 'risk score'. If A$20m of unused capacity is already committed elsewhere or the company cannot draw the facility, the A$45m capacity estimate is wrong. No number should be adopted without financial forecasts and independent treasury validation.

### 03.09.3 Different risk categories need different appetites

Illustrative risk appetite statements should distinguish (a) bounded experimentation where losses are capped; (b) ordinary operating variation; (c) financial distress, creditor or funding thresholds; (d) customer and beneficiary harm; (e) serious safety risk; (f) data security and privacy; (g) environmental damage; (h) reputational and cultural legitimacy; and (i) rights and legal authority. 'High appetite for innovation' is not permission for a high appetite for patient harm, privacy breaches or unauthorised use of cultural knowledge. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 99-105]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 14-17]].

A useful statement names positive strategic risk the board **wants** management to take: 'We will undertake limited, consent-compliant trials of new service delivery, accepting up to A$0.5m of annual pilot expenditure, provided privacy, safeguarding, service continuity and outcomes testing meet specified standards.' This can be monitored and helps avoid reflexive risk aversion.

### 03.09.4 Genuine versus ceremonial appetite

Risk appetite is genuine if it determines approvals, pricing, investment, treasury limits, project design, incentives, controls and stop conditions. It is ceremonial if an annual policy uses 'low, medium, high' without reference to finance, objectives, front-line decisions or actual reporting. AICD explicitly contrasts genuine and imitation appetite processes; director tools ask whether appetite aligns with strategic objectives, capabilities, stakeholders, metrics, governance and culture. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 107-110]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 30, 91-92]].

**Board evidence request:** statement by risk category, capacity calculations, existing exposures, limit owners, exceptions register, KRI thresholds, escalations, capital plan, stress tests and assurance. **Challenge:** What decisions were rejected or redesigned because of appetite? Are any units outside limits? Are all material strategic initiatives mapped to appetite? Can the board distinguish a temporary metric exceedance from an impermissible activity? **Red flags:** appetite never changes despite material acquisitions; policy is published but operations are not measured; 'zero risk' exists only on paper; proposed appetite exceeds capacity; risk exceptions are approved retrospectively by the manager who caused them.

### 03.09.5 CQUniversity Australia: public example, not the corporation's own rule

The AICD appendix reproduces an **extract from CQUniversity Australia's publicly available 5 July 2023 risk appetite statement**. This is a **real public organisational example**, not a fictional teaching case and not a general legal standard. It illustrates differentiated narrative categories: more openness to strategic innovation, research growth and technology-enabled teaching; more caution around disruption, workforce, information security and compliance. The appendix describes four qualitative levels, from open to unacceptable, and explains that the complete CQU statement also contains tolerance measures. Its sample excerpts are therefore not enough to reconstruct every operational threshold. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 14-17], citing CQUniversity Australia, *Risk Appetite Statement*, 5 July 2023.]

**Diagnostic application:** A university may rationally accept failure of a limited new digital learning trial while requiring rigorous student-data controls and continuity of assessment services. A director should ask whether the high innovation appetite has a capped trial budget, an accountable owner and explicit service/safety limits. **Limitation:** the appendix is a selected 2023 extract; do not state that the same categories or thresholds remain CQUniversity policy as at October 2026 without reviewing the current university publication. The qualitative wording is paraphrased here rather than reproduced.

## 03.10 Risk governance architecture: ISO 31000, COSO and controls

### 03.10.1 One enterprise process, not a separate compliance bureaucracy

Enterprise risk management (ERM) is a coherent set of governance, management and assurance processes that supports objectives and resource decisions. A risk framework should establish risk policy, risk appetite and capacity, accountabilities, assessment methods, reporting, controls and escalation. It should be embedded in strategy, capital approvals, budgets, projects, suppliers, change management and crises. A register maintained by one risk officer is not, on its own, ERM. AICD teaches frameworks and practical risk processes, including identification, analysis, evaluation, treatment, monitoring and risk culture. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 87-98, 111-145]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 18-22]].

### 03.10.2 ISO 31000:2018 and COSO ERM: correct attribution and differences

**International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 31000:2018** is a general **guideline** on risk management, usable across sectors; its approach emphasises principles, an embedded framework and a process for communication/consultation, scope/context/criteria, assessment, treatment, monitoring and improvement. The Australian adoption is **AS ISO 31000:2018**. Its emphasis includes leadership, integration, inclusion, customisation, human/cultural factors and continual improvement. It is not itself an APRA prudential standard or a mandatory law applicable to all boards. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 18-19]; ISO, *ISO 31000:2018 Risk management — Guidelines*, confirmed current in 2026.]

**Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO)** published *Enterprise Risk Management—Integrating with Strategy and Performance* in **2017**, with five interrelated components and 20 principles: (1) governance and culture; (2) strategy and objective-setting; (3) performance; (4) review and revision; and (5) information, communication and reporting. COSO more explicitly organises ERM around performance and strategic decisions. COSO is not the statutory regulator of Australian companies. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 20-22]; COSO, *Enterprise Risk Management*, official website checked 27 October 2026.]

| Comparison | ISO 31000 | COSO ERM 2017 |
|---|---|---|
| Core organising idea | Principles, framework and iterative risk process adapted to context | Risk integrated with strategy, objective-setting and performance |
| Enterprise applicability | Generic across for-profit, NFP and public entities | Corporate-origin approach adaptable to different entities |
| Director use | Is the process integrated and tailored, and are risks assessed and treated? | Do governance and culture connect to choices, performance and reporting? |
| Typical failure | A process checklist without strategic choices | Detailed performance architecture detached from controls or local legitimacy |
| Authority | Voluntary guideline unless adopted by contract, regulator or policy | Voluntary framework unless separately made applicable |

The AICD appendix has a comparison table, but neither ISO nor COSO provides a universal mechanical algorithm for a board to decide risk appetite. An entity might draw on both, with its law, rights, financial capacity and organisational context taking precedence. Do not reproduce licensed framework diagrams. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 18-22]].

### 03.10.3 Inherent, current and residual risk

**Inherent risk** is commonly assessed assuming relevant mitigating controls are absent; **current residual risk** is the risk remaining given controls as actually operating; **target residual risk** is the estimated risk after planned remediation. These are **assessment conventions**, not unique, directly measurable physical quantities. Directors must know whether an 'inherent' score means a plausible unprotected exposure or a fictional world with every safeguard removed, and whether the 'residual' rating assumes all claimed controls actually work.

**IAE hypothetical cross-contamination chain:** Source: shared baking line. Event: insufficiently removed gluten contaminates a supposedly gluten-free product. Consequences: serious consumer harm, recall, litigation, lost trust, downtime and lost cash. Preventive safeguards include physical segregation, production sequencing, cleaning and independent allergen validation; detective safeguards include batch testing and traceable records; corrective safeguards include rapid recall and effective communications. Backups preserve traceability and evidence; insurance may transfer some cash loss but cannot prevent physical harm. Risk falls only to the extent the control system actually reduces event likelihood or severity. [Curriculum foundation: AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 133-142, 173-179]].

### 03.10.4 Bow-tie, treatment and control hierarchy

AICD introduces **bow-tie analysis** to distinguish threats/causes, the central unwanted event, and the multiple consequences; preventive controls operate to the left and mitigative/recovery controls to the right. It describes responses often termed **avoid**, **reduce/treat**, **share/transfer** and **accept**. A board should distinguish the event from its causes and impacts, assess whether each critical barrier is independent, and test its reliability. Outsourcing, insurance or contract indemnity may redistribute liability or cost but rarely eliminates operational, reputational, statutory or cultural accountability. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 133-142]].

A simple control taxonomy is: **prevent** (permissions, segregation, safety interlocks); **detect** (monitoring, exception alerts, sampling); **respond** (recall, containment, incident team); **recover** (tested backups, alternative service sites, supplier substitution); **govern** (delegations, policy, independent assurance). In a hazardous process, physical and engineered controls may be more dependable than training or posters, though the correct mix depends on the hazard and applicable safety law.

### 03.10.5 Control design, operation and assurance tests

| Test | Question | Evidence to request | Red flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design | Does the control interrupt the actual causal path? | Process map, hazards, required standard, control specification | Control is a policy statement without a barrier. |
| Ownership | Who executes, maintains and checks it? | Named control owner, position description, delegated authority | Everyone assumes another team is responsible. |
| Operation | Was it performed reliably throughout the test period? | Timestamped logs, sample results, access records, reconciliations | Manual attestations with no samples or exceptions. |
| Independence | Can one failure defeat all claimed safeguards? | Dependency mapping, segregation of duties, alternate systems | Production system and backups share credentials. |
| Exception response | What happens when a control fails? | Incident and breach logs, shutdown criteria, change tickets | Repeated waivers without risk approval. |
| Assurance | Is the control tested by someone suitably independent? | Internal audit, external technical validation, corrective-action closure | Annual self-rating treated as independent evidence. |
| Recovery | Can required service levels be restored? | Restoration exercise, end-to-end scenario, communications test | 'Backup exists' but restore has never been demonstrated. |

**Board decision:** adopt proportionate governance architecture, approve appetite and major risk policies, ensure management is accountable, and insist on independent information about critical controls. **Escalate:** any uncontrolled severe harm, sustained out-of-tolerance exposure, failed critical control, inaccurate attestation, or risk accepted without the appropriate authorisation. The risk maturity director tool explicitly warns against an unaffordable, gold-plated system for a small NFP. [AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, p. 16]; AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 117-126]].

## 03.11 Board, management, committees and the Three Lines Model

### 03.11.1 The board retains accountability for the risk architecture

The board establishes risk appetite and risk governance, challenges aggregate exposure, ensures competent management, oversees culture and resilience, and decides significant matters reserved to it. Management identifies, owns, treats and reports risks in operations and projects. A specialist risk officer should support informed challenge, not become the sole 'owner of all risks'. Audit provides assurance, not management of the underlying process. AICD stresses clear policy hierarchy, directors' oversight and the need for boards to obtain advice and assurance rather than delegate accountability away. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 97-98, 111-124, 127-132]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 113-117]].

### 03.11.2 The Institute of Internal Auditors' model, not 'three defences'

The **Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA) Three Lines Model (2020)** differentiates: **governing body**, accountable for oversight and stakeholder stewardship; **first-line management**, delivering activities and owning associated risk; **second-line management expertise**, providing policy, specialist support, monitoring and challenge; and **third-line internal audit**, providing independent and objective assurance and advice. External auditors and regulators are other parties, not an automatic substitute for internal audit. Calling this 'three lines of defence' can encourage a defensive silo model and obscure the fact that risk-taking and performance are required. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 114-116]; IIA, *The IIA's Three Lines Model*, July 2020.]

The distinction concerns **roles and independence**, not a compulsory three-department structure. A small charity may combine first- and second-line responsibilities, but should then compensate with trustee/board review, segregation where possible and periodic external checks. For an RNTBC, the model does not replace rights-holder consent or cultural accountability: an assurance report may verify that a consultation process occurred but cannot confer authority if the wrong people decided.

### 03.11.3 Risk committee, audit committee and other committees

A risk committee gives time to enterprise risks, appetite, controls, culture, non-financial risks, extreme scenarios and independent assurance; an audit committee ordinarily concentrates on financial reporting, audit, accounting judgments and associated controls. A combined committee may be proportionate in smaller organisations, but can underinvest in strategy risk because periodic reporting and audit dominate its agenda. A sustainability committee may concentrate on climate, social, environmental and related disclosures, yet must coordinate with risk and audit to avoid gaps or double counting. The full board should own the strategic exposure and approval of appetite. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 111-114]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 17-23, 113-117, 125-127]].

The TOOL risk committee charter is a **model**, not an obligatory committee structure for every entity. It addresses purpose, authority to seek information and advice, membership and independence, reporting, risk policy, culture, insurance and review. The sustainability charter provides an example of ESG strategy, reporting, targets, trends and compliance oversight. A board should adapt both to actual delegation and constitution. Do not uncritically transplant template numerical membership or meeting requirements into small charities or CATSI corporations.

### 03.11.4 Listed and prudentially regulated entities: different obligations

At **27 October 2026**, the **ASX Corporate Governance Council fourth-edition Principles and Recommendations** remain in effect. The 21 July 2026 draft fifth edition was issued for **consultation** (submissions closed 14 September 2026), and was not binding or operative at the verification date. Fourth-edition Principle 7 and Recommendations 7.1-7.4 address risk oversight committee arrangements (or disclosure of alternative processes), at-least-annual risk-framework review and disclosure, internal audit disclosure/alternative process, and material environmental or social exposures. These recommendations are applied through ASX listing governance disclosure arrangements; they are not identical to directly imposed prudential standards and should not be called universal statutory duties. Principle 8 links remuneration to values and risk appetite. [AICD[GAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 8-9]; ASX, *Corporate Governance Principles and Recommendations* webpage, current fourth edition and fifth-edition consultation status checked 27 October 2026; submissions closed 14 September 2026.]

For **entities within the scope of APRA's prudential standards**, distinct binding standards apply. **CPS 220 Risk Management** requires an appropriate framework, board-approved risk appetite statement and risk management strategy, material-risk coverage, a board-approved rolling business plan of at least three years, risk reporting and specified reviews. **CPS 234 Information Security** requires appropriately sized information security capabilities and assurance. **CPS 230 Operational Risk Management**, listed by APRA as in force from **1 July 2026**, addresses material operational risks, critical operations, disruption tolerances and service-provider risk. Do not apply these standards to all ASX-listed firms, and note that some sectors use corresponding standards, such as superannuation's SPS 220. [APRA, CPS 220, CPS 230, CPS 234, standards pages checked 27 October 2026.]

### 03.11.5 A practical risk RACI and escalation system

**IAE indicative allocation** (*R = responsible for work; A = accountable for authorised decision; C = consulted; I = informed*; the board's statutory obligations cannot be removed by a table):

| Activity | Full board | Risk committee | CEO / first line | Risk/compliance second line | Internal audit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Approve appetite and strategic risk bounds | A | C/recommends | C | C/prepares analysis | I |
| Identify, assess and manage risks in operations | Oversight | Oversight | A/R | C/challenge | Independent assurance |
| Maintain risk method and reporting | Oversight | Review | A | R | Test design and performance |
| Test critical control effectiveness | Require evidence | Review results | A/R for remediation | Monitoring | Independent assurance where within audit plan |
| Major capital/strategy go-no-go | A when reserved | Risk advice | R for proposal and execution | C | May assure process, not approve investment |
| Report breach or crisis | Oversight and reserved decisions | Escalated review | A/R operational command | C/regulatory advice | Later independent review |

**Escalation protocol:** (1) define immediate triggers, time and recipients by risk category; (2) prohibit management from suppressing a breach pending the next routine meeting; (3) preserve emergency board-convening mechanisms; (4) agree who communicates with regulators and affected people; (5) keep independent assurance channels available; and (6) log board action, owners and closure evidence.

## 03.12 Risk measurement, aggregation and board reporting

### 03.12.1 A board report must describe exposure and change, not merely colour

AICD distinguishes **KPIs** (are objectives being achieved?), **key risk indicators (KRIs)** (is the exposure changing and within appetite?) and **key control indicators (KCIs)** (are risk mitigations working?). It explains the use of lead and lag indicators, risk registers, risk profiles, risk treatment, dashboards and escalation. The director-tools headline risk questions connect strategy to future free cash flow, returns, refinancing, operations and assurance. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 75-83, 102, 125-126, 130-145]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 89-90]].

| Strategic objective | KPI (outcome) | KRI (leading exposure) | KCI (control evidence) | Illustrative escalation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Safe production | Customer complaints and defects per million units | Detected contamination precursors and cleaning nonconformances | Validated cleaning tests passed and batch traceability | Any verified contaminated release: immediate incident response and chair contact. |
| Resilient cash flows | Actual free cash flow against forecast | Forecast 12-month liquidity headroom; upcoming refinancing | Treasury covenant monitoring completed; forecast accuracy | Forecast liquidity below board minimum: urgent funding review. |
| Cyber continuity | Critical service uptime and restoration duration | Unpatched critical exposure, credential compromise and unusual access | Tested restore success, multifactor authentication and segmentation | Restore exercise fails objective: remediate before critical rollout. |
| Charity outcomes | Proportion of clients with sustained outcome | Declining referrals, rising waiting time, loss of core grant | Safeguarding checks and grant acquittals completed | Service capacity below critical beneficiary threshold: board review. |
| RNTBC legitimacy | Decisions implemented in accordance with holder direction | Deferred consultations, unresolved representation disputes | Verified consent process and documentary evidence | No affected holder consent where legally required: do not enter agreement. |

These are original illustrations. Metric definitions, populations, units, denominators and periodicity must be agreed. A simple count can be misleading: 'one incident' could represent trivial error or widespread exposure, and reported incidents may initially rise when employees finally trust the speak-up system.

### 03.12.2 Why a heat map is not a quantified exposure measure

Ordinal likelihood labels (1 = rare to 5 = almost certain) and consequence labels (1 = minor to 5 = catastrophic) are **rankings**. Multiplying 4 × 5 to obtain 'risk 20' does not mean expected loss is 20 dollars, 20% or twice a score of 10. Differences between ordinal categories are not defined on a cardinal scale. Such products may assist prioritisation if definitions are consistent, but they are not values at risk, objective frequencies or additive financial exposures. A heat map can also obscure low-likelihood irreversible harm, concentration, interconnected risks and uncertain evidence. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 125-126, 136]; mathematical interpretation is IAE.]

**IAE counterexample:** Risk A is a 10% annual chance of an A$10m loss, giving an **expected annual loss of A$1m**, if probability and loss size are credible. Risk B is a 0.1% annual chance of an A$500m loss, giving **expected annual loss of A$0.5m**. A policy that ranks risks solely by expected loss might rank B lower even though B could destroy the entity. B must therefore also be assessed against capacity, tolerance and catastrophic harm. Conversely, giving B a red heat-map score does not produce a mathematically valid A$500m probability distribution. Frequency, horizon, exposure and severity belong in the source data.

### 03.12.3 Quantification where evidence permits

**IAE methods, not AICD-prescribed techniques:**

- **Sensitivity analysis** varies a single driver (price, cost, timing, adoption or volume), holding others fixed, to reveal the main determinants of value. It misses interactions unless explicitly designed.
- **Scenario analysis** jointly changes plausible correlated variables to show different futures and management responses. It need not assign probabilities where they cannot be defended.
- **Stress testing** imposes severe yet meaningful shocks to explore survival, funding, controls and recovery. Reverse stress testing starts with a failure condition and asks what combinations could produce it.
- **Decision trees** make staged choices, conditional outcomes and decision authority explicit. Where branches have defensible probabilities, expected values can be calculated; otherwise use qualitative decision branches.
- **Value at risk (VaR)** is a statistical loss quantile for a defined horizon, confidence level, distribution and portfolio, not a maximum possible loss. A one-year 95% VaR of A$25m would mean the model places approximately 5% probability on losses **exceeding** A$25m; it says little about their average size beyond that threshold.
- **Expected shortfall** averages losses in a specified adverse tail under the model and is more informative about severity there, although still dependent on modelling choices.
- **Risk-adjusted NPV / option value** accounts for state-specific cash outcomes, irreversibility and decisions. It must not double-count risk in both highly conservative cash flows and a premium-loaded discount rate without explanation.

Quantification is appropriate where it adds decision insight. An invented 97.5% confidence interval for an unprecedented geopolitical scenario is generally inferior to transparent, qualitative conditional paths with tested triggers. A numeric statement must identify entity scope, currency, nominal/real basis, time horizon, probability convention, correlation and uncertainty bounds.

### 03.12.4 Aggregation: why separate department summaries fail

**IAE numerical illustration:** Two projects each expose A$15m to a supply disruption, and both rely on the same logistics corridor. Adding expected losses is defensible only under a common scenario model; treating the two worst-case A$15m losses as independent may materially understate their simultaneous A$30m cash demand. If both also require the same limited workforce to recover operations, recovery times and indirect losses can compound. An insurer's aggregated exclusions and a bank's covenant test may further magnify the group effect.

The risk report should therefore show concentration by common driver, geography, customer, supplier, counterparty, technology platform, financing source and *decision dependency*. Explain diversification assumptions and correlations during stress; independence in normal times may disappear in crises. Do not add ordinal risk scores across categories as if they were dollars.

### 03.12.5 Board reporting format and assurance

An effective periodic report contains: the top risk and opportunity **changes** since last meeting; events or assumptions altering strategic feasibility; objective metrics relative to appetite and capacity; critical control failures and testing outcomes; correlated exposures; upcoming external events; management action and overdue closure; incidents and near misses; independent assurance; exception decisions; and a clear statement of matters requiring board attention. Include a small number of major strategic risks with deeper detail, rather than listing dozens of minor register entries. The AICD risk reporting curriculum emphasises information quality, forward-looking monitoring and insight beyond a register. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 127-132, 143-145]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 39-40]].

**Board challenge questions:** What is worse than last month, despite the red/amber/green status not changing? What is outside appetite now? Which controls have failed under realistic testing? What material risks are excluded from the dashboard because they cannot be quantified? Where do the top risks amplify one another? What would a sceptical external expert conclude from the underlying evidence? Who independently validates management's claim that corrective actions are complete?

**Red flags:** every risk marked 'stable', no material near misses, unchanging appetite after major growth, no control-failure data, risk reports prepared only for audit committee, no trend/horizon stated, probability scores with arbitrary arithmetic, overdue remediation treated as closed because a new policy was approved, and material strategic choices not reconciled to aggregate risk.

## 03.13 Strategic execution, adaptive monitoring and course correction

### 03.13.1 Strategic approval starts execution governance

The most polished strategic plan fails if capabilities, capital, incentives, dependencies and accountability are not aligned. AICD distinguishes strategy development from execution, warns that organisational structure should follow the chosen strategy and encourages directors to monitor leading as well as lagging indicators. **Execution does not mean mechanically following the original plan after evidence disproves its assumptions.** It means delivering intended value while responding intelligently when the environment changes. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 71-86]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-26]].

Every material strategic initiative should have a clear **accountable executive**, decision authority, approved budget, intended outcomes, critical path, dependencies, control gate, risk boundary, performance baseline, learning milestone and escalation trigger. A project-management report of percentage complete is insufficient if it does not show whether the business case is becoming stronger or weaker. A plant might be 95% constructed yet unfit for intended use, commercially obsolete or impossible to operate within new permit conditions.

### 03.13.2 Convert assumptions into falsifiable signposts

**IAE assumption register:**

| Approved proposition | Assumption and linked measurement | Trigger and board action |
|---|---|---|
| New product wins profitable demand | Repeat purchase and contribution margin after quality and distribution costs | Two consecutive quarters materially below the break-even trajectory: pause expansion; validate positioning and cost assumptions. |
| Technology lowers operating cost | Throughput per labour hour and defect/error rate | Benefit shortfall with rising control exceptions: require independent review before further capital. |
| Expansion can be financed safely | Monthly liquidity forecast and covenant headroom under downside | Crossing the approved cash buffer: freeze discretionary commitments and table financing options. |
| Charity intervention produces outcomes | Agreed beneficiary outcome at defined follow-up points | Outcomes stagnate despite increased service spend: test causal theory and consult beneficiaries. |
| RNTBC agreement is properly authorised and deliverable | Holder consultation/consent record, obligations and benefits ledger | Unresolved affected-holder authority or rights objection: withhold native title decision pending proper process and advice. |

Signposts should measure **the assumptions**, not just activity. A construction schedule measures progress; customer willingness to pay tests demand; verified restorative ability tests cyber resilience; ability of local communities to make decisions tests legitimacy. Avoid measuring only outputs that management can readily influence while ignoring the causal objective.

### 03.13.3 Monitoring rhythm and corrective action

The annual board calendar should separate the strategic review cycle from routine operating reports, with sufficient time allocated for longer-term decisions. A practical arrangement is **monthly** dashboards for critical execution and liquidity, **quarterly** reconsideration of strategic assumptions and major risks, **annual** structured strategy and appetite refresh, and **event-driven** review upon material triggers. These intervals are original examples, not universal legal deadlines. AICD endorses ongoing strategic conversations, periodic review and attention to material decisions that arise between plans. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 75-85]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 3-11]].

When a threshold is breached, distinguish: **correct execution** (the strategy remains sound but delivery falters); **reallocate** (other initiatives now offer better value); **redesign** (new risk treatment or operating model); **pause** (critical evidence missing); **pivot** (the original hypothesis is wrong); **exit** (no acceptable viable pathway); and **invoke contingency** (cash, safety, service continuity or rights at immediate risk). The board should ask whether the remedy truly changes outcomes or merely reschedules a failing milestone.

### 03.13.4 Incentive and capital feedback loops

Key results must be linked to both outcomes and behaviour. A team compensated only for sales, project approvals or throughput will have reasons to ignore quality, margin, compliance and cash. A renewable-energy development team rewarded for megawatts approved may select poor projects; a charity rewarded for number of services delivered may displace hard-to-serve clients; a mining or RNTBC venture assessed only by agreement revenue may disregard Country or holder accountability. Risk-adjusted incentives should recognise sustainable outcomes and the way they are achieved. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 79-83, 146-155]].

**Escalation questions:** Which strategic assumptions have already failed? Are executives reporting deteriorating prospects promptly? How much unspent capital remains genuinely cancellable? Are current controls adequate for the next operating phase? Does management's latest forecast include realistic remediation costs? **Red flags:** activity KPIs improve while cash or community outcomes deteriorate; repeated 'temporary' waivers; shifting definitions of success; assurance required only after launch; or board review deferred until the end of a multi-year plan.

## 03.14 Risk culture, incentives, accountability and challenge

### 03.14.1 Culture is revealed by behaviour under pressure

**Risk culture** is the shared pattern of incentives, judgment, decision making and accountability affecting risk-taking, escalation and control. It is observed in what people do when sales targets, productivity pressure, scarce funds and safety or compliance requirements conflict. Board policy alone does not establish culture. AICD explicitly connects performance, compliance, risk and culture, discusses incentives and oversight, and uses cases where financial success masked serious non-financial risk weakness. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 24-26, 146-156]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 89-99]].

A board shapes culture through director behaviour, CEO appointment, remuneration and consequences, policies, resources, independent assurance and attention to bad news. Directors should check whether management is consistently rewarded for **good outcomes achieved lawfully and responsibly** rather than for financial metrics alone. A person who stops unsafe production at personal cost should not be penalised for missing output targets. Employees need confidence that problems will be acted upon without retaliation.

### 03.14.2 Sources of risk blindness

AICD discusses organisational complacency, the possibility of 'high reliability' behaviour in hazardous settings, failure to learn and warning signs from financial institutions. The CBA case illustrates how strong results may reduce urgency about controls. The source also invokes the Washington Mutual example and Wells Fargo incentive failures as warnings about growth, sales and operational risk. Treat these as **illustrative historical examples**, not proof that every similar incentive structure causes misconduct. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 82-83, 150-155, 180-184]].

**IAE mechanisms:** confirmation bias selects evidence favouring a chosen strategy; authority bias discourages contradiction of a founder or CEO; normalisation of deviance converts recurring exceptions into accepted practice; diffusion of responsibility makes each committee assume another has managed the problem; success bias equates high profit with high control quality; and fear of retaliation suppresses early signals. The common corrective is disciplined challenge and independently verifiable evidence, not performative cynicism.

### 03.14.3 High-reliability and speak-up behaviours

High-reliability organisation research, discussed in the AICD notes through Weick and Sutcliffe, is relevant where consequences of error are severe. Directors should look for sustained attention to small failures, reluctance to oversimplify, sensitivity to front-line operations, capacity to recover and appropriate deference to expertise. These are behavioural themes, not certification criteria. The relevant question is whether experts can override hierarchy when safety or critical operations demand it. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 151-153]].

An effective speak-up system has confidential and protected channels appropriate to law; plain-language thresholds for bad news; independent escalation when supervisors are involved; protection against reprisal; closure feedback; and trend analysis. Governance should test both **frequency** and **treatment** of reports. Very few reports may mean a healthy operation or a fearful one; neither conclusion follows automatically. Reports must not be used to punish teams for uncovering risk.

### 03.14.4 An original culture diagnostic

| Board observation | Supporting test | Possible concern |
|---|---|---|
| Incentives emphasise growth | Compare bonus scorecards, exceptions, complaints and risk outcomes by division | Staff optimising a narrow metric. |
| Directors hear no adverse reports | Independent interviews and speak-up trend review | Filtered information, retaliation, or poor detection. |
| Controls frequently waived | Sample waiver authority, period and closure evidence | Controls exist only in policies. |
| Compliance is a second-line task only | Observe first-line decision papers and executive reviews | Risk is seen as somebody else's work. |
| Risk assessment unchanged despite strategy shift | Reconcile risk register to capital projects and new services | Ceremonial appetite; failure to evaluate new exposures. |
| Leaders publicly welcome challenge | Track treatment of actual dissent and promotion consequences | Words and conduct diverge. |
| Stakeholder participation is formally recorded | Independently validate participation, accessibility and decision rights | Consultation without influence or required consent. |

**Board interventions:** commission a targeted independent review when indicators diverge; require the CEO to address root causes, not only re-educate staff; redesign incentives; enforce consequences proportionate to conduct and context; improve safety-critical controls; ensure cultural and local language accessibility; and review board behaviour itself. Directors should minute how cultural evidence affected decisions. Culture is not a proxy for psychological diagnosis of individuals.

## 03.15 Crisis governance, continuity and organisational resilience

### 03.15.1 A crisis is a change in governance tempo, not automatic transfer of management

A crisis is a severe, time-sensitive event that threatens people, critical operations, solvency, rights, trust or the organisation's continuity and may overwhelm normal procedures. It can be sudden (cyber attack, fatality, contamination) or slow developing (liquidity erosion, culture failure, repeated misconduct, climate transition). **Operational resilience** is the capacity to prevent, withstand, adapt to and recover from disruption while maintaining critical outcomes within tolerable limits. A board cannot eliminate uncertainty; it can demand preparedness and a tested ability to respond. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 156-163]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 43-44]].

The CEO and operational incident commander usually run the response. The board must protect life and welfare, maintain governance, obtain reliable information, safeguard the entity's lawful purposes, decide reserved matters, ensure adequate liquidity and oversight, and monitor external communications and regulator obligations. Chair and management should agree who speaks and when. An emergency does not waive legal obligations, holder decision rights, conflict rules or solvency considerations. In a serious incident, rapidly obtaining specialist legal, safety, cyber, communications and financial advice may be essential.

### 03.15.2 Preparation before the crisis

The board should approve or require a proportionate crisis governance policy, with: escalation criteria; incident-command roles; emergency meeting and delegations protocol; alternative communication channels when corporate systems fail; stakeholder and regulator contacts; critical operations; inventory of dependencies; risk treatment and business continuity plans; insurance information; off-site recovery and records; authority to close unsafe operations; and support for affected people. The AICD material emphasises crisis parameters, communications, board meeting powers, realistic exercises and learning. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 157-160]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 33, 113-117]].

**Board exercise:** an industrial incident simultaneously disables the site network and primary suppliers. Require a scenario in which (a) staff are safe, (b) the safety-critical service stops, (c) employee and client data may be compromised, (d) lenders require information and (e) the regulator may expect immediate reporting. Test first hour, first day, first week and first month. If a restoration plan cannot be executed without the unavailable network or absent key personnel, the plan is inadequate.

### 03.15.3 Operational tolerances and recovery objectives

An organisation should identify **critical operations**, set maximum tolerable disruption periods and minimum acceptable service, determine recovery time objectives (RTOs), and set recovery point objectives (RPOs) for tolerable data loss. These are separate metrics: a 24-hour RTO specifies when service should be restored; a four-hour RPO describes how much recent data may be lost. They are not universally prescribed values. A recovery plan claiming 12 hours is inadequate if its last full end-to-end test took 72 hours. For APRA-regulated entities, CPS 230 sets specific requirements for critical operations and tolerances; others can adapt the concepts without claiming CPS 230 legally applies to them. [APRA CPS 230, *Operational Risk Management*, current standard checked 27 October 2026.]

**IAE illustration:** An emergency helpline needs to function during a flood. Board-approved tolerance: no more than 30 minutes without an alternative contact path, basic triage must remain available, and restoration of all case-management functions within eight hours. A plan that merely promises 99.9% average availability may obscure a single 24-hour outage. The values should be derived from service criticality, not copied from this example.

### 03.15.4 Crisis communication and board decisions

A credible crisis response prioritises affected people, verified facts, protection from further harm, regulatory obligations, honest uncertainty, corrective action and accountable updates. The board should not speculate on causes before evidence is available or focus on public relations at the expense of remedy. AICD contrasts historical responses to the Dreamworld disaster and Samarco dam failure to discuss speed, compassion, executive involvement and credibility, but those source discussions are secondary commentary, not a formal judicial determination of comparative director performance. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 158-160]].

| Phase | Board's principal role | Evidence or action |
|---|---|---|
| Detection/first hours | Confirm physical safety, operational containment, incident leadership and legal reporting | Situation report, affected persons, delegated decisions, urgent advisers |
| Stabilisation/days | Preserve liquidity and operations, secure records, communicate and assess consequences | Rolling cash, safety/technical verification, regulator and stakeholder log |
| Recovery/weeks | Challenge restoration plans, support victims/beneficiaries, oversee control remediation | Verified recovery tests, service continuity, independent evidence |
| Renewal/months | Examine root causes and accountability; change strategy or controls where needed | Board-approved action plan, assurance, cultural and capital review |

### 03.15.5 Post-crisis learning and assurance

Do not treat the crisis as resolved because media attention subsides or systems restart. Separate an **incident chronology**, **immediate causes**, **systemic causes**, **governance and incentive causes**, **harm and redress**, **control remediation**, and **long-term changes to strategy and risk appetite**. Require independent review commensurate with severity, clear ownership, completion evidence and at least one subsequent exercise to demonstrate that fixes work. Preserve documents consistent with legal requirements and investigations. AICD treats learning as an explicit stage of crisis resilience. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 161-162]].

**Immediate escalation triggers:** threat to life, safety or essential services; inability to verify safe operations; likely insolvency; material cyber/data exposure; loss of a major licence or partner; a potential unauthorised native title decision; major unlawful practice; loss exceeding risk capacity; or a crisis that requires board-reserved decisions. Escalation should be **event driven** and should not await an ordinary quarterly risk review.

## 03.16 Emerging threats and opportunities

### 03.16.1 Emerging risk is a test of the business model and governance fitness

AICD discusses global risk rankings, digital disruption, cyber, AI, complex external change, workforce and climate/environment issues. The **World Economic Forum's 2025 Global Risks Report** rankings in the appendix reflect survey perceptions for defined horizons, not objective forecast probabilities for a particular Australian organisation in October 2026. A board should use such lists to ask what might be missing, then build its own risk pathways, value consequences and preparedness. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 163-166]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 1-13]].

### 03.16.2 AI: opportunity and control failure pathways

Artificial intelligence (AI) and generative AI may improve knowledge access, forecasting, detection, productivity, service responsiveness and organisational experimentation. Risks include personal or commercially sensitive data leakage, fabricated outputs, biased decisions, lack of explainability, inaccessible services, intellectual property disputes, vendor dependence, workforce deskilling and untested automation of high-stakes judgments. An AI solution can reduce processing cost yet create unacceptable errors or loss of stakeholder trust. **Operational feasibility does not prove safe, lawful or economically useful deployment.**

AICD's LEG course summarises **eight AI governance elements** from 2024 AICD/Human Technology Institute guidance: roles and responsibilities; people, skills and culture; governance structures; principles, policies and strategy; practical processes and controls; stakeholder engagement and impact assessment; supporting infrastructure and data governance; and monitoring, reporting and evaluation. Boards should know what AI systems are actually used, including unapproved tools, assign system owners, classify high-impact uses and insist on human accountability. [AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 108-111]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, p. 7]].

**IAE AI decision gate:** require an inventory of deployed systems, intended users, permitted data, vendor terms, training-data provenance where ascertainable, data minimisation and retention, role-based access, output verification, accessibility/bias testing, human review for consequential decisions, incident/appeal mechanisms, measurable benefit and stop conditions. For a board pack summariser, test factual accuracy and confidential information boundaries. For a service eligibility model, test lawful decision basis, fairness, explanations and effective human appeal before use. Do not conflate a voluntary AI governance standard with a universally operative AI statute: check actual federal, state, sector, privacy, employment and consumer obligations for the use case.

### 03.16.3 Cyber, data and privacy: a connected operational and legal risk

The LEG course directs directors to understand **what data is held, why, where, by whom, for how long and how it is protected**, rather than treating cyber as only a technology problem. Privacy, cyber and production systems interact; a cyber event can make it impossible to demonstrate product safety or defend litigation. Legal privacy duties depend on entity coverage and exceptions; a simplistic claim that 'all entities under A$3m turnover are exempt' is wrong. Australian Privacy Principle (APP) 11 requires covered APP entities to take reasonable protection measures and, subject to exceptions, destroy or de-identify information no longer required; the October 2025 OAIC guidance emphasises technical and organisational controls. [AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 103-108]; OAIC, *Chapter 11: APP 11 Security of personal information*, updated 3 October 2025.]

**Current notification distinctions (27 October 2026):** for entities within the **Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme**, a suspected eligible breach should be assessed promptly, with reasonable steps to complete the assessment within **30 calendar days** after the relevant suspicion arises; once there are reasonable grounds to believe an eligible breach occurred, notification to the OAIC and affected individuals is required as soon as practicable, subject to law and exceptions. This is **not a generic 30-day permission to delay all notification**. Separately, the **Cyber Security Act 2024 (Cth)**, s 27, and 2025 rules require specified reporting business entities (including businesses whose previous-year turnover **exceeds A$3m**, subject to the annualisation rule for a part-year business, and relevant critical-infrastructure entities) to report **a ransomware/cyber extortion payment** within **72 hours** of making or becoming aware of the payment. It is **not** a 72-hour reporting rule for every cyber incident or every charity; other APRA, critical infrastructure, contractual and sectoral notification duties may also apply. [OAIC, NDB quick reference and Part 4 guidance, checked 27 October 2026; Cyber Security Act 2024 (Cth) s 27; Cyber Security (Ransomware Payment Reporting) Rules 2025, s 6; Australian Signals Directorate reporting page.]

**Cyber evidence:** tested restoration of critical systems, architecture and dependencies, third-party and privileged-access inventory, penetration and vulnerability outcomes, staff phishing/reporting practice, incident command, data classification, retention/deletion records, incident notification decision tree and assurance. AICD's cybersecurity checklist, ASIC's official board cyber questions and the Australian Signals Directorate's Essential Eight maturity guidance are useful reference points but no one checklist guarantees security. [AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, p. 33]; AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 3-6]; ASIC, *Key questions for an organisation's board of directors*; ASD, *Essential Eight Maturity Model* guidance, checked 27 October 2026.]

### 03.16.4 Climate, nature and the physical operating environment

Distinguish **physical risks** (flood, heat, storm, drought, fire, changing ecosystems), **transition risks** (policy, technology, market, financing, customer preferences), **liability and disclosure risks**, and **dependencies and impacts on nature** (water, biodiversity, land, approvals and restoration). Climate action can create value or lower risk in one dimension while increasing nature or community harms in another. Directors should request asset- and location-specific scenario analysis over actual economic lives, not just near-term corporate emissions targets. The consequences may include shutdown, capital replacement, permit loss, insurance affordability, supply disruption and stranded assets. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 1-2]; AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 163-165]; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 90-96]].

Australia's mandatory sustainability/climate reporting is **phased and entity-specific**. Directors should determine whether the entity falls within applicable Corporations Act sustainability reporting requirements and standards for its reporting period. A strategic risk assessment is not itself a statutory disclosure, and a voluntary climate framework should not be described as binding simply because investors expect it. Refer to the planned [File 05](05_Financial_Reporting_Audit_and_Assurance.md) for the detailed reporting thresholds and accounting/assurance requirements; do not infer those thresholds from this discussion. ASIC's current financial reporting focus stresses material business risks and balanced operating and financial review disclosure for relevant listed entities. [ASIC, *Financial reporting and audit focus areas*, checked 27 October 2026.]

### 03.16.5 Workforce, supply chains, geopolitics and technology transition

**Workforce:** scarce technical skills, ageing workforce, key-person dependence, psychosocial hazards, industrial relations, leadership succession and perverse performance incentives can stop strategy even when funding exists. Test whether capability exists over the whole project life and whether the organisation can maintain safe service during change. **Supply chain:** supplier concentration, geographic chokepoints, critical inputs, poor traceability, subcontractor conduct, sanctions and alternative sourcing need quantified interruption pathways. **Geopolitical:** conflict, export controls, trade restrictions, sovereign action and foreign-exchange shocks may compound at precisely the time capital and insurance become less available. **Technology:** obsolescence, vendor lock-in, platform collapse or poor system integration can be more significant than adoption of a competing product.

**IAE response options:** preserve strategic flexibility through dual sourcing where economic, modular system architectures, emergency funding, selective inventory buffers, contingent contracting, staff capability development and trigger-based de-risking. All carry costs. A safety stock increase may protect deliveries but consume cash; duplication of critical services may be expensive but justified by downtime consequence. Demonstrate the trade-off in financial and mission scenarios, not simply claim 'resilience' as an unlimited virtue.

### 03.16.6 Emerging-risk radar for a board

| Change signal | Question about strategic value | Quantification/assurance to request | Typical escalation |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI improves a core process | Does verified net benefit justify implementation and governance cost? | Pilot accuracy, labour impact, harm tests, cyber/rights and vendor dependency | Material incorrect or unlawful decisions; data leakage |
| New critical cyber vulnerability | Which services and evidence could become unavailable? | Exposure inventory, patch status, compensating control, restore test | Critical control absent or recovery outside tolerance |
| Demand substitution accelerates | Is the customer/beneficiary value proposition obsolete? | Cohort trends, unit margin, competitor capacity, scenarios | Strategy cannot recover costs or outcomes under updated evidence |
| Extreme weather becomes more frequent | Which sites, clients and suppliers cannot sustain services? | Location-specific asset and supply-chain interruption model | Safety limit or continuity threshold exceeded |
| Government/grant policy changes | Can the business or mission model survive lost support? | Unrestricted cash, revenue concentration, alternative services | Forecast cash below reserve minimum; forced service withdrawal |
| Significant stakeholder/holder challenge | Is the organisation lawfully authorised and legitimate? | Rights, consultation, decision-making and conflict record | Invalid or missing consent; imminent harm to Country |

A radar is valuable only if the board periodically decides what to do differently. Otherwise it becomes another risk register.

## 03.17 NFP, charity, Indigenous corporation and RNTBC adaptations

### 03.17.1 Legal form and decision rights determine what 'best' means

The generic integrated strategy-risk method has useful components across entities, but it is **not legally or ethically identical** everywhere. Directors must first identify whether they are governing a public company, proprietary company, company limited by guarantee, registered charity, incorporated association, CATSI corporation or a registered native title body corporate (RNTBC). Some categories overlap. For example, an incorporated association may also be an ACNC charity; a CATSI corporation may be a registered charity; an RNTBC is a prescribed body corporate subject to native title regulations in addition to its corporate law. The legal objects, constitution/rule book, licensing and rights constraints are prior to an economic optimisation exercise. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-35]; AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 16-18]; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 50-54]].

| Entity | Primary governance focus | Capital/resource constraint | Additional escalation and authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| ASX-listed company | Sustainable business value, shareholder disclosure and multiple stakeholder and compliance considerations | Market funding, covenants, returns on capital, cash, material commitments and disclosure | ASX Listing Rules, applicable Corporations Act and industry rules; market-sensitive information and board-approved appetite |
| Unlisted proprietary / family company | Company interests, founder/family aims within law and constitution; succession | Concentrated ownership, liquidity, related-party transactions, limited external finance | Conflicts, delegations, financial distress and minority rights; family bodies cannot usurp board duties |
| Registered charity, including company limited by guarantee | Charitable purposes, beneficiaries, public confidence and stewardship | Restricted/unrestricted funding, grants, volunteer capacity, reserves | ACNC governance standards; determine other corporate, trust and state obligations in each case |
| State incorporated association | Purposes and member/community outcomes within state statutory regime | Member fees, donations, small budgets, volunteer dependence | State/territory Associations Incorporation Act and rules; additional ACNC layer if charity |
| CATSI corporation | Rule-book purposes, culturally legitimate corporate governance, member and community responsibilities | Often constrained grants, community infrastructure, capability and benefit expectations | CATSI Act, ORIC, rule book, member decisions and specific community protocols |
| RNTBC / PBC | Native title rights and interests, affected holders, Country and lawful benefit stewardship | Corporate operating funding, agreement proceeds, long-horizon and intergenerational obligations | Native Title Act and PBC Regulations; common law holder consent and required processes distinct from ordinary members/directors |
| Public-sector body or government-owned entity | Statutory mandate, public outcomes and accountability to responsible minister/Parliament as applicable | Appropriations, borrowing controls, public resources and mandated outcomes | Enabling Act, public finance directions, ministerial expectations and sector-specific rules |

The table is not an exhaustive legal comparison. In particular, a listed financial institution may face additional APRA requirements; not every unlisted company is exempt from public disclosure; not every CATSI corporation is an RNTBC; and the statutory position of an association differs across jurisdictions.

### 03.17.2 Registered charities and incorporated associations

AICD's 2024 third-edition **Not-for-profit Governance Principles** put purpose, strategy, risk management, performance, stakeholders, sustainability and organisational culture at the centre of board work. The AICD appendix uses The Smith Family's historical shift toward education and poverty prevention as an example of changing the service model without abandoning charitable purpose. The central outcome is meaningful social improvement, not simply more distributions or fundraising. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-26]; AICD, *Not-for-Profit Governance Principles*, third edition, April 2024.]

A charity's **Responsible People** are addressed under the **ACNC Governance Standards**, including **Governance Standard 5**. The obligation is framed as the **charity taking reasonable steps** to ensure responsible people understand and carry out specified duties; it is **not** a statement that all sections of Corporations Act directors' duties apply unaltered to every charity. Other corporate, common-law, statutory or fiduciary duties may still apply. The ACNC also uses revenue-based charity size categories: **small under A$500,000; medium A$500,000 to under A$3m; large A$3m or more**, for reporting periods from 2022 onward. These are not a risk appetite threshold or a solvency test. [ACNC, *Governance Standard 5* and *Charity size*, checked 27 October 2026.]

**IAE charity reserve example:** A registered charity has A$1.2m annual recurring expenditure, A$0.25m unrestricted available cash, A$0.35m restricted grants and a board-defined minimum of three months' essential unrestricted operating expenditure. If the entire A$1.2m is essential and uniformly spent, the illustrative minimum is A$0.30m, so there is **A$0.05m unrestricted shortfall** even though the bank balance totals A$0.60m. The precise reserve requirement is a board policy choice grounded in cash timing, grant restrictions and service continuity, not a universal legal rule. A board cannot automatically spend restricted funds to meet unrelated bills.

For an incorporated association, identify the **actual state/territory statute**, reporting obligations, officer/committee duties, constitution, and registration overlaps. The March 2024 cross-jurisdiction comparison in the LEG appendices is historical teaching and should not be relied on as a current statutory map without fresh verification. For a Western Australian incorporated association, the **Associations Incorporation Act 2015 (WA)** is the relevant state starting point; ACNC obligations are additional when registered as a charity. [AICD[LAPP, 2025/26 V1, p. 5] table dated March 2024; ACNC, *Regulation of charities in Western Australia*, checked 27 October 2026.]

**Charity director questions:** If a grant ended tomorrow, which services would stop first and who would be harmed? Can volunteers safely deliver the strategy? What outcomes are attributable or plausibly linked to the intervention? Are clients meaningfully involved? Are the CEO's fundraising incentives consistent with safeguarding and donor restrictions? Is the strategy financially sustainable even with a large accounting surplus? **Escalate** when safeguarding, service continuity, eligibility, restricted-fund use, grant conditions or solvency become doubtful.

### 03.17.3 CATSI corporations: corporate governance and cultural legitimacy

The **Corporations (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander) Act 2006 (Cth) (CATSI Act)** establishes the corporate regime overseen by the **Office of the Registrar of Indigenous Corporations (ORIC)**. Corporations have a rule book, members, directors, governance powers and reporting obligations. The rule book is a legally operative corporate document and can reflect appropriately chosen community governance arrangements to the extent consistent with applicable law. It is not enough to import a standard listed-company charter and assert compliance. [ORIC, *Rule books*, official guidance checked 27 October 2026; AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 16-18]].

The **Australian Indigenous Governance Institute (AIGI)** identifies four principles: **customised governance, cultural legitimacy, two-way accountability and evolving governance**. They are guidance grounded in Indigenous governance practice, not one homogeneous set of customs applicable to all communities. A corporation should identify its own authorised protocols, modes of deliberation, language access, family/community representation, conflict management and the interface between formal corporate duties and local authority. A board cannot claim cultural legitimacy simply because directors identify as Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander. [AIGI, *Effective Indigenous governance*, official page checked 27 October 2026.]

**Decision diagnostics:** Has the corporation identified who needs to be involved and in what legal or cultural capacity? Are meetings accessible and understandable? How are conflicts of kinship, commercial interest and representation disclosed and managed? Who receives benefits, who bears costs and how are decisions explained back to communities? Are financial accounts accessible and interpreted in forms useful to members? How does the organisation change its decision processes without ignoring legitimate community authority? The answers are community-specific and must not be invented.

### 03.17.4 RNTBCs: common law holders, Country and distinct decision classes

An **RNTBC** is not simply a corporation with 'Indigenous stakeholders'. Native title rights and interests are held by **common law holders** under a determination; the RNTBC generally manages or holds those interests under the **Native Title Act 1993 (Cth)** and **Native Title (Prescribed Bodies Corporate) Regulations 1999**. **Regulation 8** requires relevant consultation and consent before specified native title decisions; regulation 8A addresses permitted alternative consultation processes; regulation 8B addresses compensation applications; regulation 9 concerns certificates. Detailed applicability depends on the decision and relevant conditions. Some common law holders **are not corporation members** and cannot be excluded from native title decisions solely on that basis. [Native Title (Prescribed Bodies Corporate) Regulations 1999, compilation F2024C00961 (14 October 2024), regs 8-10; ORIC, *Decision-making in registered native title bodies corporate*, accessed 27 October 2026.]

**Crucial decision distinction:** ordinary **corporate/operating decisions** (budget, staff, administration and some strategy) generally proceed under the CATSI Act and the corporation's rule book; **native title decisions** (such as qualifying agreements affecting rights or surrender) require the decision rights and consultation/consent processes of affected common law holders. Some boundary cases, including membership eligibility rules, have specific holder decision requirements. A signed board resolution or ordinary members' vote is not automatically a substitute for the proper holders' consent. An RNTBC may also be trustee of property or benefits, introducing further obligations.

| Proposed RNTBC action | First authority question | Board risk test |
|---|---|---|
| Enter a major Indigenous Land Use Agreement affecting native title | Which common law holders are affected and what consent process is prescribed? | Valid informed decision, adequate disclosure, cultural impacts, distribution arrangements, implementation and intergenerational consequences. |
| Change the corporation's annual operating budget | What do CATSI Act and rule book reserve to directors/members? | Liquidity, reporting, conflict management, delivery of authorised services and any restricted funds. |
| Apply for native title compensation | Which affected common law holders or persons entitled to compensation must decide under the relevant rules? | Authority, evidence, representation, legal costs, transparency and future distribution. |
| Employ a CEO or procure accounting services | Which corporate delegation and conflict rules apply? | Skills, probity, value for money, independence and board control. |
| Invest agreement benefits | Who beneficially owns/controls the funds and directs their use? | Trust/contract restrictions, risk capacity, cultural/accountability implications and distribution rights. |
| Permit activity with potential damage to Country | Are native title/heritage permissions and relevant holder decisions established? | Cultural and legal red lines, irreversible harm, environmental controls and independent advice. |

**IAE non-commensurability example:** A development proposal promises A$20m of royalties but would require an activity affecting a place for which the lawful affected holders have not consented. The board cannot 'net' the royalty against a generic cultural loss score to approve the proposal. Its appropriate response is to establish the applicable rights and required process, obtain independent legal and cultural advice, and ensure affected holders decide where the law requires. If consent is not obtained, the proposal cannot proceed through that decision path. If lawfully authorised, directors must still test financial stewardship, benefits, adverse impacts and management capability. Economic appraisal may inform affected holders but does not displace their authority.

### 03.17.5 Public-sector adaptations are not automatic charity rules

The AICD appendix separately discusses government/public-sector strategy, ministerial directions or statements of expectations, enabling legislation, budget and appropriation controls, public value, transparency, mandated planning and the **Australian National Audit Office (ANAO)** perspective on corporate plans and performance monitoring. These are sector-specific governance features; they should not be transposed automatically into a registered charity or CATSI corporation. Public-sector strategy may have several principals and be constrained by statutory functions and policy decisions outside a board's control. [AICD[RAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 27-35]].

**Director test:** distinguish **what the entity is legally required to do**, **what the relevant minister or funder may lawfully direct**, **what management may choose**, and **what the board is responsible for approving or monitoring**. Explicitly model policy and appropriation uncertainty as part of strategic risk, while maintaining accountability for service outcomes and lawful resource use.

## 03.18 Worked AICD strategy-risk cases and decision diagnostics

### 03.18.1 Biscuit Makers of Australia (BMA): fictional instructional case

**Classification and provenance.** *Biscuit Makers of Australia Ltd* is a **fictional AICD case organisation**, using a constructed narrative and figures for teaching; no inference should be drawn about a real Australian business. Course narrative: BMA is a mature biscuit manufacturer with declining market share, ageing plant, higher costs and supermarket concentration. Board attention moved to gluten-free biscuits, which were initially promising, but a product safety failure, recall, cyber-related records loss, insurance gaps and financing strain exposed weaknesses in strategy, risk and assurance. The final case poses a renewed strategic choice, including a possible ketogenic ('keto') biscuit line. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 173-179]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23, 45-46]].

**Source-reported financial evidence**, in A$ million:

| Financial year | Sales | Net profit after tax | Net profit margin (derived) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2020/21 | 102.50 | 6.16 | 6.01% |
| 2021/22 | 99.30 | 4.44 | 4.47% |
| 2022/23 | 105.40 | 5.23 | 4.96% |
| 2023/24 | 95.80 | −2.98 | −3.11% |

Derived: sales fell **A$9.60m (9.11%)** from 2022/23 to 2023/24; NPAT deteriorated by **A$8.21m** and the net margin fell by about **8.07 percentage points**. These are arithmetic comparisons of the fictional source data, not a causal attribution of all deterioration to the gluten-free venture. The source narrative on p.178 states BMA made a loss in 2022/23, **inconsistent** with p.173's positive A$5.23m NPAT for 2022/23; the p.173 table is retained as the numerical source and the inconsistency is unresolved, rather than silently corrected. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 173, 178]].

**What the board knew or should have sought.** The prior board had an audit-and-risk committee but treated risk mainly as an annual CEO discussion and register, rather than a full ERM process. The gluten-free proposal was the only alternative presented; it had strong chair support and involved manufacturing on facilities also used for conventional biscuits, even though gluten cross-contamination was a defining hazard. BMA sold property and obtained a reported **A$10m loan** to fund the initiative; after contamination, it faced consumer claims and an inadequately insured recall. The case reports a cyberattack that caused manufacturing-system disruption and loss of production evidence. About **60%** of traditional sales depended on two dominant supermarkets and **40%** of manufacturing equipment was approaching end of economic life. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 174-178]].

**Integrated failure diagnosis:**

1. **Strategic framing:** trend and market size were examined, but alternative product-market paths, a 'defend-and-renew core' counterfactual and risk-adjusted economics were insufficiently challenged. Having demand for gluten-free products did not prove that BMA could safely and profitably supply them.
2. **Capability and control:** existing hazard analysis and good manufacturing practice for traditional lines did not demonstrate competence in allergen isolation, test frequency, record keeping or new regulatory costs. 'Technically possible to run batches' did not establish safe operating feasibility.
3. **Risk ownership and committee failure:** the audit-and-risk committee did not challenge the novel food safety risk before approval. Management ownership did not replace board responsibility for governance and risk appetite.
4. **Cyber as a common-cause risk:** unavailable data and production systems aggravated operational hazard and impaired legal evidence. Risks were not merely separate 'cyber', 'food quality' and 'litigation' register entries.
5. **Insurance and finance:** policy exclusions left material retained losses; the bank facility, covenant pressure and equipment renewal reduced risk capacity when the strategy failed.
6. **Culture and decision process:** chair advocacy and a single option may have crowded out alternatives, dissent and severe downside analysis. The report identifies complacent standards, deficient staff training and inadequate monitoring.

This diagnosis is an **independent analytical interpretation** of the source events; it is not a court finding that BMA directors breached particular duties.

**Original board decision table, late 2024:**

| Pathway | Potential benefit | Main risk/cost | Evidence needed before approval |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stabilise and renew traditional products | Defends cash and brand, funds safe plant replacement | Mature category and concentrated retail buyers | Unit margins, retailer contracts, equipment renewal/capacity and liquidity stress. |
| Adjacent 'health' range, including keto | May access differentiated segments | Demand fad, misleading product claims, ingredient and manufacturing costs, safety | Independent demand testing, nutrition/claims legal review, segregated pilot, margin and consumer safety evidence. |
| Partnership or contract manufacture | Reduces internal technical burden and capex | Supplier liability, cost, control and traceability | Third-party qualification, contractual assurance, recall arrangements, customer economics. |
| Enter a carefully separated gluten-free operation | May preserve product opportunity | Prior trust failure and very high allergen-control burden | Fresh complete business case, independent safety validation, full funding and board reconsideration of categorical prohibition. |
| Consolidate, divest or orderly retreat | Protects liquidity when renewal returns weak | Jobs, closure costs, lost options and reputational effects | Independent asset valuation, employee obligations, cash waterfall, alternative bids and stakeholder plan. |

**Recommended governance stance:** first stabilise financing, safety, data, claims and core operations. Commission a joint board-management strategic review with multiple genuine alternatives. Do **not** endorse a keto extension just because it is labelled 'healthy'; separate market attractiveness from product safety, claims compliance and BMA's right to win. Require a new integrated ERM process, board-approved risk appetite, control assurance and escalation. Any full launch is conditional on demonstrated competence, positive risk-adjusted value and liquidity resilience. If a proposed gluten-free re-entry is currently explicitly prohibited by the board, managers cannot proceed without a fresh, lawfully made board decision and all required safety safeguards.

**On the 2022 exit decision:** exiting the contaminated line may have been necessary to contain harm and cash exposure. Whether immediate permanent exit was financially optimal cannot be established from the case because credible alternative containment options, full claims, unit economics, costs of safe re-entry, insurance recovery, liquidation value and future costs are not supplied. The most defensible answer is **conditional**: urgent suspension and consumer protection are justified; permanent market exit requires a properly informed comparative decision once immediate dangers are controlled. The board could rationally decide re-entry remains beyond risk appetite. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 176-179]].

**Case questions:** Why did the committee fail to test the new hazard? What were the genuine 2021 alternatives? When should a cross-functional assurance review have occurred? What production and cyber controls are *critical*? How many months of cash survive the recall, debt stress and renewal program together? What would disconfirm the keto demand narrative? What did management know before 2022 that did not reach the board? Which rights of injured consumers cannot be treated merely as a financial cost?

### 03.18.2 Commonwealth Bank / APRA: real prudential inquiry and regulatory remediation

**Classification.** The **Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) 2018 Prudential Inquiry into Commonwealth Bank of Australia (CBA)** was a **real regulatory inquiry**, not a fictional AICD simulation. APRA announced the inquiry in August 2017 and released the final report on **1 May 2018**. It found governance, risk culture and accountability weaknesses, notably involving non-financial risk, despite strong financial performance. The final report set out **35 recommendations**. AICD's 2025/26 case discusses chair Catherine Livingstone's account of a remedial action plan (RAP), board priority, committee structures, 'meta-team' leadership, direct regulator engagement and changes to incentives and escalation. [APRA, *Prudential Inquiry into the Commonwealth Bank of Australia*, final report, 30 April/1 May 2018; AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 180-184]; AICD[RSH, 2025/26 V1, p. 7]].

**Regulatory timeline and outcomes:** APRA's 2018 response included an enforceable undertaking and an additional **A$1bn** operational-risk capital requirement; a financial cost borne by an otherwise strongly performing bank because governance failures altered prudential risk. APRA reduced the add-on to **A$500m on 20 November 2020** and removed the remaining **A$500m effective 30 September 2022** after validation that remediation was sufficiently sustainable and embedded. CBA reported the independent review had assessed its remedial milestones as complete and effective at 30 September 2021; completion of a plan should not be confused with permanent proof that governance risk has disappeared. [APRA, releases dated 1 May 2018, 20 November 2020 and 30 September 2022; CBA, *Completion of Prudential Inquiry Remedial Action Plan*, October 2021.]

**Later sustainability evidence (2026).** On **11 February 2026**, CBA published a separately commissioned review by Promontory, dated 29 January 2026, which concluded that outcomes from the RAP had been sustained across its review focus areas. This is stronger longitudinal evidence than a bare closure announcement, but the review was scoped to RAP outcomes, relied partly on information provided by CBA, and was not an unrestricted certification of all governance, culture or operational risk. [CBA, *RAP Sustainability Review*, Promontory, 29 January 2026; CBA release, 11 February 2026.]

**What failed:** APRA's official materials described institutional success dulling sensitivity to negative signals, complacency, weak challenge, inadequate attention to non-financial risks and unclear or insufficiently enforced accountability. This was more than inadequate policies: intelligence existed but was not escalated, tested, owned or acted upon with enough urgency. Management's culture of collegiality could dilute uncomfortable disagreement. The failure illustrates why an institution's accounting returns and capital adequacy do not alone demonstrate effective governance. [APRA, *Prudential Inquiry Final Report* and follow-up statements.]

**Governance remediation and strategic continuity:** The AICD case describes the board preserving dedicated strategy time while reviewing remediation and operations, using independent review and closure evidence, strengthening the risk function and clearer accountability, bringing the board closer to information beyond the executive team, and revisiting remuneration, conduct and speak-up expectations. This is relevant because a board responding to failure must govern both **repair of controls** and **the organisation's future**. Delegating all remediation to risk staff would fail; halting all strategic thinking for several years could also destroy value. [AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 180-184]].

**IAE director diagnostic:**

| Question | What CBA teaches | What a competent board should verify |
|---|---|---|
| Are good profits proof of governance fitness? | No. Strong results coexisted with material non-financial risk and external capital consequences. | Independent control testing and conduct outcomes, not just earnings. |
| Does a completed remediation plan prove cultural change? | No, though independent milestones and regulator validation strengthen assurance. | Behavioural durability, incentives, follow-up audits and fresh incidents over time. |
| Can the board preserve strategy in a crisis? | Yes, with appropriate committee architecture and information discipline. | Clear decision rights and sufficient protected strategy time. |
| Who owns risk failures? | Accountabilities must connect executives, first-line operations, second-line challenge and board oversight. | Consequence management, control owners, failure escalation, audited closures. |
| Are regulators and stakeholders receiving timely information? | Direct, transparent engagement can improve understanding and trust. | Coordinated, lawful reporting, consistent evidence and well-supported claims. |

**Limits:** CBA is an APRA-regulated large bank with substantial resources. Its capital add-on, prudential risk framework and committee resources cannot be assumed applicable to an ordinary company, charity or RNTBC. The AICD interview and historical findings explain a governance approach, not a universal proof that any particular governance change caused subsequent business performance. Distinguish verified APRA determinations from AICD's interview narrative and this document's interpretation.

### 03.18.3 Comparative diagnosis: BMA, CBA and CQUniversity

| Material | Nature of evidence | Central governance lesson | Misinterpretation to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biscuit Makers of Australia | **Fictional AICD teaching case**, RISK 173-179 | Strategy and risk failures compound through food safety, cyber, financing, committee oversight and culture. | Treating BMA as a real enforcement finding or inventing probabilities and product economics. |
| Commonwealth Bank / APRA | **Real regulatory inquiry, 2018**, followed by documented remediation | Strong financial performance can mask weak non-financial risk; accountability and independent assurance matter. | Assuming the bank's specific prudential standards bind every Australian board. |
| CQUniversity | **Real public 2023 risk appetite example**, selectively excerpted by AICD | Different strategic and compliance activities warrant differentiated appetite and thresholds. | Assuming a qualitative example provides current policy, full limits, or a universal statutory template. |

The common mechanism is **misalignment of ambition, controls, information and authority**. BMA shows the consequence when strategic enthusiasm exceeds actual capability. CBA demonstrates that risk governance must function even during financial success. CQUniversity illustrates how clear risk preferences can support innovation while protecting core obligations. They should be used as complementary examples, not as equivalent empirical research designs.

### 03.18.4 Reusable independent board decision diagnostic

For any new strategic initiative, directors should document the answers to the following integrated questions. A completed checklist is not the decision itself; the board must exercise informed judgment and record its basis.

1. **Mandate:** What is the legal and mission purpose? Whose decision rights apply and what is prohibited?
2. **Strategic thesis:** Which integrated choice is being made, and what credible alternatives, including the counterfactual, are excluded?
3. **Evidence:** What facts and estimates support demand, outcomes, ability to execute, competitive position and timing? What would disprove them?
4. **Capital:** What cash, assets, people, funding and management attention are committed? Does expected ROIC/NPV or mission efficiency support the choice, on consistent assumptions?
5. **Capacity:** What is the actual liquidity, operational and governance capacity to bear loss over the relevant horizon, including existing obligations?
6. **Appetite:** What qualitative restrictions, tolerance/limit metrics and formal prohibitions apply? What is the aggregate position after approval?
7. **Controls:** What are the critical preventive, detective and restorative safeguards, and how has their effectiveness been tested independently?
8. **Authorisation:** What consultation, consent, statutory approval, contract or market disclosure is necessary? Which decisions are reserved to whom?
9. **Execution:** Who is accountable; which milestones test strategic assumptions; what expenditures can be stopped; and what triggers review?
10. **Culture and crisis:** What incentives could undermine the safeguards? Would bad news reach the board promptly and could the entity respond to a severe failure?
11. **Decision:** Approve, approve conditionally, stage, defer, redesign or reject, with explicit reasons and minutes.
12. **Review:** When and by whom will the board receive fresh evidence of value, risk, rights compliance and control effectiveness?

### 03.B.3 Verification tests and interpretive boundaries

1. **Coverage:** Eighteen canonical major sections, each independently searchable; each assigned blueprint row C18 and C21–C30 has a substantive section and source location; worked cases clearly identify their evidence category.
2. **Quantification:** All made-up cash figures are labelled IAE or hypothetical; ROIC–capital-charge, funding-buffer, probability/loss, financial-movement and charity runway arithmetic is traceable. Ratios, ranges, times and aggregation must use a consistent time horizon, unit and entity boundary.
3. **Legal scope:** Ordinary companies, incorporated associations, registered charities, APRA-regulated entities, CATSI corporations and RNTBCs are not treated as legally identical. Statutory and rights-based non-negotiables override an economic scorecard.
4. **Source rights:** Licensed ISO/COSO/AICD diagrams and template checklists are not reproduced. AICD instruction is attributed separately from independent analytical methods and current regulator material.
5. **Knowledge-file integrity:** Cross-file links refer to **planned corpus filenames**, not a claim that the other eight files exist or have been verified in this conversation. The present file is independently readable.

## 03.C Unresolved issues and maintenance

1. **BMA numerical contradiction.** The fictional case's RISK p.178 narrative implies a loss in 2022/23; the p.173 table reports positive net profit after tax of A$5.23m for that year and a negative result the next year. This file does **not** reconcile the inconsistency by assumption. Resolve against the course publisher before using it as an assessed historical fact.
2. **CQUniversity currency.** The cited risk-appetite excerpt is dated **5 July 2023** in RAPP pp.14–17. Its latest university-approved text, tolerance metrics, approval date and any subsequent changes have not been independently established; use only as a dated public example.
3. **CBA causation.** APRA findings, dated capital-remediation milestones and the bank-commissioned 2026 independent sustainability review are source-based within their respective scopes. Statements linking individual governance reforms to later shareholder value or claiming complete cultural transformation would require a separate causal study; none is asserted.
4. **Live legal developments.** Verify the final status of ASX's draft fifth-edition principles following the 2026 consultation; changes to the Privacy Act, Cyber Security Act rules and regulatory guidance; prudential changes; and Native Title PBC regulation amendments before future legal application. **No draft is treated as operative law.**
5. **Specific entity law.** State incorporated-association legislation and reporting thresholds vary by Australian jurisdiction. CATSI and RNTBC governance depends on the actual rule book, membership and determination, applicable ILUAs/agreements and identity of affected holders. No generic director memo can substitute for the operative instrument, local authority and legal/cultural advice.
6. **Foresight and model uncertainty.** Major strategic probabilities, correlations, tail losses, long-term competitive advantages and intangible social benefits are not independently estimable from the AICD examples. Use qualitative severe-but-plausible scenarios where probabilities lack defensible foundations, with quantified capacity tests where cash-flow information permits.
7. **Unseen evidence.** The separately delivered Director Mindset eLearning, NRGworks materials and complete assessment case packets mentioned in the blueprint are not part of the identified five core sources. They are not reconstructed. The original BMA and CBA course cases remain the governing scenario evidence.
8. **Future corpus integration.** Proposed links to Files 00–02 and 04–08 require validation once those files are produced and loaded together. Their titles come from the blueprint, not inspection of completed files. Their future content must not be inferred from a title alone.
