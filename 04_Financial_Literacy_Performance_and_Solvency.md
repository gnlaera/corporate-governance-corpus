## Reader's navigation and central argument

**Financial literacy for directors is the ability to test a financial story, not merely to read ratios.** A competent review asks six distinct questions: (1) Was value or mission outcome produced? (2) Was it recognised properly in the accounts? (3) Did it turn into cash? (4) Can obligations be met when due? (5) What resources, financing and risks are required next? (6) What must the board decide, request, challenge, disclose or stop? An accounting profit, a favourable ratio, a large asset base and a reassuring audit opinion answer different questions; none substitutes for the others.

The four statements form one system: **profit or loss** measures recognised performance over a period; **financial position** identifies recognised assets, obligations and residual equity at a date; **cash flows** track cash movements over the period; **changes in equity** reconcile movements in residual claims. Notes explain assumptions and hidden granularity. Management accounts and forecasts bring those records forward into actual decisions. All comparisons require an exact reporting period, consistent consolidation boundary, numerator and denominator, classification, currency and treatment of exceptional items.

This file follows the AICD's source methods while critically distinguishing pedagogical screening rules from legal tests. In particular, a current ratio below 1.5 is **not** proof of insolvency, a ratio above 1.5 is **not** proof of solvency, and EBITDA is neither operating cash flow nor the cash left after maintaining assets and paying financiers. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 11–22, 50–100; IFS pp. 5–38; METHOD pp. 1–4; TOOL pp. 53–60, 108, 124. Independent analysis: Sections 04.10, 04.14.]

## [04.01] What financial information directors receive and why it matters

### [04.01.01] Distinguish information for accountability from information for decisions

The same organisation needs several distinct reporting systems. A **statutory financial report** communicates the result and financial position for an identified historical period on a prescribed accounting basis to members, regulators, lenders and other users. **Management accounts** are internally designed, normally more frequent, and may distinguish stores, projects, contracts, entities, service programmes, regions and costs. A **budget** is the approved resource allocation and expected outcome against which management is held accountable. A **rolling forecast** updates the probable outcome and financing need using newer evidence; a **scenario** makes alternative assumptions explicit. **Treasury and covenant forecasts** focus on contractual cash and financial tests, sometimes on a different definition and perimeter from statutory statements. **Audit reports** assess specified subject matter to a defined level of assurance; they are not insurance against business failure.

| Information | Usual horizon and use | Strength | Material blind spot | Director's minimum request |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly management profit and loss, balance sheet and cash flow | Historical month, year to date and business-unit detail | Timely diagnosis and decisions | Unaudited estimates; inconsistent management adjustments | Actual, budget, prior year, revised forecast, definitions and exceptions |
| Statutory annual and half-year reporting | Historical, usually consolidated | Consistent rules, notes, external accountability | Timeliness; estimates; not a forecast | Complete statements and notes, sign-off papers, audit/review findings |
| Annual budget and capital plan | Next 12 months, sometimes multiyear | Allocates scarce resources, sets accountability | Can become an optimistic negotiating target | Driver-level assumptions and bridges to previous forecast |
| Latest estimate / rolling forecast | Next 12–24 months, updated monthly or quarterly | Best current outlook | Anchoring, optimism, absent balance sheet | Three linked statements, variances and forecast accuracy history |
| Liquidity and covenant forecast | Daily, weekly, monthly and to refinancing dates | Tests ability to settle obligations | Can assume uncommitted money arrives | Sources and uses, maturity dates, committed capacity, minimum headroom |
| Capital investment case | Project life, including exit/decommissioning | Exposes opportunity cost and risk-adjusted return | Valuation assumptions and terminal estimates | Unlevered cash flows, real/nominal consistency, capital cost, downside case |
| Internal audit, external audit and management letters | Control or annual reporting scope | Independent checks and specific assurance | Materiality, sampling, omitted scope | Findings, management responses, overdue actions, assurance coverage |
| Grant/contract/acquittal reports | Funding term and performance obligations | Tracks restricted usage and deliverables | May not reflect entity-wide solvency | Restricted funds ledger, receivables, clawback and timing risk |

The AICD distinguishes management information from published statements by audience, purpose, time orientation, detail, timeliness and regulation. Directors should not interpret its teaching distinction between management estimates and statutory precision as claiming that statutory accounts contain no estimates: impairment, provisions and credit losses require substantial judgment even under fully compliant accounting standards. [AICD teaching: IFS pp. 8–11; FIN pp. 11–21; FSH slides on internal reporting, PDF pp. 5–7. Independent clarification: FIN pp. 50–79; AASB standards in 04.08.]

### [04.01.02] Design the board's information rights and escalation triggers

Every monthly pack should contain: a one-page decision summary; a complete three-statement actual-versus-budget view; year-to-date and trailing-twelve-month results; 12–18-month rolling forecast; a 13-week receipts-and-payments forecast where liquidity is consequential; gross and net debt with facility availability; trade and grant receivable ageing; capital commitments; covenant headroom; key accounting judgments; and a list of exceptions, control failures and decisions required. Segment and legal-entity appendices should be available for drill-down rather than buried in the narrative. The board should agree its threshold for material differences and require explanations of **price, volume, mix, cost, acquisition, currency, timing and nonrecurring** drivers, not just unexplained percentage variances.

Financial packs must balance backward reporting with forward signals. A board may see record earnings while order intake falls, stock ages, an investment requires a capital call, a funding agreement approaches expiry or a balloon debt repayment becomes current. Leading indicators should be chosen for the economics of the organisation, not simply what accounting software can output. Management produces the data and explains it; the board tests the reliability of information, authorises risk and capital choices, and follows up exceptions. An audit committee can examine technical accounting but cannot absorb the full board's need to understand the entity. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 14–22; FSH pp. 4–7, 24; TOOL pp. 3–11, 108.]

### [04.01.03] Read assurance reports without confusing their objects

An **external audit** ordinarily provides reasonable, not absolute, assurance about a defined historical financial report under applicable standards. A **review** gives limited assurance and is not equivalent to a financial statement audit. An **internal audit** assesses selected governance, controls and risk processes. A **management representation letter** is an assertion by management, not independent evidence. A **clean audit opinion** does not guarantee forecast profitability, absence of fraud, asset market value or the ability to repay a debt falling due in nine months. A material uncertainty related to going concern, an emphasis of matter, a modified opinion and a key audit matter have different meanings. The classification, reporting and audit obligations themselves are developed in File 05. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 23–44; IFS pp. 5–7; FSH PDF pp. 6–7.]

**Board decision test:** What important decision would change if this report were materially wrong? Who independently checks its inputs, and what is the earliest adverse indicator that will reach directors?

## [04.02] Accounting foundations: one economic event, different reporting consequences

### [04.02.01] Accounting equation and stocks versus flows

At every reporting date, **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**, so **Equity = Assets − Liabilities**. A purchase funded by a loan typically raises both assets and liabilities, not equity. Repayment reduces cash and debt, not necessarily profit. A provision expense normally reduces profit and equity and increases liabilities, yet may involve no cash payment in the period. An issue of shares increases cash and equity but does not create operating profit. Gains in other comprehensive income can increase equity without affecting current net profit after tax (NPAT).

The balance sheet is a **stock at a date**; income, cash flow and equity changes are **flows over an interval**. Comparing a year's profit with a year-end asset stock is a common but imperfect return measure; a defensible return usually uses average capital employed during the earning period. A cash balance at 30 June cannot be added to an annual cash inflow without accounting for the opening balance. A return on capital computed using post-acquisition closing assets but earnings from only two months of the acquisition is mechanically depressed, not necessarily economically poor. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 50–54, 74–76; IFS pp. 9–11, 18–23, 35–38; GLOSS pp. 1–6.]

### [04.02.02] Accruals, matching, cash and revenue obligations

**Accrual accounting** recognises revenue when accounting recognition requirements are met and expenses when incurred or when the related economic benefit is consumed, not solely on receipt or payment of cash. The recognition test depends on the nature of the arrangement: customer performance obligations under AASB 15, grants and contributions under applicable AASB 1058/AASB 15 requirements, financial instruments under AASB 9, and other sector-specific standards. Customer receipts in advance generally create deferred revenue or contract liabilities until the relevant obligations are fulfilled. Paying for inventory normally creates an asset first; the cost becomes cost of goods sold when the related inventory is sold. Buying machinery creates an asset and investing cash outflow, followed by depreciation over its useful life. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 50–75; IFS pp. 12–38; FAPP pp. 1–6. Verified standards: AASB 15, AASB 1058, AASB 102 and AASB 116, applicability subject to period and entity.]

**Illustration, independent:** A retailer takes a $10,000 customer deposit in June on furniture delivered in August. In June, cash rises $10,000 and a customer obligation rises $10,000; no ordinary product sale is yet recognised if delivery is the satisfaction event. In August, revenue is recognised and the liability released, even if no additional cash arrives. A liquidity ratio may improve in June, but the cash must finance inventory and fulfilment. Similar timing issues affect government grants, sponsorships, trust money and prepaid subscriptions, although the relevant legal and accounting treatment is arrangement-specific.

**Trade credit illustration:** A $20,000 invoice for June services received in July is usually recognised as a June expense and an accrual or payable, with payment in July. A director reviewing a June cash balance without the accrual overstates available cash. Conversely, a $100,000 invoice to a customer in June can boost revenue while cash is not received until September. A financial report can be technically correct but economically misleading if a director assumes revenue means cash collected.

### [04.02.03] Four statements and notes, with exact time descriptions

| Statement | Typical heading | Questions answered | Does not answer alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profit or loss and other comprehensive income | For the year ended 30 June 2024 | What income and expenses were recognised; what is NPAT or surplus? | When customers paid; whether assets can be realised at book value |
| Statement of financial position (balance sheet) | As at 30 June 2024 | What assets, liabilities and equity were recognised at that date? | Exact future payment calendar; enterprise market value |
| Cash-flow statement | For the year ended 30 June 2024 | Where cash came from and went: operating, investing, financing | All noncash obligations or future cash commitments |
| Changes in equity | For the year ended 30 June 2024 | How profit, OCI, capital transactions and distributions changed equity | Whether restricted funds can be spent on general operations |
| Notes (integral to financial statements) | For relevant reporting period | Policies, estimates, contingencies, maturities, instruments, segments, relationships | Every management risk not required by accounting disclosure rules |

The AICD sometimes calls three statements the "key" statements because it focuses its review methods on profit, position and cash; IFS correctly identifies **four** primary financial statements. Statement of changes in equity is not optional conceptual background. The components required by the reporting framework and entity type are addressed in File 05. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 11–12, 50–76; IFS pp. 9–10, 35–38.]

### [04.02.04] Reporting period, currency, measurement and comparability discipline

The annual period may end on 30 June, 31 December or another authorised date. A seven-month management result is not a full financial year. Financial statements may be in dollars, thousands or millions; a line marked `$'000 = 80,612` means $80.612 million. The functional and presentation currencies may differ. Foreign-currency movements, an extra reporting week, acquisitions partway through a year, discontinued operations, accounting policy changes and restatements can invalidate a superficially reasonable percentage change. GST-inclusive cash receipts may differ from GST-exclusive reported revenue. A director should demand a bridge whenever reporting scope or definitions change.

A **general-purpose** report, a permitted **special-purpose** report, Tier 1 and Tier 2 disclosures, small-charity cash reporting and a lender-specific covenant schedule may apply different requirements and need not be interchangeable. **[Verified as at 27 October 2026]** ACNC guidance says medium and large charities use accrual accounting in their required financial reports; small charities may use cash or accrual where their rules and other requirements allow. AASB 18, which changes presentation and disclosures, generally first applies to for-profit Tier 1 general-purpose reporting periods beginning on or after **1 January 2027**, with an adapted commencement for NFP and relevant superannuation entities beginning **1 January 2028**. It should not be retrofitted to the 2024 worked examples. [ACNC, *Cash and accrual accounting*, https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/obligations-acnc/reporting-annually-acnc/cash-and-accrual; AASB, *New Standard AASB 18 issued*, 14 June 2024, https://www.aasb.gov.au/news/new-standard-aasb-18-issued/; AASB standards register, accessed 27 Oct 2026.]

## [04.03] The balance sheet: claims, financing and the quality of assets

### [04.03.01] Classify current and non-current by economic and contractual reality

**Current assets** typically include cash, receivables, inventory, prepayments and short-term investments expected to be realised, sold or consumed in the normal operating cycle or within the applicable classification criteria. **Non-current assets** include long-lived property, plant, equipment, right-of-use assets, intangibles, goodwill and some deferred tax items. **Current liabilities** include trade payables, accruals, wages and employee entitlements, taxes, short-term financing, amounts due within twelve months, some contract liabilities and obligations for which the entity lacks the requisite right to defer settlement. **Non-current liabilities** include longer-term debt, leases and provisions meeting the standard's classification rules. Ordinary operating-cycle assets and liabilities may be current even when settlement extends beyond twelve months. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 51–64; IFS pp. 18–26; FAPP pp. 1–4; GLOSS pp. 3–6. Verified standard: AASB 101 paras. 66–76, and AASB 18 according to applicable commencement.]

Debt classification is especially consequential. A long-term loan that becomes repayable on demand because of an unwaived reporting-date covenant breach may need to be shown as current, even if management expects support or obtains a waiver only after year-end. Conversely, forecast refinancing does not by itself grant a right to defer. A current-classified term loan can make a ratio look weak without proving the company will default; it still creates a financing and legal risk that directors must resolve with dated evidence. [AASB 101 paras. 69, 74–75; official AASB standard browser, https://standards.aasb.gov.au/; FIN pp. 55–63.]

### [04.03.02] Asset-quality tests by account

| Account | What the reported number means | Typical hidden risk | Evidence to request |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash and deposits | Recorded balances held by or controlled for the entity | Restricted, pledged, trapped overseas, held for third parties; reconciliations | Bank confirmations, restrictions and account ownership, treasury reconciliation |
| Trade receivables | Customer and contract amounts due, net of allowances | Collection delay, concentration, dispute, nonrecoverability | Aged debtors, post-close receipts, write-offs, top-ten concentrations, expected credit loss model |
| Inventory | Recognised cost subject to accounting valuation rules | Obsolescence, shrinkage, slow sales, margins below carrying cost | Ageing, stocktakes, forecast sell-through, write-downs, returns, net realisable value |
| Prepayments | Cash spent for future service | Cannot readily be monetised to pay creditors | Schedule by supplier and cancellation rights |
| Property/plant/equipment | Carried cost/revaluation less depreciation and impairment | Underutilisation, technology change, remediation and maintenance liabilities | Asset register, physical verification, capex backlog, recoverable amount tests |
| Right-of-use assets | Rights under recognised leases | Lease payments still required; store closures may impair rights | Lease-by-lease cash schedule, options, sublease assumptions |
| Goodwill/intangibles | Acquired expectations and identifiable intangible benefits | Cash-generation assumptions, synergies and indefinite-life impairment | Cash-generating-unit (CGU) model, headroom and discount-rate sensitivity |
| Deferred tax assets | Expected benefit of future deductible differences or losses | Insufficient future taxable profit | Reversal schedule, taxable profit evidence, tax advice |
| Short/long debt | Contractual financial obligations and classifications | Covenants, guarantees, accelerations, uncommitted refinancings | Facility documents, amortisation, security, covenants, maturity ladder |
| Trade payables and provisions | Obligations including uncertain amounts | Unrecorded invoices, tax and employee obligations, litigation | Creditor ageing, subsequent payments, legal letters, provision roll-forward |

The book value of a building does not equal its sale proceeds after taxes and costs; goodwill cannot be sold separately from a viable business; a related-party receivable may be uncollectible; a prepaid expense is not funding for wages. Directors must test the **cash realisation and lawful availability** of assets, not stop at their accounting classification. The AICD methodology's instruction to treat every major balance-sheet line as a risk is useful as a review prompt, not a claim that growing assets or equity is necessarily healthy. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 52–64; IFS pp. 18–26; METHOD pp. 1–2; HEALTH pp. 1–3; TOOL pp. 53–58.]

### [04.03.03] Working capital and business models

**Net working capital (NWC) = current assets − current liabilities.** A positive NWC position is a general buffer, but accounting NWC includes inventory that must be sold, prepayments that cannot pay bills and liabilities that may not require immediate cash settlement. For commercial operating analysis, **operating NWC** is typically trade receivables + inventory + other operating current assets − trade payables − other operating current liabilities, excluding cash, interest-bearing debt and, where appropriate, taxes. Define treatment of customer deposits and contract liabilities explicitly. A business can succeed with negative NWC if cash is collected from customers before suppliers are paid; it can also fail with positive NWC if stock is obsolete and payables mature today.

| Business model | Likely working-capital dynamics | Board question |
|---|---|---|
| Cash retail with customer deposits | Cash arrives before product delivery; deferred revenue creates obligations | How much cash must remain to deliver existing orders? |
| Manufacturing and distribution | Stock, work in progress and receivables consume cash | Are unit margins sufficient after carrying costs and spoilage? |
| Engineering/project contracting | Milestones, retentions, claims and contract assets dominate | Are recognised margins backed by collectible cash claims? |
| Subscription/software | Prepayments fund activity but create future service costs | What portion of deferred revenue is economically encumbered? |
| Charity with advance grants | Grants may improve bank balance while future service obligations increase | What cash is uncommitted and legally spendable? |
| RNTBC with third-party funds | Corporate operating money and monies held for holders/trusts may differ | Whose funds are these; who has legal authority over them? |

A model can release cash for a few quarters by stretching payables or consuming inventories. Those releases are finite and may conceal declining supplier confidence or underinvestment. Measure cash conversion across a full cycle and compare aged balances with operational throughput. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 56–64, 70–74; IFS pp. 18–38. Independent analysis: working-capital adjustments and operating-cycle interpretation.]

### [04.03.04] Debt, leases, contingencies, goodwill and residual equity

**Gross financial debt** normally includes drawn bank loans, bonds and other interest-bearing financing, but whether lease liabilities, supplier finance and restricted project debt enter a ratio depends on the stated definition. **Net financial debt = gross financial debt − eligible cash/cash equivalents**; eligible cash is not automatically all money in a bank. Disclose cash that is pledged, inaccessible, legally restricted or held for someone else. An apparent net-cash position can coexist with substantial lease obligations, undrawn commitments, contingent guarantees, pension obligations or construction costs.

AASB 16 generally requires lessees to recognise right-of-use assets and corresponding lease liabilities, subject to recognised exemptions, including elective accounting for some short-term and low-value leases. This changes apparent leverage, EBITDA, depreciation, interest and cash flow classification. A pre- and post-lease comparison should not be mixed without adjustment. For a retailer with long premises leases, ignoring lease cash payments exaggerates discretionary cash. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 55–63, 109–112; HEALTH p. 3; NICK 2024 annual report pp. 23, 25, note 14. Verified standard: AASB 16 paras. 5–8 and other relevant provisions.]

**Equity** is a residual *accounting* claim: net assets at book values, not the cash owners could withdraw, the company's enterprise value or its liquidation recovery. A positive equity balance may disappear after asset impairments or become unusable due to borrowing restrictions. Negative equity increases distress concern but is not itself Australia's definition of insolvency. An entity can be profitable, net cash-positive on paper and simultaneously unable to pay one particular debt on its due date; conversely, a growth company can have little retained profit but ample irrevocable committed finance.

**Questions before approving a major investment:** What is the funding source at each settlement date? What assets are genuinely liquid? What debt accelerates if a covenant breaches? Is the economic return measured on the same capital base and with the same lease treatment? What contingent liabilities or recourse arrangements are omitted from simplistic net-debt calculations?

## [04.04] Profit, performance and the limitations of accounting earnings

### [04.04.01] Build the income statement from economic drivers

Revenue is not the same as cash receipts, sales orders or contracted backlog. A retailer may recognise revenue on delivery; a contractor may recognise revenue progressively as qualifying performance occurs; an NFP may recognise funding differently depending on enforceable obligations. Ask for the revenue recognition memo where a shift in policy, business model or contract terms could accelerate reported performance. A sale to a related party or an uncollectible customer may be recorded as revenue but should be challenged on substance, recoverability and the applicable rules. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 64–70; IFS pp. 12–17; NICK 2024 annual report pp. 6–7, note 3.]

A useful reporting bridge is **volume × realised unit price**, adjusted for sales mix, returns/discounts, foreign exchange and acquisitions. **Gross profit = revenue from goods sold − cost of goods sold**. **Operating profit** reflects operating income and costs, but account classification can vary. **EBIT**, earnings before interest and tax, usually equals profit before tax + finance costs − finance income *if finance income is excluded from EBIT by the chosen definition*. Some AICD examples calculate EBIT as profit before tax plus disclosed finance costs without subtracting finance income classified within other income; both may be useful, but label them accurately. **EBITDA = EBIT + depreciation + amortisation** under the same EBIT definition. **NPAT = profit before tax − income-tax expense** (with relevant ownership attribution and discontinued operations disclosed). A loss on sale, revaluation or impairment may affect a reported operating subtotal differently from the economics of current production.

### [04.04.02] Why EBITDA cannot fund itself

EBITDA is before depreciation/amortisation, interest and tax and normally before working-capital changes, capital expenditure and principal/lease repayments. It ignores that equipment, software, working capital and a maintained network must be continually replaced. Its usefulness is as an intermediate measure of trading earnings in some business models, not as a universal cash valuation or solvency metric. Under AASB 16, lease expense is often replaced by depreciation and interest, increasing EBITDA even though lease cash payments continue. A financially distressed retailer can show positive store-level EBITDA but negative corporate cash after head office, refurbishment, rent/lease payments, financing and tax.

**Independent numeric illustration:** A company reports EBITDA 100, depreciation 25, interest 20 and tax 12; EBIT is 75 and NPAT 43. It spends 35 on maintenance capex, another 20 on growth capex, and 15 on receivables/inventory. If cash tax paid is 12 and cash interest 20, an approximate unlevered-to-levered cash bridge is EBITDA 100 − operating working-capital investment 15 − cash taxes 12 − cash interest 20 − capex 55 = **−2** before financing principal or dividends. EBITDA 100, profit 43 and distributable cash −2 are not contradictory. Actual cash flow must reconcile additional items such as provisions, payables and the classification of cash interest. A capital-intensive operation may exhibit strong EBITDA and destroy economic value if maintenance and replacement capex exceed economic depreciation.

### [04.04.03] Gross margin, fixed-cost leverage and quality of earnings

Gross margin can move because selling prices, freight, procurement, input costs, product mix, stock write-downs, contract profitability or accounting allocation change. If sales decline while store rents, employee costs and central overheads are inflexible, EBIT and NPAT may fall faster than revenue even as gross margin improves. Examine **contribution margin** (sales less truly variable costs) when assessing break-even, but do not confuse it with statutory gross margin. Separate like-for-like growth from acquisition effects and compare margins within genuinely comparable sectors and lease accounting policies.

A company may show increasing NPAT because of a tax credit, reversal of provisions, gain on disposal, accounting estimate change or interest income on funds from a capital raise rather than operating improvement. **Other comprehensive income (OCI)** includes specified recognised gains and losses outside profit or loss, such as some hedging and revaluation changes; it flows to equity and can affect capital without changing NPAT. Total comprehensive income is NPAT plus OCI, subject to attribution. A substantial OCI loss can expose currency or hedging risk while reported profit rises. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 64–70, 74; IFS pp. 12–17; NICK 2024 annual report p. 22; DISC 2024 pp. 2, 4.]

### [04.04.04] 'Underlying', 'adjusted', 'normalised' and nonrecurring earnings

Management-defined performance measures can be informative when an item is economically unusual. They can also be used to sanitise recurring restructuring costs, acquisition integration expenses, stock write-downs, litigation, maintenance or repeated asset impairments. Request a complete **reported-to-adjusted bridge**, with item, gross amount, tax effect, cash impact, historical recurrence and whether similar gains were excluded. Confirm the calculation of adjusted earnings per share uses the same share count and appropriate tax assumptions. Treat an adjustment as *analytical*, not equivalent to Australian Accounting Standards profit.

A cost labelled 'one-off' can recur every year across different projects. A legal settlement may be nonrecurring, but the conduct that produced it could signal persistent risk. Goodwill impairments may be noncash in the current period yet reveal an earlier capital-allocation failure. Depreciation is noncash today yet often proxies future replacement requirements. For profitability decisions, use at least three lenses: **reported accounting earnings**, **sustainable after-tax cash generation**, and **return on incremental capital versus its cost**. For charities, assess whether reported surpluses are unrestricted and whether the programme actually creates the intended public benefit.

**Director questions:** What changed economically rather than merely in presentation? Which costs were deferred or capitalised? Do earnings reconcile to cash and debt movements? Which seemingly exceptional charges indicate repeatable organisational weaknesses?

## [04.05] Cash flow: funding the business after the accounting result

### [04.05.01] Read three categories separately, and then together

**Operating cash flow (OCF or CFO)** is cash generated or consumed by operating activities under the entity's reporting policy. **Investing cash flow (CFI)** usually includes purchases and disposals of long-lived assets, businesses and financial investments; a purchase of a short-term deposit may be classified within investing if it does not meet the definition of a cash equivalent. **Financing cash flow (CFF)** reports financing inflows and outflows including borrowings, equity, dividends and lease principal payments. Cash balance reconciliation is **closing cash and cash equivalents = opening cash and cash equivalents + CFO + CFI + CFF + separately presented foreign-exchange movement (where applicable)**. A reported cash-flow reconciliation may have additional exchange components; do not invent balancing cash flows.

Cash-flow classification needs careful checking. Under AASB 107, permitted presentation policies for interest/dividends can affect which subtotal contains them for the reporting period; management should explain the policy and apply it consistently. AASB 18 makes consequential changes to cash-flow presentation when it becomes applicable. For a group reporting cash interest on debt and leases in financing, CFO will be higher than for an otherwise comparable group with cash interest within operating activities. Where the director wants an economic after-interest measure, deduct cash interest **once**, whatever its accounting category. A borrower paying principal is using cash even though principal is not an income-statement expense. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 70–74; IFS pp. 27–38; METHOD pp. 3–4; FAPP pp. 5–6; NICK 2024 annual report p. 25. Verified standard: AASB 107, Australian standard effective for the relevant period, https://standards.aasb.gov.au/.]

| Cash flow | Positive does not necessarily mean | Negative does not necessarily mean | Cross-check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating | Operating economics are sustainable: may be customer advances or creditor stretch | Failing business: may be deliberate inventory build for contracted growth | Profit-to-cash bridge, debtors, customer advances, grant liabilities |
| Investing | Stronger operations: may be disposal of essential assets or term-deposit maturities | Loss-making activity: may be high-return maintenance or expansion capex | Project pipeline, capex type, replacement requirements |
| Financing | Higher value: cash may be new borrowing needed to fund losses | Weakness: debt or equity can be repaid from sustainable cash | Debt maturity, issue dilution, refinancing, finance cost |
| Net cash change | Free discretionary cash increased: may reflect capital raise | Economic loss: may reflect distributions or transfer to term deposits | Cash equivalents versus deposits, restrictions, full uses of cash |

A financing cash inflow of $50 million does not repair a loss-making business model. A profitable business selling an asset for $50 million may have more cash but less future earnings capacity. Conversely, cash invested in a productive project reduces near-term cash but can raise future value if expected incremental return exceeds risk-adjusted capital cost. Accountancy tells us **where the cash went**, not whether the allocation was economically good.

### [04.05.02] Cash versus profit: derive the indirect reconciliation

An indirect profit-to-cash bridge begins with NPAT, adjusts for noncash charges and gains, reverses items classified in other cash-flow sections, incorporates taxes/interest according to presentation, and recognises changes in receivables, inventories, payables, other operating assets and provisions. Sign conventions must be audited; an increase in receivables is generally a cash use, while an increase in payables is generally a source of cash, all else equal. With acquisitions or currency translation, the difference between balance-sheet openings and closings does **not** equal operating cash movement, because the balance sheets may include acquired receivables and liabilities or foreign-exchange translation.

**Independent example, all figures $m:** NPAT 40 + depreciation 18 + noncash impairment 8 − profit on asset sale 3 − increase in trade receivables 12 − increase in inventory 10 + increase in trade payables 7 + increase in accrued expenses 2 = **CFO 50** before any additional adjustments for interest/tax policies. Higher earnings than CFO could indicate uncollected sales; higher CFO than earnings could reflect capital consumption, noncash costs or deferred customer receipts. Request a waterfall split into recurring, one-off and cash-timing items, including the cash cost expected in future periods from provisions booked today.

The director should obtain a **rolling twelve-month profit-to-cash reconciliation**, not merely a single period. The question is whether earnings convert to cash **over the commercial cycle**. Excessively positive cash conversion in one period can come from nonrepeatable working-capital release. A consistently high conversion ratio can still coexist with inadequate capex, poor capital returns or imminent refinancing.

### [04.05.03] Free cash flow is a definition, not a statutory line

The most common starting measure is **free cash flow before financing = CFO − cash capital expenditure (capex)**. Define whether capex includes purchased intangibles, capitalised software, leases or acquisitions. A useful operating sustainability version is **cash after recurring operating costs, cash tax and interest, maintenance capex, and lease principal**; whether cash interest is already inside CFO must be documented. To measure *cash available for equity distributions*, also deduct contractual debt principal payable, required reserves and finance obligations, while considering working-capital, capital-call and regulatory restrictions. New borrowing and equity issue proceeds are sources of finance, not operating free cash flow.

| Measure | Formula convention | What it tests | Major caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic free cash flow (FCF) | CFO − cash purchases of PPE − purchased intangible capex | Capacity to fund capital assets from operations | Does not automatically deduct principal, lease payments or interest in financing |
| Post-lease FCF | CFO − cash capex − lease-principal payments − lease interest not in CFO | Cash economics after recurring lease financing | Some interest may already be in CFO; no double subtraction |
| Pre-distribution cash | Post-lease FCF − cash borrowing interest outside CFO − required debt principal | Available before owner transfers, subject to restrictions | Investment/acquisition spending, covenant requirements and cash traps |
| Self-generation test (AICD) | Operating cash flow − PPE purchases − relevant lease payments − dividends | Whether operations self-fund these categories | A teaching score, not an enterprise valuation or solvency test; add intangibles/debt service when material |
| Cash reinvestment ratio | (Cash maintenance + growth capex) / CFO, if CFO > 0 | Reinvestment intensity | Negative CFO makes the ratio difficult to interpret |
| EBITDA to FCF conversion | FCF / EBITDA, with denominator and lease policy explicit | Translation of earnings proxy into cash after capex | Can be distorted by tax and capex cycles |

Separate **maintenance capex** (preserves existing productive and service capacity, including necessary safety obligations) from **growth capex** (expands it). Managers may misclassify refurbishment as growth to present an attractive maintenance burden. For mines, energy assets and infrastructure, future abandonment, rehabilitation and substantial replacement campaigns belong in economic analysis even if they are not current cash capex. For charities, programme assets and volunteer infrastructure may be necessary to sustain mission outcomes even though depreciation or EBITDA is not the main performance objective. [AICD teaching: METHOD p. 4; HEALTH pp. 4–5; FIN pp. 70–74, 111–112. Independent extensions: lease-adjusted FCF, maintenance distinction and pre-distribution cash.]

### [04.05.04] Debt service, dividends and cash availability

**Debt service** comprises contractual interest, fees and principal by date. A company can cover interest comfortably yet fail because a $100 million bullet repayment occurs next month and refinancing is uncommitted. Dividends may be declared in one period and paid in another; compare cash paid with the relevant cash flow and earnings period. A sale of productive assets, an emergency capital raise or a delay in supplier payments can fund dividends temporarily without demonstrating sustainability.

A useful short-cycle board schedule is **opening eligible cash + committed facility drawdowns + conservative collections − scheduled supplier/wage/tax/interest/lease/debt/capex/grant payments = forecast closing eligible cash** for each week. Include cash legally restricted for other parties only in separately restricted schedules, not as if available to general creditors. A board should also see worst-case timing when an invoice is delayed but payroll and tax are not. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 70–74, 90–100; TOOL p. 124. Independent application: liquidity waterfall.]

**Director questions:** Is operating cash positive after removing advances and delayed payments? Are maintenance, interest and leases fully counted? What capex can be deferred without creating greater future loss, legal breaches or safety risk? Which cash distributions are reversible, and which debts are contractually due?

## [04.06] Changes in equity, reserves and distributions

### [04.06.01] Read the full equity bridge, not only closing net assets

For a typical shareholder-owned company, the reconciliation is **opening equity + period profit attributable to owners + OCI attributable to owners + new equity contributions − dividends/buybacks + other recognised equity movements = closing equity**, with non-controlling interests presented separately when present. Individual columns can include contributed capital, retained profits, cash-flow hedging reserves, revaluation reserves, foreign currency translation, share-based payment reserves and tax recognised directly in equity. Profits or losses do not necessarily explain all movements. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 74–76; IFS pp. 9–10, 35–38; DISC 2024 p. 4; NICK 2024 annual report p. 24.]

**Independent illustration:** Opening equity $100m + NPAT $12m − negative hedging OCI $5m + share issue $20m − dividends $8m + share-based reserve credit $1m = **closing equity $120m**. If a director simply takes opening equity plus NPAT, the calculated $112m is wrong. OCI is not inherently available for distribution. A revaluation reserve can raise equity without adding a dollar of cash. Conversely, a dividend can reduce equity without being an expense in calculating NPAT.

### [04.06.02] Retained earnings are not a cash account

Retained earnings are accumulated recognised profit less distributions and applicable transfers, not cash in a bank. They can be invested in inventory, property, receivables or subsidiaries, or offset by large liabilities. A business may report $200 million in retained earnings but hold only $2 million in cash. The ability to distribute funds depends on the law and constitution, financing covenants, creditor interests, actual liquidity and governance purpose. In a charity or incorporated association, accumulated surplus generally cannot be paid to members as commercial dividends; however, funding can be applied to legitimate purposes and lawful expenses. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 51–63, 74–76; GLOSS pp. 5–6; FAPP pp. 3–4.]

For Australian companies, do not substitute the AICD 2016 heuristic of 'sufficient profits and cash' for the statutory dividend provisions. **[Verified law, 27 Oct 2026]** *Corporations Act 2001* s 254T requires, among other conditions, assets sufficiently exceeding liabilities immediately before declaration, fairness to shareholders as a whole, and no material prejudice to ability to pay creditors; the constitution, legal duties, reporting standards and distributions provisions must also be considered. A profitable company may not be able to pay a lawful or prudent dividend. A company with accumulated losses may require specialist advice rather than relying on a simple retained-profit rule. [*Corporations Act 2001* (Cth), s 254T, C149 19 Sep 2026, https://www.legislation.gov.au/Latest/C2017C00328; AICD FIFTY, 3rd ed., Oct 2016, p. 11, historical.]

### [04.06.03] Charities and RNTBCs: equity is not necessarily freely usable

**Accumulated funds** or **retained surplus** are accounting aggregates. Within them, restrictions may come from grant contracts, legislation, donor conditions, charitable trusts, native title agreements, corporation rules, fiduciary duties and resolutions. They differ from **board-designated reserves**, which are internally earmarked but may be redeployable by a properly authorised board decision, subject to external restrictions. An asset revaluation reserve is not operating cash; an externally restricted fund is not a discretionary rainy-day reserve merely because its accounting presentation is within equity. A charity's surplus should be used toward its purposes rather than distributed privately. [ACNC, *Charity reserves: financial stability and sustainability*, accessed 27 Oct 2026, https://www.acnc.gov.au/tools/guides/charity-reserves-financial-stability-and-sustainability; AICD FAPP pp. 7–14.]

**Board control:** Require an independently reconciled funds schedule with four columns: **legal owner/beneficiary**, **bank or investment account**, **recognised accounting balance**, and **permitted purpose/approver/withdrawal conditions**. For funds held on trust or for third parties, determine whether they should appear in the corporation's own statements at all; an explanatory memorandum cannot convert third-party money into general revenue. Details of authority and native title holder decision rights belong in File 07.

## [04.07] Consolidation, control and economic exposure

### [04.07.01] 'Group' reporting is not a simple sum of ownership percentages

A reporting parent generally consolidates entities it **controls** under AASB 10, not merely companies in which it owns a majority of shares. Control analysis considers power over the investee, exposure or rights to variable returns, and ability to use power to affect those returns. The illustrative AICD slide depicts a parent with controlled subsidiaries of varying shareholdings, but it should not be read as a mechanical rule that 51% always establishes control or 49% cannot. Contractual rights, substantive voting arrangements, structured entities and power over relevant activities may matter. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 76–78; FSH consolidation slide, PDF p. 9; verified standard: AASB 10 *Consolidated Financial Statements*.]

If parent P controls 75% of subsidiary S, the consolidated statements generally include **100%** of S's assets, liabilities, revenues, expenses and cash flows, eliminate intragroup balances/transactions, and attribute the residual 25% equity and relevant profit to **non-controlling interests**. They do **not** generally recognise 75% of each asset on a line-by-line basis. Parent-only accounts and a project management dashboard can look very different from the consolidated group. A joint venture or associate may be equity-accounted rather than line-by-line consolidated, while arrangements with joint control and direct rights to assets or obligations for liabilities may have a different treatment. [AASB 10, AASB 11 *Joint Arrangements*, AASB 128 *Investments in Associates and Joint Ventures*; FIN pp. 76–78.]

### [04.07.02] Reconcile intercompany eliminations and financial support

If the parent lends $20m to a subsidiary, the parent records a receivable and the subsidiary a payable. On consolidation, both are eliminated. The group's external borrowing and cash may remain, and the subsidiary can still be unable to pay its legal obligations if cash is located elsewhere. Likewise, profit recognised on sales within a group must be eliminated to the extent required by consolidation rules until realised externally. Uneliminated intercompany balances can overstate assets and revenue.

**Independent numerical example:** Parent external revenue $100m, subsidiary external revenue $40m and intragroup sales from parent to subsidiary $15m. Total management sales lines might be $155m. Consolidated external revenue is **$140m**, not $155m; the internal $15m is eliminated. If S is 75%-owned and controlled, consolidated revenue is still $140m, not $100m + 75% × $40m. Profit attribution is a separate issue. A board considering acquisition returns must reconcile enterprise-level cash generation, net debt assumed, minority distributions, parent guarantees and its legal rights to transfer funds.

### [04.07.03] Economic ownership, legal recourse and reportable cash

Consolidation does not make every subsidiary's cash available for the parent. Subsidiary directors have their own duties; lenders may have security and cash sweeps; local law can limit dividends; tax and foreign exchange can trap cash; RNTBCs may hold assets or money on behalf of specific common law holders or trust beneficiaries. Conversely, a parent may be exposed through guarantees or performance undertakings not obvious from a superficial ownership percentage. A minority investment can involve significant capital commitments or financial guarantee exposure without full consolidation.

| Lens | Typical result | Why boards need it |
|---|---|---|
| Statutory consolidation | Defines assets/liabilities and earnings for the reporting group | Financial reporting and overall performance |
| Legal-entity cash and solvency | Cash, creditors and debts for each legal person | Legal solvency and funding capacity, distributions and tax |
| Look-through economic ownership | Share of joint venture cash, capex, production, obligations | Investment return, valuation and capital at risk |
| Credit facility/guarantor group | Obligors, lenders' covenants and encumbered assets | Debt availability and acceleration risk |
| Restricted/trust-fund perimeter | Funds subject to external purpose and control limitations | Protect beneficiaries and avoid unlawful diversion |

**Director questions:** Who legally owes each material liability? Which subsidiaries can upstream cash, under what documents and after which obligations? Have elimination entries been checked? Are minority interests, unconsolidated commitments and guarantees reflected in the board's economic exposure?

## [04.08] Major accounting judgments: where management's estimates matter most

### [04.08.01] Impairment, recoverability and goodwill

**Impairment** occurs when an applicable asset's carrying amount exceeds the relevant recoverable amount or measurement threshold. Under AASB 136 for assets within its scope, recoverable amount is generally the higher of **value in use** and **fair value less costs of disposal**. Value in use involves discounted expected cash flows, assumptions about volume, price, cost, asset life, terminal values and a suitable discount rate. Some assets have different impairment rules, including inventory under AASB 102 and financial assets under AASB 9. Goodwill is allocated to the relevant CGU(s), subject to applicable requirements, and tested at least annually, rather than systematically amortised. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 54–64, 75–79; METHOD pp. 1–2; TOOL pp. 47–52; independently verified AASB 136, official compilation, https://standards.aasb.gov.au/aasb-136-jan-2026, especially paras. 1, 9–10, 59–66.]

When an acquisition fails to achieve planned synergies, the initial overpayment may be revealed years later in goodwill impairment. The impairment charge can be noncash *this year* but still represents a real historic loss of invested capital. Directors should avoid the opposite error: capitalising something does not itself create economic value. A large goodwill balance may have little resale value in a forced-sale scenario and can expose equity and covenants to sudden accounting shocks. However, book impairments and fair value changes do not on their own measure current enterprise value with precision.

**Sensitivity illustration, independent:** An asset's carrying value is $150m; management's estimated recoverable amount is $170m, giving $20m or 13.3% headroom relative to carrying value. A 15% reduction in recoverable amount produces $144.5m, implying a **$5.5m shortfall** and a possible impairment, subject to applicable standards and CGU allocation. The analysis needs the *absolute* and *percentage* headroom, the sensitivity parameters and whether multiple adverse drivers are correlated. A value-in-use model that assumes 4% terminal growth in a declining market deserves challenge even if the reported base-case headroom is positive.

### [04.08.02] Provisions, contingent liabilities and unrecorded commitments

A provision under AASB 137 generally arises for a present legal or constructive obligation from a past event, where an outflow is probable and a reliable estimate can be made. It is not a discretionary pot of money for any future challenge. **Contingent liabilities** may require disclosure rather than recognition under the applicable criteria. **Future operating losses** alone do not create a provision. A **restructuring provision** has specific thresholds and cannot merely reflect a board's intention to reduce costs. Employee entitlements, warranties, litigation, remediation and onerous contracts may involve uncertain cash timing and, where material, discounting.

Boards should ask for a probability-weighted or scenario-based settlement range where useful, the legal advice underpinning recognition and nonrecognition, and an obligations calendar. Check whether a litigation settlement or environmental rehabilitation estimate is expressed in today's dollars or nominal future costs, and whether discount rates are consistent with liability measurement. A solvent-looking balance sheet can omit significant commitments appropriately because accounting rules do not require a liability until conditions are met; the board must still incorporate their cash impact in forecasts. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 55–64, 74–79; HEALTH pp. 2–3; TOOL pp. 47–52. Verified standards: AASB 137, AASB 119 and applicable industry guidance.]

### [04.08.03] Inventory, receivables, depreciation and fair value

Inventory usually requires an assessment of net realisable value against cost under AASB 102. Aged stock may require write-down even if purchasing costs are rising. Trade receivables and relevant financial instruments require expected credit loss assessment under AASB 9, not just a check that invoices exist. Depreciation and amortisation depend on useful life, residual value, pattern of economic consumption, capitalisation policy and impairment; lengthening useful life can increase current earnings without changing current cash. Fair value measurement under AASB 13 differentiates market-based observable inputs from more judgmental unobservable inputs (commonly described as levels 1–3); a level 3 number is not inherently wrong, but it requires substantial governance over assumptions and independent checks.

| Judgment | How results might look better without more cash | Board's independent evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Longer asset life or higher residual value | Lower depreciation expense; higher NPAT/equity | Engineering condition and replacement data; peer estimates |
| More capitalised software or development | Expenses deferred; current EBITDA improves | Project approval, recognition criteria, future-benefit test |
| Lower expected credit losses | Receivables and profit increase | Ageing, collections after date, counterparty exposure |
| Less inventory obsolescence | Higher inventory and profit | Real sales prices, ageing, stocktake, return rates |
| Optimistic CGU forecast | Goodwill impairment delayed | External market demand, independent sensitivity, actual versus prior valuation |
| Favourable fair value | OCI/profit or equity rises | Valuation method, market evidence, independent appraisal |
| Underestimated provision | Profit and equity higher | Legal/technical advice and range of potential outcomes |
| Aggressive revenue cut-off | Sales recognised early | Contract terms, delivery evidence, customer acceptance, returns |

### [04.08.04] Going concern is an accounting basis, not proof against insolvency

The going-concern basis assesses whether financial statements appropriately assume continuation of operations, subject to applicable accounting/auditing requirements and disclosure of material uncertainties. It is related to but different from the cash-flow solvency test, the legal timing of debt incurrence and a board's risk appetite. Management may prepare on a going-concern basis when a credible refinancing is sufficiently supported yet uncertainty requires clear disclosure; the board must consider what evidence underpins that judgment and how the next financing deadline is handled. A clean audit opinion or an unmodified opinion with a going-concern disclosure should not be oversimplified. See 04.14 and File 05.

**Board questions:** Which assumptions have the greatest effect on the accounts, and who owns them? When did the board last see actual outcomes against prior-year impairment and provision forecasts? Have financial-report assumptions been reconciled to budget, strategy, asset operations and risk scenarios? What is the earliest evidence that would force a change in the accounting judgment?

## [04.09] Integrated three-way forecasts, budgets, covenant models and controls

### [04.09.01] Every plan must link profit, balance sheet and cash

A **three-way financial model** forecasts the income statement, balance sheet and cash-flow statement consistently. Revenue drives receivables and cash receipts; purchases drive inventory, payables and cash payments; capex drives cash and fixed assets; depreciation reduces profit and fixed assets; new borrowing creates cash and debt; interest affects profit and cash; and retained profit flows into equity. Without these links, a profitable budget may have an impossible cash balance or understated debt. The AICD promotes budgets, forecasts, management accounts and board monitoring; this section develops an explicitly linked board-level model. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 11–22, 50–76; FSH pp. 5–8; IFS pp. 8–11, 35–38. Independent analytical architecture.]

**Independent fully reconciled quarterly example, $m; debt interest and income tax are paid in operating cash flow:**

| Line | Opening balance | Quarter movement | Closing balance / quarter flow |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | — | +120 | 120 income |
| Cost of sales | — | −75 | 75 expense |
| Operating expenses including depreciation 5 | — | −30 | 30 expense |
| **EBIT** | — | **+15** | 15 |
| Interest and tax expense | — | −3 −3 | 6 expense |
| **NPAT** | — | **+9** | 9 |
| Trade receivables | 30 | +10 | 40 |
| Inventory | 25 | +5 | 30 |
| Trade payables | 20 | +5 | 25 |
| Property/plant/equipment | 105 | +12 capex −5 depreciation | 112 |
| Bank debt | 70 | −5 principal repaid | 65 |
| **Operating cash** | — | 9 +5 depreciation −10 receivables −5 inventory +5 payables | **4** |
| **Investing cash** | — | −12 capital expenditure | **−12** |
| **Financing cash** | — | −5 debt principal | **−5** |
| **Cash** | 40 | 4 −12 −5 = −13 | **27** |
| **Total assets** | 200 | 27 cash +40 receivables +30 inventory +112 PPE | **209** |
| **Total liabilities** | 90 | 25 payables +65 debt | **90** |
| **Total equity** | 110 | +9 retained NPAT | **119** |
| **Balance check** | 200 −90 −110 = 0 | — | **209 −90 −119 = 0** |

The model is internally consistent, yet it forecasts cash falling from 40 to 27 despite positive NPAT. It assumes no distributions, transactions in other assets/liabilities, deferred tax or currency movements, no other financing changes and interest included in operating cash. Change these assumptions and the bridge must change. A real group model requires entity-specific tax, depreciation, debt, covenants, working-capital ageing, leases, funding restrictions and consolidations. **Never fill a cash gap with a plug labelled 'additional borrowing' unless drawdown is genuinely committed and permitted.**

### [04.09.02] Build the forecast from observable business drivers

For a large ASX company, forecast volume, realised price, variable cost per unit, fixed cash overheads, royalties/taxes, annual capital expenditure, project construction schedules, debt maturity, financing facilities and covenant ratios. Include the impact of commodity-price, exchange-rate, interest-rate and completion-date distributions where relevant. Translate major capital allocation decisions into risk-weighted cash at stake and return outcomes, not merely a capital budget ceiling.

For a charity or association, forecast contracted programme income, grant receipt timing, restricted and unrestricted programme costs, donor concentration, annual salaries, capital maintenance, fundraising, volunteer capacity and unfunded service obligations. For a CATSI corporation, model corporation operating funds separately from trust/third-party balances and native title funding linked to authorised purposes; project manager estimates are not permission to use those resources. A cash balance supported by an advance grant is not free capital for unrelated projects.

Every forecast needs **base**, **credible downside** and **severe but plausible** cases, with explicit trigger points. The board should be able to identify the earliest month of minimum headroom, the largest maturity or contractual bottleneck, the first covenant breach, and management's executable countermeasures. A reverse stress test asks: **What combination of adverse events would eliminate cash or covenant headroom, and how early would we know?** Don't hide shared assumptions: lower revenue can simultaneously lengthen debtor days, increase inventory, reduce asset values and weaken lending appetite.

### [04.09.03] Budget-to-forecast accountability is not an exercise in defending targets

Set the budget once and keep it as the approved accountability baseline. Update the latest forecast when facts change, rather than altering the baseline to erase poor performance. Monthly reporting should include budget, latest forecast, prior year, year-to-date and whole-year projection, with bridges. Distinguish controllable management performance from external shocks and one-off assumptions; do not allow a favourable commodity price to conceal weak cost control. Reconcile each subsidiary or business unit to the group and reserve the capacity for unaudited material adjustments. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 14–22; FSH internal reporting slides; TOOL board calendar pp. 3–11.]

**Forecast control environment:** independent bank reconciliations; segregation of duties; approved payment limits; anti-fraud vendor onboarding; month-end cut-off controls; stock count and asset verification; audit trails on model assumptions; independent formula and version checks; management sign-off; budget variance escalation; grant account restriction and acquittal review; and explicit review of projections by treasury and the chief financial officer (CFO). Where a small organisation lacks staff to segregate duties, adopt compensating board/treasurer review and external reconciliation. Control failures reduce the evidential value of even an attractive dashboard.

### [04.09.04] Model covenants as contracts, not general market ratios

A lending agreement might test **net debt/EBITDA**, **interest cover**, **debt service coverage**, **minimum liquidity**, **asset value-to-debt** or **minimum net tangible assets**. Each covenant can define leases, cash, subordinated debt, EBITDA adjustments, interest, testing dates, permitted acquisitions, cure rights and accounting-policy changes differently. A bank might exclude unrestricted offshore cash, add lease liabilities or limit the amount of extraordinary earnings adjustments. Some covenants apply to a restricted guarantor group rather than the listed consolidated entity. A company can comply with statutory AASB reporting yet breach a lending definition.

**Independent covenant example:** Net covenant debt $180m, permitted covenant EBITDA $60m, maximum net leverage 3.50×. Current ratio is **3.00×**, giving nominal debt headroom of $30m at current EBITDA (3.5 ×60 −180). If EBITDA falls 20% to $48m, leverage rises to **3.75×** and breaches without any change to debt; allowable debt becomes $168m, requiring $12m debt reduction or other remedy under the actual agreement. The board needs the contractual cure window, reporting obligations, lender communication and credible cash funding, not merely a ratio. A 3.00× ratio is not inherently safe if next month's financing maturities are uncommitted.

## [04.10] Financial ratios: formulas, choices, periods and interpretation

### [04.10.01] Ratio protocol: state the definitions before calculating

For each ratio, disclose: (1) the **reporting unit**, currency and whether amounts are in thousands or millions; (2) the **period** for income/cash figures, e.g. year ended 30 June versus trailing twelve months; (3) the **date** and **average** used for balance-sheet figures; (4) the consolidation and legal-entity perimeter; (5) whether leases, finance costs, tax, restricted cash and discontinued operations are included; (6) accounting versus adjusted measures; and (7) any zero or negative denominator. Ratios using annual profit divided by a point-in-time stock are approximate if the stock changes materially during the year. Use beginning/end averages at a minimum and weighted averages where acquisitions distort the endpoints. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 80–89; TOOL pp. 58–59, 108; IFS pp. 18–38. Independent consistency protocol.]

The AICD Quick Guide (TOOL p.108) lists illustrative thresholds: current ratio ≥1.5, quick ratio ≥0.75, interest cover above 3–4 and ROE above 10% as rough screens; its NFP ratio tool (TOOL pp.58–59) presents even higher current/quick screens. **These are historical teaching heuristics, not accounting rules, market-wide empirical minimums, contractual covenants or legal solvency tests.** Risk depends on collection cycles, cash conversion, maturity dates, credit access, sector economics and funding restrictions. The toolkit's total-liabilities-to-equity calculation is also a different measure from interest-bearing financial debt/equity. Avoid silently labelling both as a single gearing ratio.

### [04.10.02] Liquidity and short-term working capital

| Measure | Exact formula, default period | Variants and what changes | Director interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Current ratio** | Current assets at reporting date / current liabilities at same date | Both stocks, same entity; 1.5 AICD general heuristic and 3.0 NFP teaching heuristic, not universal | How liquid are assets and how near is liability settlement? |
| **Net working capital** | Current assets − current liabilities at reporting date | **Operating NWC** excludes cash/debt and sometimes tax/contract liabilities, if expressly stated | A buffer in book terms; not cash legally available today |
| **Quick ratio (AICD)** | (Current assets − inventory) / current liabilities | More conservative acid test: (cash + eligible short-term investments + collectible trade receivables) / current liabilities; excludes prepayments and other illiquid currents | Is rapid conversion sufficient? Which assets are truly collectable? |
| **Cash ratio** | (Unrestricted cash + eligible cash equivalents) / current liabilities | Optional inclusion of highly liquid near-cash deposits with separate maturity and restriction disclosure | Immediate cover, but assumes all liabilities equally due and ignores near-term collections |
| **Available liquidity** | Eligible unrestricted cash + *committed, usable* undrawn facilities − cash legally reserved or trapped | May also deduct minimum operational cash and security margins; prevent double deduction of restricted cash | How much funding is legally accessible before the next obligations? |
| **Minimum liquidity headroom** | Forecast eligible cash + executable committed drawings − near-term payments and mandatory minimum cash | Weekly/daily schedule; use downside receipts and dated payments | More relevant to imminent insolvency than a year-end current ratio |

**Quick-ratio example, independent:** Current assets $100m comprise cash $20m, receivables $20m, inventory $40m and prepaid expenses $20m; current liabilities $60m. Current ratio is **1.67×**, the course's inventory-only quick ratio is **1.00×**, and the stricter cash-plus-receivables quick ratio is **0.67×** (20+20)/60. The cash ratio is **0.33×**. All are mathematically correct under their definitions; the board must not quote 'quick ratio 1.00×' as if prepayments were cash. If half the receivables are overdue or disputed, rapid liquidity is worse again.

### [04.10.03] Operating working-capital cycle: DSO, DIO, DPO and CCC

Define **D** as the number of days represented by the chosen period (365 for a 365-day financial year; actual days for other periods). Define **average balance** as (opening + closing)/2 unless a more representative time-weighted average is available. For an interim period, use sales/COGS only for the same period and its actual days, not the full 365 by habit. All sales and costs should be consistently GST-exclusive where the underlying statement is GST-exclusive, and the appropriate trade balances, not unrelated 'other receivables/payables', should be used.

| Measure | Preferred formula | Alternate common formulation and limitation | Signal to investigate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Debtor days / Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)** | Average *trade* receivables / credit sales × D | Total sales if credit-sales detail unavailable; non-credit retail sales understate collection days | Rising due to slow customers, billing disputes, weak underwriting or project retentions |
| **Inventory days / Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)** | Average inventory / cost of goods sold × D | Closing inventory / annual COGS is point-in-time approximation; distortions from seasonal stocking | Obsolescence, demand weakness, supply disruption, deliberate safety stock |
| **Creditor days / Days Payable Outstanding (DPO)** | Average *trade* payables / credit purchases × D | COGS used as proxy if purchases unknown; mix of non-stock operating payables distorts | Improving financing or increasing creditor stress? |
| **Cash conversion cycle (CCC)** | DSO + DIO − DPO | Only comparable if same business activities, periods and balance definitions | Cash required to support one turnover of the supply chain |
| **Operating NWC / sales** | Average operating NWC / revenue, or closing NWC / annualised revenue, explicitly labelled | May be negative by design | Capital employed in trade cycle and scalability |

**Independent worked cycle:** Credit sales $365m, COGS $240m, credit purchases $230m, average receivables $36.5m, inventory $48m and trade payables $34.5m. DSO = 36.5/365×365 = **36.5 days**. DIO = 48/240×365 = **73.0 days**. DPO = 34.5/230×365 ≈ **54.75 days**. CCC ≈ **54.75 days**. A five-day rise in DSO at $365m sales, all else equal, ties up approximately **$5m** of extra receivables. A five-day increase in DIO at $240m COGS ties up approximately **$3.29m** of inventory. These are working-capital approximations, not precise sales forecasts. For a cash-sale retailer, reported DSO may be near zero even if suppliers are not paid for weeks; that is a legitimate operating-model difference.

### [04.10.04] Debt, financial structure and coverage

| Measure | Formula | Denominator/boundary questions and pitfalls |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross financial debt** | Sum of interest-bearing loans, bonds and financing obligations at date | Clarify leases, overdrafts, convertible instruments, supplier finance, project debt, guarantees and non-controlling interests; gross debt is a currency amount, not a ratio |
| **Net debt** | Defined gross financial debt − eligible cash/cash equivalents | Negative result indicates 'net cash' under that definition; ring-fence pledged/restricted cash; optional lease-inclusive version |
| **Debt/equity, financial** | Gross interest-bearing debt / book equity | Use consolidated amounts or contractually relevant legal entity; negative equity makes result economically unhelpful |
| **Debt/equity, total-liability** | Total liabilities / book equity | AICD quick-guide variant; includes payables, provisions, deferred revenue, etc.; do not compare directly with financial-debt/equity |
| **Net debt/EBITDA** | Net financial debt at a date / EBITDA for matching trailing twelve months | Lessee and acquisition adjustments, negative EBITDA, asset disposals, seasonality and covenant definitions matter |
| **Interest cover, EBIT** | EBIT for period / finance interest expense for same period | Gross vs net finance expense; borrowing versus lease interest; accounting interest versus cash interest |
| **Cash interest cover** | CFO before cash interest and tax / cash interest paid for period, using explicitly normalised CFO | Some interest already in CFO; eliminate double counting; tax and contract definition vary |
| **Fixed-charge cover** | (EBITDA − maintenance capex − cash taxes) / (cash interest + fixed rent/lease cash costs), one illustrative cash variant | No universal definition; lease, principal and preferred dividend charges require agreement-specific treatment |
| **Debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR)** | Cash available for debt service (CFADS) / (scheduled principal + interest and relevant fees) | CFADS must be derived from legal/project cash flows and reserve mechanics; covenants set their own definition |
| **Equity ratio** | Book equity / total assets | Can rise after share issue even if capital deployment earns below its cost |
| **Debt / long-term assets** | Defined total borrowings / non-current assets | AICD NFP educational screen, *not* equivalent to debt/equity, security coverage or cash leverage |

**Coverage examples, independent:** EBIT $80m and cash/expense interest $20m produce interest cover **4.00×**. If EBIT declines to $30m with interest unchanged, cover falls to **1.50×**. If scheduled principal of $35m is added and the company has CFADS of $48m, DSCR = 48/(20+35) = **0.87×**, meaning internally generated cash is short of scheduled service even though interest alone appears covered. A bullet maturity can make a company illiquid before leverage looks extreme. Interest cover is not an amount of free cash.

### [04.10.05] Profitability and efficiency: match earnings to the capital that generated them

| Measure | Standard formula and timing | Important interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross margin** | (Revenue − cost of goods sold) / revenue ×100, same period | Product mix, mark-ups, transfer pricing, stock valuation |
| **Operating/EBIT margin** | Defined operating EBIT / revenue ×100 | Show excluded other income/finance; sector differences and leases |
| **EBITDA margin** | Defined EBITDA / revenue ×100 | Capital and lease accounting bias; not free cash |
| **Net margin** | NPAT attributable to defined owners / revenue ×100 | Tax, finance costs, minority interests and exceptional items |
| **Return on assets (ROA)** | EBIT / average total assets ×100 (AICD EBIT variant) | An alternative NPAT/average assets exists; label before comparison |
| **Return on equity (ROE)** | NPAT attributable to ordinary shareholders / average ordinary equity ×100 | Leverage can inflate; negative equity or loss distorts; exclude/adjust minorities |
| **Return on invested capital (ROIC)** | Net operating profit after tax (NOPAT) / average invested capital ×100 | NOPAT often EBIT × (1−normalised operating tax rate); define goodwill, surplus cash, leases and minorities |
| **Capital employed** | Operating assets − non-interest-bearing operating liabilities; alternatively equity + net financial debt under consistent perimeter | Avoid adding debt to a capital measure that already embeds it; average consistently |
| **Asset turnover** | Sales / average total assets (times per year) | Capital-intensive assets, vintage, depreciation and acquired assets bias |
| **Fixed-asset turnover** | Sales / average operating PPE and other defined productive fixed assets | Leased/owned mix must be aligned |
| **Unit margin** | (Realised unit revenue − defined variable or full unit cost) / physical unit | Allocation basis, capacity utilisation and quality differ by sector |
| **Staff productivity** | Revenue, gross value added, output or outcomes / FTE or hours | Outsourcing and volunteer input complicate peer comparisons |
| **Capacity utilisation** | Actual throughput / sustainable available capacity, by consistent unit and period | A high figure may reflect overuse, deferred maintenance or bottlenecks |

**ROIC example, independent:** EBIT $120m, normalised cash-neutral operating tax rate 30%, NOPAT $84m. Average operating capital employed $900m, so ROIC = **9.33%**. If the after-tax weighted average cost of capital (WACC) is 10.0% on the same nominal currency basis, the economic-profit proxy is **(9.33% −10.0%) ×$900m ≈ −$6m**. Revenue growth or EBIT growth is not proof of shareholder value creation when additional investment earns below the marginal capital cost. Accounting ROIC, weighted average funding costs, intangible treatment and the proper risk-adjusted discount rate carry uncertainty, so treat small spread differences with care.

### [04.10.06] Cash discipline, shareholder return and economic value

| Measure | Calculation / convention | Use and principal risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Cash conversion, profit basis** | CFO / NPAT, same period | Large values may reflect noncash charges/advances; near-zero or negative NPAT makes unstable |
| **Cash conversion, EBITDA basis** | CFO / EBITDA | CFO interest/tax classification and lease adjustments must be normalised |
| **FCF margin** | (CFO − defined capex) / revenue ×100 | Can be boosted temporarily by cancelling essential reinvestment |
| **Cash capex / depreciation** | Defined recurring cash capex / depreciation and amortisation | A rough replacement test only; inflation, growth and ROU accounting distort |
| **Dividend cash coverage** | Defined post-maintenance FCF / cash dividends paid | Reinvestments, maturities and restriction matter; not statutory distribution test |
| **Basic EPS** | Profit attributable to ordinary holders / weighted-average ordinary shares | Weighted shares, dilution and share issues; usually cents/share |
| **Diluted EPS** | Profit adjusted for dilutive instruments / diluted weighted-average shares | Potential options, conversions, contingently issuable shares |
| **Dividend yield** | Dividend per share for defined trailing or expected period / share price at stated date | Price can change; income may be unsustainable |
| **Dividend payout** | Declared annual DPS / EPS, or cash paid / NPAT, each labelled | Declared FY dividends and dividends *paid* in FY are different cohorts |
| **Total shareholder return (TSR)** | (Ending share value + dividends received, adjusted for capital events/reinvestment policy − opening value) / opening value, for specified holding interval | Need treatment of rights, splits, buybacks, currencies and reinvestment; distinct from operating return |
| **Enterprise value (EV)** | Equity market capitalisation + net financial debt + defined non-controlling interests and other claims | Lease debt, pensions, cash eligibility and contingent claims require consistency |
| **Economic profit** | (ROIC − WACC) × average invested capital, or NOPAT − WACC×capital | Accounting proxy for value creation, not market value, not cash solvency |
| **NPV** | Sum of risk-consistent discounted future *incremental* cash flows − upfront investment | Scenario, terminal value, discounting and liquidity constraints; complementary to ROIC |

For investment analysis, distinguish **accounting performance** (NPAT), **liquidity** (cash available by date), **solvency** (ability to pay all debts when due), **economic value** (risk-adjusted present value of future cash) and **shareholder value realisation** (TSR/market prices and distributions). These can diverge sharply. A company can increase ROE by increasing leverage while destroying value through greater financial distress risk; acquire revenue at unattractive prices; or report a large accounting equity balance while market equity values the business much higher or lower because of future prospects. In a charity, market-equity metrics generally have no meaningful equivalent, and mission outcomes must be measured directly. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 80–89; TOOL pp. 58–59, 108. Independent corporate finance extensions: ROIC, WACC, EV, TSR and NPV.]

### [04.10.07] Ratio traps and what to request instead

- **Current ratio high, cash low:** ask for asset-realisation calendar, stock ageing, debtors and available facilities. 
- **EBITDA rising, cash falling:** ask for capex, working-capital movement, leases, finance charges and customer advance bridge. 
- **ROE rising, equity falling:** ask whether leverage or buybacks, not better unit economics, drove the ratio. 
- **Debt/EBITDA falling after an acquisition:** ask for pro forma full-year earnings, cash-paid price, capex, minority ownership and covenant exclusions. 
- **Strong profit despite large impairment reversal, tax benefit or disposal gain:** request recurring operating earnings and associated cash flows. 
- **NFP surplus improves, free liquidity falls:** ask for restricted/unrestricted funds, future grant obligations and cash reserve coverage. 
- **A ratio outside AICD benchmarks:** ask what the actual cash and financing risk is; don't automatically pronounce the organisation insolvent or healthy. 
- **Ratio has a zero or negative denominator:** display the numerator and denominator separately, record 'not meaningful' rather than report a misleading percentage or negative multiple.

## [04.11] Comparing performance across years, peers, budgets and cycles

### [04.11.01] Separate genuine productivity from mechanical growth

Begin with a comparison bridge in **absolute dollars, percentage change and percentage-point change**, preserving unrounded input values. For revenues, separate selling volume, realised price, product/service mix, foreign currency, new sites, acquisitions, closed sites and accounting timing. For profit, add operating leverage, input costs, one-time expenses and tax effects. For invested capital, distinguish organic capex, acquired balances, depreciation, impairment, working-capital movements and revaluation. For cash, reconcile financing, divestments, restricted funds, and genuine operational cash conversion.

A year's growth can be distorted by a newly acquired subsidiary with only two months of income but the whole balance sheet consolidated at year-end. A four-year trend can be distorted by the pandemic, reopening effects and extraordinary order backlogs. A retail chain's headline revenue can grow as store count rises while same-store revenue and margin fall. A higher NPAT can occur after a large capital raise because interest income rises, not because the business model improves. A high return on equity can be caused by a low or shrinking accounting equity denominator rather than improved economics. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 64–89, 105–126; IFS pp. 12–38; FSH Nick Scali materials.]

### [04.11.02] Comparable peers require a common economic perimeter

When comparing companies, document reporting currencies, consolidation boundaries, acquisitions, fiscal periods, tax structures, lease accounting, vertical integration, owned versus leased property, capitalised expenditures, inventory or revenue recognition methods and accounting estimates. A lower EBITDA margin may coexist with superior cash conversion; capital-intensive infrastructure may have higher EBITDA but also much higher maintenance capex. ROIC comparisons require approximately comparable goodwill and invested-capital treatment. A listed company's equity market value includes forward expectations; peer multiples should distinguish enterprise-value-to-EBITDA from price/earnings, and the definition of debt and lease liabilities must align.

**Illustration, independent:** Company A has EBITDA $100m, cash maintenance capex $60m and assets employed $1bn. Company B has EBITDA $80m, maintenance capex $10m and assets employed $300m. At identical working capital, taxes and finance costs, A has greater EBITDA but materially lower cash yield on assets than B. A narrative promoting EBITDA growth alone could recommend allocating capital to the wrong business. Value judgment requires expected future after-tax *incremental cash* and capital cost, not only historical reported multiples.

### [04.11.03] Inflation, cycles and the baseline problem

Nominal revenue growth of 5% during relevant cost inflation of 6% is not necessarily real growth, although the correct comparison is an entity-specific price/volume decomposition rather than a single broad consumer price index (CPI). A 2023 CPI rate cannot be applied mechanically to 2026. For a commodity producer, compare results over the full commodity cycle using consistent price assumptions. For a charity, check that annual funding escalation covers actual wage and service inflation; flat nominal grants can reduce service capacity even where reported expenditure remains within budget. When benchmarking against peers, use a common currency and exchange basis, and consider regional costs and asset vintage. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 80–89; METHOD pp. 1–3; HEALTH pp. 1, 3; TOOL pp. 53–59. Independent extension: real/nominal and cycle normalisation.]

### [04.11.04] Manipulation and incentive risk

Financial reporting can be biased without an obvious fraudulent transaction. Examples include: booking sales before enforceable performance; releasing provisions close to bonus targets; increasing capitalisation of operating costs; understating obsolete stock; delaying goodwill impairment; changing depreciation useful lives; cutting maintenance to lift cash flow; stretching payables just before the balance date; drawing down a facility temporarily to window-dress cash; excluding routine costs from 'underlying' profit; pursuing acquisitions to lift reported revenue; and designing rewards around EBITDA without charging for capital employed. The board's information system should test **unintended behavioural incentives**, not simply whether KPIs are mathematically correct.

For each headline metric, pair a balancing metric: revenue with cash collections and contract quality; EBITDA with maintenance capex and free cash flow; ROE with leverage and risk-adjusted ROIC; programme cost ratios with outcomes and service quality; fundraising efficiency with donor retention and compliance; liquidity with payable ageing, restrictions and legal due dates. Independent director questions should distinguish *honest estimation risk* from *pressure to meet targets*. A control failure can require an accounting investigation even where an independent valuation eventually supports management's number.

**Board questions:** What facts contradict this favourable narrative? What would the result have been on an unchanged accounting and asset perimeter? Which profitability gains required additional capital, financial risk or diminished service quality? Has the same favourable adjustment appeared for several years under different names?

## [04.12] Financial governance for charities, WA associations and CATSI/RNTBC corporations

### [04.12.01] Financial purpose and resource ownership are not uniform

A listed company's central financial goal normally includes sustainable shareholder value creation within law and stakeholder obligations. A charity's finances support its charitable purposes; making a surplus and maintaining adequate reserves can be prudent, but distributing private profits to members is not its purpose. A WA incorporated association must use resources consistently with its rules, lawful purposes and statutory obligations. A CATSI corporation may combine service delivery, cultural responsibilities, community economic development and, for an RNTBC, statutory native title-holder functions. The corporation's own operating money, community programme funds and third-party or trust property are not interchangeable merely because the same staff or bank provider handles them. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 11–13, 80–89; FAPP pp. 7–14; TOOL pp. 58–59. Legal structures and governance addressed comprehensively in Files 06–07.]

**[Verified regulatory distinction, October 2026]** The Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission (ACNC) classifies registered charities by annual **revenue**: small under $500,000; medium $500,000 to less than $3 million; large $3 million or more. Reporting/assurance obligations differ. A charity may also be a CATSI corporation regulated and reporting to the Office of the Registrar of Indigenous Corporations (ORIC) rather than following ordinary ACNC financial submission routes; verify its actual registrations and statutory exemptions. A WA incorporated association's state obligations are not identical to a company limited by guarantee, even if both are registered charities. These regimes are handled in detail in Files 05–07. [ACNC, *2026 Annual Information Statement Hub*, https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/annual-information-statement/2026-annual-information-statement-hub; *Charity size*, https://www.acnc.gov.au/tools/topic-guides/charity-size; accessed 27 Oct 2026.]

### [04.12.02] Restricted grants, donations, deferred revenue and unrestricted liquidity

A grant agreement can restrict spending by service type, beneficiary, geography, project, timing or deliverables. Donation restrictions, charitable trusts, native title trusts and funds held as agent may impose other obligations. Accounting recognition of grant receipts may produce **deferred revenue** or another liability when the relevant criteria are met, but *legal expenditure restrictions* are a separate analysis. Conversely, an amount recognised as revenue or equity may still be restricted by law or contract. A board cannot infer lawful spending authority from a consolidated cash balance or surplus. [AICD FAPP pp. 7–14; ACNC, *Charity reserves: financial stability and sustainability*, https://www.acnc.gov.au/tools/guides/charity-reserves-financial-stability-and-sustainability; ACNC, *National Standard Chart of Accounts*, https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/national-standard-chart-accounts, accessed Oct 2026.]

**Illustration, independent:** Bank cash $12m comprises unspent conditional grants $7m, identified trust money $3m and unrestricted operating cash $2m. General corporate creditors cannot assume $12m is freely available: legal title, reporting control and grant/trust terms must be examined, and the $3m might not be the organisation's beneficial asset at all. If unrestricted overhead commitments next month are $3m, the relevant gap may be **$1m**, notwithstanding a headline bank balance four times those costs. A transfer between bank accounts does not extinguish restriction; nor can it authorise a payment that the trustee or corporation lacks power to make.

**Restricted-funds matrix to request:** Funding source and original instrument; recipient and legal owner; accounting classification; cash held and location; current receivable or liability; amount committed to approved purposes; obligation fulfilment; required consent for changes; expiry or repayment/clawback conditions; person or decision-making body legally empowered to authorise expenditure. The treasurer should reconcile this to cash, contract liabilities, program budgets, grant acquittals and the general ledger monthly. Seek legal advice if documentation is ambiguous.

### [04.12.03] NFP metrics must measure both financial capacity and social outcomes

| NFP measure | Defined calculation | What a director should test |
|---|---|---|
| **Unrestricted liquid reserve** | Unrestricted cash + immediately realisable unrestricted investments − cash already pledged/committed to near-term obligations | Legal release conditions, timing, and whether investments can really be sold |
| **Unrestricted liquidity coverage** | Unrestricted liquid resources / forecast unavoidable unrestricted cash expenses over chosen period | Debt dates and unfunded programmes, not only current liabilities |
| **Reserve cover, months** | Unrestricted operating reserves / average monthly *unrestricted recurring operating cash costs* | Choice of burn rate; seasonality, emergency spending, strategic reserve policy |
| **Grant/donor concentration** | Largest single funder income or top five funder income / total relevant funding over period | Correlated renewals, political and contractual concentration |
| **Income diversity** | Share of earned services, grants, donations, investments and other income; optionally concentration index | Reliability and common exposure, not merely number of funding streams |
| **Unfunded commitment cover** | Available unrestricted liquid cash / unavoidable commitments not funded by enforceable grants | Cost and timing of obligations already undertaken |
| **Programme expense ratio** | Defined programme expenses / relevant total expenses | Allocations must be consistent; low administration cost is not always efficient |
| **Administration expense ratio** | Defined administration expenses / total expense | Governance, safety and reporting quality can require legitimate overhead |
| **Fundraising cost ratio** | Direct and allocated fundraising expense / cash and noncash fundraising revenue, consistently defined | Beware delayed donor lifetime value, counting donated goods, and unstable campaigns |
| **Cost per outcome** | Cost attributable to a programme / measured verified relevant outcome quantity | Specify attribution, quality, counterfactual, and whether outcomes are comparable |
| **Asset reinvestment** | Maintenance/replacement capex / estimated annual sustainable renewal requirement | Voluntary fire/surf safety equipment, IT, fleet and accessible facilities |
| **Mission-value dashboard** | Outcomes, equity of beneficiary access, cultural and community legitimacy, financial sustainability | Do not monetise every human outcome artificially |

The AICD NFP tool illustrates a current ratio of **3×**, a quick ratio of **1×** and fundraising-cost heuristics, but itself notes that universally accepted NFP ratios and sector benchmarks do not exist. Its proposed 'asset sustainability' relationship (non-current assets relative to depreciation) must not be confused with a cash-funded replacement ratio; non-current book asset value divided by annual depreciation has units of **years**, not a replacement percentage. A programme with high safeguarding and governance costs may deliver better outcomes despite an apparently less efficient administration ratio. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 87–89; TOOL pp. 58–59. Independent critique.]

### [04.12.04] RNTBC and CATSI board financial questions

CATSI corporations have a distinct statutory governance regime. The CATSI Act applies various Corporations Act insolvency provisions through statutory mechanisms, including s 531-1; the directors' duty and legal exemptions require entity-specific verification. RNTBC directors must distinguish corporation membership, common law native title holders, relevant beneficiaries and the authority governing native title decisions and funds. **A director cannot substitute a board vote or an accounting label for required native title holder authorisation, trust terms or third-party consent.** [CATSI Act 2006 (Cth), ss 262-1 and 531-1; official Federal Register of Legislation, https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2006A00124; ORIC, *Dealing with insolvency*, https://www.oric.gov.au/for-corporations/resolving-problems/financial-difficulties/dealing-insolvency, verified 27 Oct 2026.]

A board should request a legally mapped funding flow: incoming compensation or benefit payments, trust account or corporation account, distribution/administration expenses, future obligations, beneficiary records, investment mandates, tax and reporting classification, and entity-by-entity cash forecasts. Separately assess ability to finance the corporation's core administration, such as directors, accounting, governance meetings, community engagement and native title processes. Failure to fund this infrastructure can leave substantial trust assets inaccessible for ordinary operations, or create pressure to use money for unauthorised purposes. The solution is not an assumed transfer; it may require a documented permitted administration fee, a properly authorised funding agreement, external support or expenditure reductions.

**Board questions across all three contexts:** Which money can *this legal entity* spend, on *which purpose*, with *whose authority*? Is there sufficient unrestricted cash to pay wages, suppliers, tax and lease costs as they fall due? Are we performing our purpose and measuring the real cost and benefit of outcomes? If one funder stops in three months, what obligations remain and how will they be met?

## [04.13] AICD step-by-step financial-review methodologies and their limits

### [04.13.01] Seven-step balance-sheet screen, critically applied

The standalone IFS review methodology teaches an ordered high-level scan. The following is an original restatement retaining its **seven steps** rather than reproducing its wording. [AICD METHOD pp. 1–2; IFS pp. 22–26 and duplicate annex pp. 68–69; HEALTH pp. 1–3; TOOL pp. 53–55.]

| Step | Source's focal measure | Strengthened director interrogation |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Total assets | How and why changed? Include acquisitions, accounting revaluations and impairment |
| **2** | Total liabilities | Which obligations are cash due, conditional, contractual, noncash or contingent? |
| **3** | Total equity | Reconcile profit, OCI, capital, distributions; negative equity signals concern but not a statutory verdict |
| **4** | Current assets and mix | Liquidity of cash, deposits, debtors, inventory, prepaid items and restrictions |
| **5** | Current liabilities versus current assets | Net working capital, maturity ladder, customer advances and covenant acceleration; disregard universal 1.5 test |
| **6** | Non-current assets | Capex, disposals, lease assets, impairment, goodwill, maintenance backlog |
| **7** | Non-current liabilities | Debt and lease maturity, transfers from long-term to current, new facilities and guarantees |

**Methodological corrections:** Higher assets can result from an expensive acquisition or debt-financed asset purchase rather than value creation. Higher equity may merely reflect new issuance. An increase in non-current assets does not prove capex; currency, revaluation, acquisitions and lease recognition can move balances. A fall in non-current debt may be a **reclassification to current** rather than repayment; Discount Depot FY2024 illustrates this. Treat the source's suggested 1.5 current ratio as a screening prompt; confirm legal solvency with cash payment dates. For goodwill, investigate absolute book value, fraction of equity, cash-generating-unit sensitivity and asset-sale recoverability rather than interpreting a 50% threshold as a rule.

### [04.13.02] Four-step income-statement screen

| Step | Source's focal measure | Stronger review |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Sales/revenue change | Split price, volume, mix, acquisitions, inflation, reporting calendar and contract quality |
| **2** | Total and material expenses | Compare with revenue and capacity; cost of goods sold, labour, lease/rent, impairments and capitalisation |
| **3** | Profit before tax | Reconcile EBIT, EBITDA, interest, nonrecurring items and finance income |
| **4** | NPAT | Explain tax, minorities, one-off adjustments, cash conversion and retained profit |

Avoid judging a cost increase as inherently adverse: expenditure on productive capacity, legally necessary safety, volunteer support or strategic capability can be entirely rational. The question is whether **marginal benefit and return exceed full marginal cost**, with financing resilience and mission obligations taken into account. [AICD METHOD p. 3; IFS pp. 14–17; FIN pp. 64–70.]

### [04.13.03] Five-step cash-flow screen and source 'three tests'

| Step | Source's focal measure | Independent extension |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Closing cash compared with opening cash | Reconcile term deposits, restricted balances, FX and funding events |
| **2** | Positive, improving OCF versus NPAT | Explain noncash and working-capital causes; do not equate CFO to profit or solvency |
| **3** | Investing cash flow | Split maintenance, growth, acquisitions, investment deposits and disposals |
| **4** | Financing cash flow | Gross debt raised/repaid, leases, equity and dividends; check cash under contracts |
| **5** | Self-generation | CFO versus relevant maintenance/growth capex, leases, debt service and shareholder distributions |

IFS p. 29 also describes three pedagogical checks for adverse cash generation: operations producing net cash, customer cash receipts relative to supplier/employee payments, and OCF adequacy for investment and financing/distributions. These are **indicators**, not the three separate legal tests for Australian insolvency. IFS pp. 30–31 show a four-step cash-review exercise in one presentation, while METHOD p. 4 explicitly adds the **fifth self-generation step**. Preserve that difference in source formats instead of claiming all versions contain identical step counts. [AICD IFS pp. 27–34; METHOD p. 4; HEALTH pp. 4–5.]

**Stop condition for any checklist:** The board should not conclude 'healthy' or 'insolvent' solely from its green/red answer. Each exception is a question to investigate using notes, cash forecasts, contracts and business knowledge. A low ratio with credible unrestricted cash receipts next week may be manageable; favourable ratios with uncommitted refinancing or restricted funds can conceal imminent default.

## [04.14] Liquidity crises, cash-flow insolvency and directors' legal responsibilities

### [04.14.01] The Australian legal solvency question is payment when due

**[Verified law as at 27 October 2026: Corporations Act companies]** Under *Corporations Act 2001* (Cth) s 95A, a person is solvent if and only if able to pay **all debts as and when they become due and payable**; otherwise the person is insolvent. A director therefore needs a timely, evidence-based view of cash resources, realisable assets, likely receipts and actual payment dates. Net profit, positive equity and a current ratio do not establish that ability. Conversely, accumulated losses and current liabilities exceeding current assets are concerning but are not each a statutory declaration of insolvency. [*Corporations Act 2001*, s 95A, compilation C149 dated 19 September 2026, https://www.legislation.gov.au/Latest/C2017C00328; ASIC, *Insolvency for directors*, https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/insolvency/insolvency-for-directors, verified 27 Oct 2026; AICD FIN pp. 90–97.]

**Cash-flow insolvency versus other concepts:**

| Concept | Question | Relevant evidence | What not to infer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquidity | Do we have accessible cash or credible liquidity soon enough? | Dated receipts, payments, banks, committed facility capacity | Reported cash is automatically available |
| Legal solvency | Can *this entity* pay all debts when legally due? | Current and forecast full obligation calendar, realistic sources of payment | A current-ratio threshold is a legal rule |
| Balance-sheet net assets | Do reported assets exceed liabilities on an accounting basis? | Balance sheet, impairments, contingencies | Positive book equity proves cash solvency |
| Financial performance | Is profit/surplus being generated in the period? | Income statement, margins, cash conversion | Positive NPAT means existing debt can be refinanced |
| Going-concern accounting | Is use of the going-concern reporting basis appropriate and are disclosures sufficient? | Management forecasts, funding evidence, auditor challenge | Auditor has guaranteed future survival |
| Enterprise value | What is the risk-adjusted present value of enterprise future cash flows? | DCF and market values, contingencies | High enterprise value pays this week's wages |

**Independent short illustration:** A company has $100m of valuable property, $40m of current accounting assets, $30m of liabilities and $10m in bank. A $20m secured debt matures tomorrow; no lender has agreed to refinance, the property cannot be sold tomorrow and the entity has no accessible other cash. The potential long-term value or accounting surplus does not solve the immediate payment shortfall. Conversely, a supermarket with current liabilities above current assets can have strong daily collections and contractually stable trade credit; it still needs evidence that all debts can be paid at the required dates.

### [04.14.02] Debt-incurrence duty, safe harbour and legal entity differences

**Corporations Act companies:** s 588G addresses the director's duty to prevent a company incurring relevant debt when insolvent or becoming insolvent, including the required awareness/reasonable-ground elements. Sections 588H and 588GA provide distinct statutory defences and a conditional safe harbour from civil liability; they are not a general licence to conceal distress or incur debts without a documented turnaround. Safe harbour under s 588GA is connected to developing a course of action reasonably likely to produce a better outcome than immediate administration or liquidation, with statutory conditions including employee entitlements and tax reporting compliance and an evidentiary burden. ASIC's revised Regulatory Guide 217 was issued **6 December 2024**. Directors should seek prompt independent advice because relief depends on the facts and precise debt and date. [*Corporations Act 2001*, ss 588G, 588GA, 588H, C149; ASIC, *RG 217 Duty to prevent insolvent trading: Guide for directors*, https://asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/find-a-document/regulatory-guides/rg-217-duty-to-prevent-insolvent-trading-guide-for-directors/; AICD FIN pp. 92–97; LEG pp. 47–50.]

**WA incorporated associations:** The *Associations Incorporation Act 2015* (WA), s 127, has its own offence concerning a management committee member when an association incurs debt while insolvent or becomes insolvent, coupled with its statutory state-of-knowledge criteria and defences. Its wording is not the same as s 588G, and Corporations Act safe harbour should **not** automatically be transplanted. The official WA consolidation checked is **00-c0-00, current from 23 August 2025**. [WA Parliamentary Counsel, *Associations Incorporation Act 2015*, s 127, https://www.legislation.wa.gov.au/legislation/statutes.nsf/main_mrtitle_13715_homepage.html, checked 27 Oct 2026; AICD LEG pp. 47–54 and LAPP p. 5 (comparison table historically dated March 2024).]

**CATSI corporations/RNTBCs:** The CATSI Act has its own duties, applies specified Corporations Act provisions through mechanisms such as s 531-1 and includes specific interactions with native title obligations. Directors must establish the precise applicability, corporation's legal obligations and ownership of relevant funds. ORIC's insolvency guidance identifies delayed payments, growing borrowing, grant interruption and inaccurate records as warning signs. The existence of assets held on trust for native title holders does not necessarily establish funds available to pay the corporation's own creditors. [*CATSI Act 2006*, ss 262-1, 531-1, official https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2006A00124; ORIC, *Dealing with insolvency*, https://www.oric.gov.au/for-corporations/resolving-problems/financial-difficulties/dealing-insolvency; verified 27 Oct 2026.]

**Registered charities:** ACNC Governance Standard 5 requires a charity to take reasonable steps concerning its Responsible People's duties, including responsible financial management and not allowing operation while insolvent, with statutory entity overlays that cannot be assumed identical. Seek advice concerning a charity that is simultaneously a company, association or CATSI corporation. [ACNC, *Charity reserves: financial stability and sustainability*, cited in 04.12; AICD FIN pp. 11–13; File 01/06 for legal detail.]

### [04.14.03] Forecast cash and evidence, not only a solvent-looking balance date

A solvency review should use a **cash-payments calendar** for suppliers, employees, tax, superannuation, leases, rent, lenders, bonds, grants and restitution obligations; identify payment timing under each contract; separately test disputed and accelerated liabilities; and assess financing on evidence rather than aspiration. A 13-week detailed forecast is particularly useful for emerging distress, combined with a 12–18-month integrated outlook and longer maturities. The AICD's historical 2016 *Fifty Matters* guide suggests a detailed forecast covering at least 12 months from signing as a sign-off prompt, but the actual statutory test, accounting going-concern horizon and board's risk horizon must be evaluated for each entity and date. [AICD TOOL p. 124; FIFTY 3rd ed., October 2016, p. 11; FIN pp. 90–97; IFS pp. 27–34.]

A good forecast has a daily or weekly opening bank position, expected collections by debtor and probability, cash expenses by exact due date, grant or trust restrictions, minimum operating cash, committed drawable facilities, covenant and security conditions, tax and superannuation schedules, and forecast residual. Show base, downside and downside-without-new-funding. List specific actions by date: accelerate legitimate collections, negotiate real extensions, cancel optional spending, seek subscribed equity or lender agreement, dispose of assets subject to proper authority, obtain professional restructuring advice, or initiate external administration if required. The board must ask whether each action can be executed **before** the funding gap, without breaching other duties.

### [04.14.04] Escalation indicators and directors' actions

Early warnings include deteriorating net operating cash, repeated negative cash conversion, aged creditors, overdue tax/superannuation, payment plans, nonpayment on agreed terms, supplier cash-on-delivery demands, exhausted overdrafts, withheld information, unexpected finance director departures, covenant waivers, inability to collect debts, rapid losses, loss of a major funder, and reliance on a vague 'next major sale' to survive. **No single sign conclusively proves insolvency**; several correlated signs demand urgent independent evaluation. [AICD FIN pp. 91–97; ASIC, *Insolvency for directors*, cited above; ORIC, *Dealing with insolvency*.]

Directors should request same-day or urgent cash and creditor information where warranted, call a properly convened board meeting, seek qualified insolvency and legal advice, challenge all assumptions, consider stopping additional debt incurrence, preserve records, review potential conflicts and related-party dealings, and document the legal advice, evidence, alternatives and timing of decisions. Do not prefer connected creditors or move assets improperly. Recording a general board assurance of 'solvency' without investigating contradictory cash evidence is weak governance. In companies facing real risk, the board must consider creditor interests under applicable law. Full director-duty analysis belongs in File 01.

**Decision threshold:** If the entity will miss a material obligation and has no credible, lawful and timely source of payment, the board must address it as a potential insolvency event, not as a routine budget variance.

## [04.15] Financial distress: choosing among recovery, restructuring and closure

### [04.15.01] Identify the actual problem before selecting the remedy

A **temporary liquidity mismatch** arises where long-run economics and funding are sound but cash arrives after a debt deadline; negotiation or bridge financing may help. **Balance-sheet overleverage** exists where the business produces adequate unlevered cash but debt maturities or interest are unsustainable; recapitalisation or creditor restructuring may help. **Operating-model failure** exists when demand, margins and costs cannot support the business even without debt; refinancing alone merely postpones losses. **Governance or funding-purpose failure** arises where resources exist but may not lawfully fund the proposed activity; it requires purpose-aligned funding and proper approval, not creative accounting.

The directors' first task is to preserve lawful options and compare expected outcomes for creditors, the company and relevant beneficiaries. A turnaround should be supported by integrated cash projections, operational measures, staff and supplier costs, contract termination liabilities and probabilities. It should not depend on perpetual capital raising or optimistic future margins. [AICD teaching: FIN pp. 90–100; LEG pp. 47–50; FSH insolvency slides, PDF pp. 20–22.]

### [04.15.02] Remedies and whose position they change

| Option | When it might work | Costs, timing and governance issues |
|---|---|---|
| Operational turnaround | Underlying demand and contribution margin can recover | Close weak activities; meet employee/lease obligations; finance transition; credible milestones |
| Supplier/payment negotiation | Temporary timing shortfall | Consent, enforceability, equality/creditor risks, future supply reliability |
| Refinancing and covenant waiver | Earnings sustainable; debt maturity or covenant is the bottleneck | New security, cost, conditions precedent, guarantee and execution date |
| Shareholder/member capital injection, donations or unrestricted funding | Viable entity needs capital not more debt | Dilution for equity companies; donor or members' purpose and restrictions for NFPs |
| Asset disposal | Noncore asset sale has executable net proceeds before need | Realisable value, tax, bank consent, trust conditions; avoid destroying future mission |
| Formal small-business restructuring | Eligible Corporations Act company with viable proposal | A registered restructuring practitioner; specific eligibility and procedural conditions |
| Voluntary administration | Insolvency or likely insolvency and prospect of better outcome under administration | Administrator's control and creditor votes; possible deed of company arrangement (DOCA) |
| Receivership | Security holder enforces relevant security | Receiver's role, secured property and obligations differ from whole-company administrator |
| Liquidation/winding up | Continuation unlikely or inappropriate | Liquidator realises property, investigates affairs and distributes under priorities; creditors may lose money |

**[Verified current guidance]** ASIC's small-business restructuring pathway generally requires, among other eligibility conditions, **total company liabilities not exceeding $1 million** at appointment, plus director/entity history and other conditions. This is not a generic scheme available to every large charity, association or RNTBC. [ASIC, *Small business restructuring and the restructuring plan*, https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/insolvency/insolvency-for-directors/small-business-restructuring-and-the-restructuring-plan, verified 27 Oct 2026.]

### [04.15.03] Administration, DOCA and liquidation are not synonyms

**Voluntary administration** provides a formal process to examine whether the company, or its business, can be preserved and whether creditors receive a better outcome; it is not a guarantee of continued trading. A **DOCA** is a binding arrangement approved under relevant procedures that can compromise creditor claims and provide a route out of administration, subject to its terms. **Receivership** commonly focuses on secured property and enforcement rights, which may coexist with other insolvency processes. **Liquidation** involves winding up the company's affairs and distributing property according to statutory priorities; a solvent members' voluntary winding up differs from an insolvent creditors' winding up. An association or CATSI corporation may have specific administration, winding-up and approval provisions and should not be forced into a generic Corporations Act template. [AICD FIN pp. 97–100, 115–126; ASIC, *Wind up an insolvent company*, https://www.asic.gov.au/for-business-and-companies/companies/company-deregistration-and-winding-up/wind-up-an-insolvent-company; ORIC financial difficulties guidance.]

The Godfreys historical case in 04.17 illustrates why a sale of selected assets or the trading business can fail to rescue every legal entity or fully repay all creditors. A DOCA can give certain creditors a better result than liquidation while others still recover little or nothing. Directors must separately consider employee entitlements, tax, secured financing, suppliers, grant creditors and related parties. **The decision is not whether to preserve the old shareholders' equity at any cost; it is the lawful, evidenced course that best addresses the entity's condition and statutory responsibilities.**

## [04.16] Board-ready reporting, questions, warning indicators and annual sign-off

### [04.16.01] A proportionate monthly board financial pack

The first page should answer: **What changed, what matters, what is forecast, what could fail and what decision is required?** The remaining pages should make the conclusion auditable. A suggested layout follows; this is an original design rather than a reproduction of the AICD checklist. [AICD FIN pp. 14–22; FSH PDF pp. 5–8, 24; TOOL pp. 53–59, 108, 124.]

| Board-pack page | Critical content | Decision / escalation |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Exceptions and decisions | Profit, cash, liquidity, covenant and mission/strategic performance: change, cause, ownership | Approve action, defer choice or demand evidence |
| 2. Three-statement scorecard | Actual month and YTD vs budget, prior year and forecast; reconciled balance sheet | Identify unexplained reconciliations or presentation changes |
| 3. Revenue, cost and outcomes bridge | Price, volume, mix, programme outputs, margins, productivity | Challenge economics and quality of service |
| 4. Cash and working capital | CFO/NPAT bridge, cash conversion, receivables ageing, stock, payables, restricted cash | Alert if cash quality deteriorates |
| 5. Liquidity/treasury | 13-week dated cash, 12-month outlook, debt schedule, facilities, covenants | Trigger remedial plan before cash headroom breach |
| 6. Capital and investment | Committed/project capex, maintenance, value-at-stake, returns, alternatives | Accept/reject/rephase capital allocation |
| 7. Accounting judgments and assurance | Impairment, provisions, inventory, revenue cut-off, audit findings | Request independent technical review if necessary |
| 8. Risk and controls | Loss of funding, stress results, overdue audit actions, fraud exceptions | Assign accountable owner and target closure |
| 9. Legal-entity and purpose funds | Entity cash, support/guarantees, grants, trust money and restrictions | Prevent inappropriate transfer or expenditure |

### [04.16.02] Set financial warning signals tied to board action

A dashboard threshold is useful only if someone must do something when it is crossed. Use a tiered response: routine analysis, CFO/treasurer escalation, chair/audit committee escalation, full-board intervention and specialist legal/insolvency advice. Signals may include: cash runway below a board-approved number of weeks; liquidity buffer falling below a defined seasonal low; forecasts showing a financing shortfall; a covenant forecast within a specified distance of limit; debtor days more than ten days over normal contracted terms; inventory older than its commercial life; multi-period CFO below NPAT with no convincing cause; unrestricted funds below committed expenditure; forecast programme underdelivery or clawback; late tax/superannuation; material unreconciled bank or intercompany differences; repeated audit actions overdue; and an impairment sensitivity leaving negligible headroom.

Do not invent universal cut-offs. For example, a company with daily retail receipts may operate at a different cash ratio from a small association paying rent quarterly. A long-cycle infrastructure developer has different DIO and ROIC timing from a services business. A grant-funded NFP should measure **unrestricted liquidity and obligations**, not merely total bank cash. Where the risk is critical, the trigger should refer to a concrete action: board approval to suspend discretionary expenditure, negotiations with lender, funding application, independent investigation or emergency meeting.

### [04.16.03] Financial review and the annual declaration are connected, not redundant

Before annual sign-off, directors should have seen management's monthly accounting trajectory, audit adjustments, major estimates, post-reporting-date events, known financial risks and a suitable solvency/going-concern forecast. A signed annual declaration is not an opportunity to begin learning where loans or customer deposits are recorded. Review whether the annual report's profit narrative agrees with segment performance and cash flows, whether key estimates agree with the approved forecast, and whether events after year-end have changed the risk. Reconcile regulatory disclosures, related-party transactions and material commitments. Consider the auditor's independence, modified matters and management-letter deficiencies, and obtain CEO/CFO representations appropriate for the entity.

The detailed statutory composition of financial reports, reporting exemptions, external-audit process, directors' declaration, consolidated entity disclosure statements, climate reporting, directors' report and historical 'Fifty Matters' sign-off guide belong in **File 05**. The relevant AICD sources are FIN pp. 23–49, TOOL pp. 47–52 and FIFTY pp. 4–12 (October 2016, historical). This file covers only what a financially competent director must understand and challenge before placing reasonable reliance on those sign-off processes. [FIN pp. 11–22, 23–44; IFS pp. 5–7; TOOL pp. 47–52.]

### [04.16.04] Questions to ask in the boardroom, and evidence worth receiving

1. **What changed in cash, not just earnings?** Request CFO bridge, deposits/restrictions and post-date receipts.
2. **Which balance-sheet asset is least realisable?** Request ageing, physical verification, CGU impairment assumptions and subsequent events.
3. **Which obligation becomes due first under a legal contract?** Request payment calendar, leases, tax and debt documents, not a generic ratio.
4. **How much credit capacity is actually committed and drawable?** Request bank letters, conditions precedent, security and covenant calculations.
5. **What assumptions must be true for the budget to fund itself?** Request three-way model and historical forecast accuracy.
6. **Which adjustment is improving the earnings narrative?** Request reported-to-adjusted bridge, tax treatment and cash consequences.
7. **Are maintenance, lifecycle liabilities and growth needs included?** Request project and asset-life schedules and independent technical validation.
8. **Which funds have purpose restrictions or belong to beneficiaries?** Request instrument-by-instrument funds ledger and permitted-use advice.
9. **Which part of the business or programme creates the greatest value or outcome for incremental capital?** Request marginal return or cost-per-outcome comparison.
10. **What would management tell us if there were no earnings or fundraising target?** Request adverse cases and independent challenge.
11. **What has changed since the reporting date?** Request latest debtor collections, lender correspondence, claims and service performance.
12. **If our cash forecast is wrong, who decides what stops and when?** Request escalation policy, authorisations, advisers and minute-ready decision alternatives.

**Meeting record discipline:** Minutes should capture material information considered, the issues challenged, alternative courses, professional advice obtained, conflicts declared, decisions made, the assumptions explicitly accepted, owners, deadlines and triggers for revisiting the decision. Minutes should reflect real deliberation, not supply a retrospective formula for diligence.

**Cash self-generation audit:** The FY2023 figure is **−$0.571m CFO −$7.499m PPE −$13.748m intangibles −$15.691m lease cash −$2.611m interest = −$40.120m**. For the seven months to January 2024, management's **−$14.081m operating cash already includes lease cash payments** according to the explicit source footnote; the subsequent self-generation calculation is **−14.081 −1.839 PPE −4.824 intangibles −1.133 interest = −$21.877m**, **without subtracting lease payments a second time**. The apparently inconsistent presentation is a different management cash-flow classification, not permission to double count. [FIN p. 125.]

**The corporate finance mechanism:** Thirty-six franchised stores were acquired during FY2021–January 2024 for a combined **$27.080m**, but many produced marginal or negative store-level EBITDA. Purchasing stores can bring retail revenue into group accounts while eliminating prior franchise revenue and adding wages, occupancy and operating costs. In the seven-month 2024 period, the 36 stores generated roughly $1m combined store-level EBITDA, with 23 either loss-making or marginal on the source definition. FY2023 recorded a **$17.785m impairment**, including about $10.5m of goodwill and $7.1m of right-of-use assets according to narrative approximations. FY2023 operating EBITDA was negative *before* this separately reported impairment; treating the impairment as wholly exceptional would not rehabilitate the trading model. [FIN pp. 118–122.]

**Financial and governance interpretation:** By FY2023 the company exhibited negative EBITDA, negative operating cash, rapidly deteriorating working capital, negative equity and escalating short-call borrowings, making the dependence on shareholder-associated secured funding material. The Jan 2024 financial evidence intensified these warnings. AICD describes support being withdrawn and administrators appointed on 30 January 2024. The source's later DOCA proposal, approved by creditors on **4 June 2024**, funded outstanding employee claims but proposed no return for other unsecured creditors; this is a historical example of divergent creditor outcomes, not proof of the legality or attractiveness of a generic DOCA in other cases. [FIN pp. 115–126.]

**Board questions a director could have raised earlier:** What is the acquisition price *after* foregone franchise margin, added corporate overhead and unavoidable closure liabilities? What returns were actually achieved on the $27.08m store purchase programme? At what point did negative self-generation cease to be temporary? What happens if the related financier stops renewing funds? How do the legal debt maturity and employee obligations affect the time available to restructure? Which stores would be economic to close, and is there enough cash to pay the closure costs? These questions separate growth in store count from creation of value and improvement in solvency.

**Check the balance-sheet identity:** 2024 total assets $151.528m − total liabilities $97.159m = **$54.369m equity**. The equity change from $51.365m is exactly **$3.004m**, consistent with $0.778m surplus plus $2.226m OCI. Current ratio = **48.174/41.397 =1.1637×**; prior year **27.227/15.297 =1.7799×**. Net working capital falls **$5.153m**. Gross borrowings/non-current assets, the AICD *teaching* geared-assets definition, equals **68.490/103.354 =66.27%** versus **11.096/50.996 =21.76%** in FY2023; this is not bank leverage under an agreed covenant. Gross borrowing/equity is **1.26×** in FY2024. [FAPP pp. 9, 11–12.]

**Cash-flow arithmetic:** $12.478m operating −$5.477m investing +$5.393m financing = **+$12.394m**, reconciling $3.746m opening to $16.140m closing cash. PPE purchases were only **$2.608m**, while purchases of financial assets were **$4.000m** and sales proceeds for financial assets and PPE totalled **$1.131m**: the **net investing outflow of $5.477m is not equivalent to gross PPE capex or to all newly purchased long-lived productive assets**. CFO less PPE purchases is **$9.870m**, before lease principal, financial asset purchases and other noncash considerations. CFO/surplus = **12.478/0.778 =16.04×**, a ratio strongly influenced by timing and accounting; it is not a distributable cash measure. The AICD commentary identifies approximately **$17.3m** government grants, around **30.25%** of 2024 total recognised income of $57.195m, but this grant category comes from narrative reference to note 3.1, not a separate note included in the extracts. [FAPP pp. 8, 10, 12–14.]

**Three consequential unresolved questions:**

1. Reported gross borrowings rise **$57.394m**, while cash-flow borrowings of **$7.396m** less cash repayments of **$0.977m** equal only **$6.419m net cash borrowing**. The **$50.975m difference** requires a proper debt reconciliation, potentially involving noncash financing or acquisitions, reclassification or a reporting presentation issue. FAPP's narrative says SLSQ obtained an aircraft construction facility, but the extract does **not** supply an adequate reconciliation. Do not invent one. The unusual increase in PPE from $45.550m to $96.509m alongside only $2.608m cash PPE purchases reinforces the request for the full asset/financing notes. [FAPP pp. 9–12.]
2. Current deferred revenue grows from **$5.385m to $14.250m**, an increase of **$8.865m**; the teaching material associates this with grants received ahead of delivery. It does not establish that the entire corresponding cash is legally restricted, nor what portion of cash and financial investments is truly unrestricted. An unrestricted liquidity ratio or reserves cover **cannot be responsibly calculated** from these abbreviated statements. Request grant contract schedules and the restricted-funds ledger. [FAPP pp. 9, 11, 13.]
3. The AICD narrative describes the continuing surplus as falling **32.8%**, which is correct from $1.158m (2023 continuing) to $0.778m (2024). Against **total FY2023 surplus $1.230m** including $0.072m discontinued activity, however, the decline is **36.75%**. Both comparisons can be stated, but only when the denominator is specified. Its categorical conclusion that SLSQ could pay debts as due is **not independently proven by the abbreviated dated statements alone**. A board needs the contractual debt and funding schedule, eligible unrestricted cash and forecast. [FAPP pp. 8, 11, 13.]

**Board insight:** A surplus-making, service-delivering NFP can materially expand its asset and borrowing footprint while shrinking working-capital cover. Positive operating cash may partly reflect grants received in advance. Good governance asks whether aircraft/other long-lived investment is mission-effective, properly financed, and maintainable, and whether the organisation has cash legally available to pay its own obligations. The FY2024 numbers cannot establish either a legal insolvency event or unrestricted financial resilience without further evidence.

### [04.17.05] Comparison: why the four cases lead to different decisions

| Case and date | Accounting performance | Liquidity/cash issue | Financing/economic issue | Strongest evidence-based board request |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nick Scali FY2024 | Positive NPAT but down 20.25%; gross margin improved | CFO $131.18m; cash/term deposits up, large lease payments | Lease-inclusive leverage and funded UK expansion | Reconcile post-lease cash narrative, UK return on invested capital and covenant definitions |
| Discount Depot fictional FY2024 | NPAT +20.09%, gross margin −1.86pp | CFO −45.34%; dividends exceed basic FCF | $12m borrowings now current | Debt maturity, other current assets and cash conversion bridge |
| Godfreys FY2023–Jan 2024 | Negative EBITDA and deep losses | Severe worsening current ratio and cash burn | Debt and store acquisition programme funded ongoing deficits | Urgent legally dated solvency forecast and independent turnaround/appointment advice |
| SLSQ FY2024 | Small $0.78m surplus, large OCI and asset expansion | CFO $12.48m, falling NWC, restricted amounts unknown | $57.39m debt increase poorly reconciled to financing cash flows in extract | Full aircraft/debt notes and unrestricted funds/liability calendar |

**Synthesis:** The financial statements identify patterns and questions; they do not automatically provide a board resolution. Nick Scali illustrates the need to account for lease and growth cash commitments even alongside profits. Discount Depot demonstrates why profit growth must be reconciled to cash and refinancing. Godfreys shows how deteriorating unit economics and acquisitions can overwhelm financing support. SLSQ illustrates that a service organisation can have increased cash and assets while its restricted funding, liability timing and noncash financing arrangements remain critical. None of the four should be used as a template for current investment advice or as a source of universal solvency thresholds.

### D. Ten compact retrieval conclusions

1. **Profit is not liquidity:** EBITDA and NPAT ignore material uses and timing of cash; cash-flow forecasts are indispensable.
2. **Liquidity is not legal solvency:** the operative question is whether every debt can be paid when contractually due, using genuinely available resources.
3. **Book equity is not enterprise value:** investments and accounting judgments can move equity without proportionate market value or cash.
4. **Ratio definitions are part of the result:** leases, debt, cash restrictions, averaging and periods must align before comparing.
5. **Working capital reflects a business model:** negative working capital may be sustainable or a warning; the cash cycle and legal payment terms decide.
6. **A clean audit cannot promise survival:** it provides assurance over specified historical reporting, subject to its scope and opinion.
7. **A good acquisition needs post-transaction returns:** growth in stores, assets, revenue or EBITDA is not itself capital productivity.
8. **Unrestricted money matters in NFPs:** neither restricted grants nor third-party/trust funds can be treated as a general cash buffer without legal authority.
9. **Early distress action preserves choices:** preserve records, examine dated cash and financing, seek specialist advice and document the decision before further debts are incurred.
10. **Director judgment integrates the system:** accounting statements, cash, legal duties, mission outcomes, economic returns, risk appetite and management quality all matter to the board's decision.
