## Reading and source conventions

This chapter separates five kinds of propositions:

- **[LAW]** Binding statutory, constitutional, regulatory or contractual duties, carefully limited to the relevant entity and circumstances. Statutory provisions and regulatory interpretation are not interchangeable.
- **[REG]** Official guidance or applicable listed-entity governance recommendations. ASX Corporate Governance Council recommendations are generally subject to the ASX 'if not, why not' disclosure framework, rather than all being freestanding statutory commands.
- **[CUR]** AICD 2025/26 teaching, including frameworks, illustrative questions and historical case material. It is a dated educational source, not automatically current law or conclusive behavioural science.
- **[RESEARCH]** Attributed empirical or theoretical organisational-behaviour research. Findings must be interpreted in light of methods, population and transferability to boards.
- **[ANALYSIS]** Original synthesis, practical control design, diagnostic prompts and decision tests in this chapter. These are proposals, not AICD quotations or approved legal/cultural protocols.

Citations such as `AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 33-42]` refer to the **original printed page labels**, not PDF viewer offsets. Source aliases are defined in Appendix A. Web sources carry their original organisation, title, publication year when established, official URL and verification date. GSH is a two-slides-per-PDF-page handout; it is cited by PDF page and, where relevant, displayed slide number. Text from sources is paraphrased rather than reproduced as proprietary teaching material.

**Three lenses recur throughout.** **Listed** means a large Australian Securities Exchange (ASX)-listed company with a professional executive team. **Community** means a small/medium charity, company limited by guarantee or Western Australian (WA) incorporated association, potentially relying mainly on volunteers. **Indigenous corporation** means a corporation under the *Corporations (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander) Act 2006* (CATSI Act), including a registered native title body corporate (RNTBC) where applicable. These categories can overlap: a CATSI corporation or WA association may also be a charity registered with the Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission (ACNC). The relevant law, constitution/rule book and specific rights must be checked, not assumed.

A **board effectiveness** finding asks not merely whether procedures exist but whether the board's particular people, processes, information and decisions serve its entity's purpose, obligations and long-term outcomes. A result can be favourable by luck despite a weak decision process, or adverse despite a reasonable, diligent process. The quality of judgement therefore requires both forward-looking process review and retrospective outcome review.

## 02.01 What makes a board effective: conformance, performance, purpose, trust, accountability and outcomes

### 02.01.1 Definition and causal model

**Board effectiveness** is the board's capacity, over time, to fulfil its legal and constitutional responsibilities, choose and oversee a viable direction, appoint and hold leadership accountable, protect people and resources, and enable the organisation to achieve its legitimate purpose. **Conformance** concerns legal compliance, risk oversight, control, reporting and accountability. **Performance** concerns strategy, organisational capability, adaptation, value creation and mission achievement. Both are necessary, but neither alone is sufficient. A formally compliant board can approve a damaging strategy; a commercially successful board can tolerate misconduct or fail to protect beneficiaries. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 9-12]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 35-41, 52-55].

A useful original causal chain is: **purpose and authority -> board composition and information -> interaction and challenge -> quality of choices -> management execution -> results, learning and adaptation**. Risk culture and ethical norms influence every step. This is not a deterministic equation. External markets, funding decisions, technology, unforeseen events and chance also affect results. A board should therefore evaluate (1) whether it did the right governance work, (2) how competently it did that work, and (3) what happened thereafter, rather than infer performance solely from profits, surpluses or share prices.

**Purpose** is the reason the entity exists and the set of objectives it is authorised to pursue. For a company it informs strategy within legal duties; for a charity it includes charitable purposes and responsible stewardship; for an RNTBC it also intersects with managing or holding native title rights and interests under applicable law. Purpose limits what counts as success. Revenue growth that degrades beneficiary outcomes, or a major contract secured without properly authorised native title decision-making, may not constitute organisational success. The board's role is not to maximise every stakeholder claim simultaneously but to navigate duties, rights, trade-offs and legitimate expectations transparently.

**Trust** in a board context is an expectation that colleagues will act competently and in good faith, share relevant information and not exploit candour. Trust enables frank disagreement; it does not remove independent verification. **Accountability** means identifiable duties, delegated authorities, reporting obligations and corrective consequences. **Outcomes** include financial resilience, mission delivery, safety, ethical conduct, risk exposures, stakeholder legitimacy, succession and adaptability. Jeffrey Sonnenfeld's 2002 analysis of boards emphasises that formal arrangements are not enough without a candid, high-functioning social system. That is an influential governance interpretation, not proof that structural controls are dispensable. [RESEARCH] Sonnenfeld, *What Makes Great Boards Great*, *Harvard Business Review*, September 2002, https://hbr.org/2002/09/what-makes-great-boards-great; [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 9-12].

### 02.01.2 Effectiveness is a portfolio of obligations, not a single score

The board must allocate scarce meeting time. Routine compliance is necessary but should not crowd out strategy or major emerging risks. Equally, a board celebrating its strategic vision while failing to read the cash-flow forecast, investigate safeguarding incidents or identify unresolved consent requirements is not effective. The allocation changes with organisational maturity, financial stress, crisis and the availability of competent management. A useful board-review method is to classify agenda time as strategic direction, performance monitoring, compliance/assurance, people/culture and stakeholder legitimacy, and ask whether that mix reflects today's exposure rather than last year's habits. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 40-41, 53-55, 64-65]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 94-103].

**Illustration [ANALYSIS].** A board may improve its decisions by moving from 70% routine reporting to 40% routine reporting, 30% strategic choices, 20% risk/assurance deep dives and 10% capability and culture. These percentages are hypothetical, not AICD targets. During a liquidity crisis, cash, solvency and continuity may legitimately dominate. What matters is a documented, revisited rationale for the allocation.

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** effectiveness includes transparent market reporting, executive accountability, capital allocation and resilience across a complex group; use independently assured information where material. **Community:** the central test may be reliable delivery to beneficiaries and financial sustainability with minimal administration; avoid judging volunteers by listed-company process volume. **Indigenous corporation:** formal compliance is necessary but effectiveness may also require members' confidence, cultural legitimacy, decision rights of common law holders and durable community benefit, which cannot be inferred from financial statements alone. [REG] ASX fourth edition, Principles 1-4 and 7; ACNC Governance Standards; AIGI *Effective Indigenous governance* (sources in Appendix A).

**Not-for-profit governance benchmark.** AICD's *Not-for-Profit Governance Principles* (third edition, April 2024) organise governance into eight linked topics: purpose, vision and strategy; roles and responsibilities; board composition and effectiveness; risk management; performance and accountability; stakeholders; sustainability; and organisational culture. The 2025/26 governance slides also present this set. It is a **voluntary contextual framework**, not a replacement for the ACNC Governance Standards, WA association law or CATSI law. An unpaid board may address all eight through proportionate processes rather than eight separate committees or policies. [CUR] AICD[GSH, 2025/26 V1, PDF p. 8]; [REG/GOOD PRACTICE] AICD, *2024 Not-for-Profit Governance Principles: Third Edition*, https://www.aicd.com.au/corporate-governance-sectors/not-for-profit/studies/2024-not-for-profit-governance-principles.html.

**Director questions:** Which major outcomes would fail if this board became passive for one year? What did our last three meetings change for the better? What adverse information reached us late? Where are purpose, safety, financial capacity and legitimacy pulling in different directions? Which conclusions are based on evidence, rather than the confidence of their presenters?

## 02.02 AICD Corporate Governance Framework and Tricker's board models, properly attributed and compared

### 02.02.1 The AICD framework: four domains of governance practice

The **Company Directors' Corporate Governance Framework(TM)**, created and owned by AICD, is a structured map of governance practices across four domains. It is not a statute and should not be described as Tricker's model. The **individual** domain covers the director's role, duties, competence, leadership, behaviour and chairing. The **board** domain concerns the collective role, committees, meetings, dynamics, competencies and legal standards. The **organisational** domain includes governance arrangements, culture, policy and assurance, management relations, strategy, risk and outcomes. The **stakeholder** domain concerns engagement with members, reporting, stakeholder relationships, society and community. The published wheel places these domains within wider values and conduct concepts, including integrity, transparency, accountability and respect. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 39-40, 55]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 129-130].

Its value lies in exposing blind spots. A board may have a technically excellent audit committee yet neglect stakeholder relationships; individual directors may be diligent yet have dysfunctional board meetings; or the board may function congenially while overlooking organisational safety. The framework is best applied as a **coverage and development map**. For each domain, ask: What is expected? Who owns it? What evidence shows it operates? What requires development? It is not a numerical performance certification, and the graphic should not be copied into this corpus.

### 02.02.2 Tricker's model: two roles, two orientations

Professor **Robert I. (Bob) Tricker** developed an analytical view of board activities based on the tension between **performance** and **conformance**, with an inward/outward orientation. In the 1994 framework cited by AICD, the four familiar functions are **strategy formulation** (outward and future-oriented performance), **policy making** (internal performance direction), **monitoring and supervising** (internal conformance) and **accountability** (outward-facing conformance). The outer and inner functions overlap in real decisions. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 40-41], citing R. Tricker, *International Corporate Governance: Text, Readings and Cases* (1994), p. 149; AICD[GSH, 2025/26 V1, displayed slide 27, PDF p. 14].

| Framework | Central question | Analytical unit | What it reveals | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AICD Corporate Governance Framework | Are individual, board, organisational and stakeholder practices connected and adequate? | Domains of governance responsibility | Missing capabilities, responsibilities and relationships | Whether the board has proportioned its scarce attention optimally |
| Tricker's board-activities model | Is the board balancing strategic performance and conformance, internally and externally? | Functions and allocation of board attention | Agenda imbalance and confusion between oversight and strategy | The quality of individuals, diversity, ethics or actual decisions |

The models should be combined rather than merged: use AICD's domains to identify *what* governance must cover, and Tricker's categories to examine *where the board allocates its attention*. Neither model licenses management to withhold information or directors to abdicate mandatory oversight. Neither proves that half of meeting time should be spent in each quadrant. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.02.3 Practical mapping

For an annual review, classify each planned agenda item by Tricker function and note the relevant AICD domain. A CEO succession review, for example, touches individual leadership, board processes, management relationships and future performance. An incident escalation review concerns monitoring, accountability and organisational culture, but may also challenge strategic assumptions. If a board has a strong formal audit architecture yet recurring unanswered issues, the discrepancy may be in management information flow or board culture, not committee structure.

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** a committee-heavy structure can cover AICD domains but obscure full-board integration; use Tricker to test strategy time. **Community:** one committee may cover most domains; the two models can be used as a one-page annual work plan without creating unnecessary charters. **Indigenous corporation:** retain these as external analytical aids, then ask whether the categories fit the corporation's own community-authorised governance arrangements. A Tricker quadrant never overrides the CATSI rule book or the native title consent regime.

**Director questions:** Which AICD domain has the weakest evidence? Which Tricker function dominates discussion? Are we confusing discussion of operational results with long-term strategic choice? Would members and affected rights-holders recognise our definition of accountability?

## 02.03 Board-management boundary and chair, CEO, executive and non-executive relationships

### 02.03.1 Collective authority and delegated management

A board normally acts through properly authorised collective decisions; individual directors do not usually command staff simply because they are directors. The board sets direction, reserves material decisions, appoints and supervises the CEO, determines risk appetite and evaluates organisational performance. The CEO and management organise resources, develop detailed plans, implement strategy and administer operations within delegations. The boundary is functional, not an absolute separation of knowledge or responsibility. Directors need independent understanding of operations, and a board may temporarily engage more closely during crisis while maintaining clarity about who is directing staff. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 52-55, 62-66]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 6-8, 50-52]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 36-38].

**Decision rights** should be written into the constitution/rule book where required, the board charter, CEO contract, delegations schedule and committee charters. Reserved matters might include major acquisitions, significant capital expenditure, material borrowing, CEO appointment, major changes to strategy, approval of budgets, risk appetite and critical policies. Thresholds should reflect the entity's scale and resilience, not copied from another company's schedule. Delegations should specify the power, delegate, monetary or risk limits, conditions, reporting and revocation. In a crisis, the board may tighten authorities, increase reporting or establish a temporary crisis governance arrangement, documenting the rationale and return-to-normal triggers.

**Legal distinction [LAW].** Corporations Act companies operate under the *Corporations Act 2001 (Cth)* and their governing documents. WA incorporated associations operate under the *Associations Incorporation Act 2015 (WA)* and rules. CATSI corporations operate under the CATSI Act and rule book; RNTBCs have additional native title obligations. A listed-company board charter is recommended under ASX Recommendation 1.1. These legal regimes are not interchangeable. [REG] ASX fourth edition, Recommendation 1.1; WA Consumer Protection, *Committee and officer duties*; ORIC, *Rule books* (Appendix A).

### 02.03.2 The chair is a process leader, not a substitute CEO

The **chair** leads the board's work: agenda quality, participation, procedural fairness, time management, director performance, connection among committees and the board, and relationships with the CEO and company secretary. A chair should typically canvass other views before expressing a strong personal preference, particularly where status could anchor the discussion. Chair leadership combines genuine authority with restraint. The chair cannot alone confer authority that belongs to the board or become the only channel for significant bad news. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 49-51]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 65-66].

A constructive **chair-CEO relationship** combines trust with boundaries. The chair should help the CEO understand board priorities, provide feedback and make difficult messages easier to raise. The CEO should not conceal incipient trouble to protect the relationship. Directors should have agreed protocols for accessing management and outside expertise without undermining executive accountability. In-camera sessions can enable candid non-executive discussion, but the chair must promptly relay operationally relevant conclusions to the CEO. When the board has lost confidence in the CEO, its duty is to confront the issue through an agreed evaluation and succession process, not to manage by informal instructions and factions. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 65-66]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 7-8, 50-52].

### 02.03.3 Preventing information capture

A functioning boundary requires **constructive challenge without parallel management**. Appropriate questions concern assumptions, controls, capacity, evidence, consequences and escalation. Inappropriate behaviour includes assigning tasks to employees outside approved channels, repeatedly substituting a director's operational preference for the authorised executive's judgement, and making promises to stakeholders without authority. Equally dangerous is an executive team that presents decisions as effectively complete before directors can evaluate alternatives. [ANALYSIS]

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** extensive professional management requires clear reservations, board committees and reliable assurance, not direct supervision of every division. **Community:** board members may also volunteer operationally; declare which 'hat' is being worn, who supervises volunteer work and when a conflict arises. **Indigenous corporation:** directors may hold respected community roles, but cultural standing and corporate delegation need not be identical. The rule book, member decisions and any native title holder consent requirements determine formal authority; the board-CEO boundary should be intelligible in community languages or preferred communication formats as authorised locally.

**Director questions:** Could any staff member name the person authorised to direct them? Does the CEO bring adverse information before options narrow? Which decisions have drifted beyond their delegation? Has the chair become too closely identified with management's preferred answer?

## 02.04 Individual director effectiveness, competence, ethics, independence of mind and development

### 02.04.1 The director mindset

**Individual director effectiveness** is the sustained ability to understand the entity, exercise independent informed judgement, behave ethically, work with others and meet the role's legal and practical demands. Competence combines basic governance and financial literacy, entity-specific knowledge, sound questions, ethical judgement and sufficient time. Independence of mind is a behavioural capability: a formally independent director can be intellectually captured by a dominant chair, while a director who is not classified as independent may still ask rigorous questions. Formal independence, conflicts and statutory duties remain separately relevant. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 13-21, 29-32]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 25-27, 56-59].

**Integrity** concerns the director's use of authority in the entity's interests rather than personal advantage. **Competence** means being capable of analysing the relevant issue, knowing the limits of one's knowledge and seeking assistance when necessary. **Judgement** requires correctly weighting evidence and consequences without pretending uncertainty has disappeared. **Vision** translates purpose and future direction into meaningful questions and choices. The curriculum discusses these four leadership qualities through Robert Hogan's research, but they are a useful descriptive lens rather than a statutory test or a validated ranking of directors. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 15-17], citing Hogan and Kaiser (2005).

Directors should be able to explain the entity's business model or mission delivery model; major cost, revenue and resource drivers; risks and risk controls; cash runway; relevant stakeholders; and the circumstances that might make its current strategy untenable. They must interrogate rather than merely receive expert opinions. **Reliance on expertise is not the same as delegation of personal responsibility.** ASIC's Information Sheet 183 emphasises directors' active involvement in guiding and monitoring the company; the required standard is not limited by an individual's professional specialism. [REG] ASIC, *Directors and financial reporting*, INFO 183, https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/financial-reporting-and-audit/directors/directors-and-financial-reporting; [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 21-34].

### 02.04.2 Self-awareness, emotional competence and reflective practice

A director's influence depends partly on tone, listening, empathy, impulse control and how others experience their behaviour. The curriculum uses personality traits, the five-factor ('Big Five') description, emotional intelligence, experiential reflection and reflexivity to explain why talented individuals may contribute differently in a group. **Self-reflection** looks back at behaviour and outcomes; **reflexivity** also questions the assumptions, position, social relationships and incentives through which the director interprets events. A director who can acknowledge having anchored on an early forecast may become more useful than one who never admits doubt. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 14-22, 29-32].

The Big Five dimensions provide a descriptive research tradition, not a governance qualification. Emotional-intelligence instruments differ in what they measure, and studies of personality or leadership are not equivalent to clinical diagnosis. The curriculum's discussion of Paul MacLean's three-part brain is best treated as a historical explanatory metaphor, **not** a literal or adequate contemporary neuroscientific model for board behaviour. Psychometric feedback, when used, should be voluntary or appropriately governed, interpreted by qualified practitioners where relevant, privacy-protective and linked to observable work behaviours rather than identity labels. AICD's risk-personality and 'convincer' exercises similarly support discussion of preferences; they should not become predictive hiring rules. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 15-22]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 128, 131-132].

### 02.04.3 Individual development plan

An original development cycle is: **baseline -> gap -> deliberate practice -> feedback -> evidence of improvement**. A director might choose one technical gap (e.g. cash-flow interpretation), one strategic gap (e.g. sector regulation), and one relational gap (e.g. asking incisive questions without dominating). The plan should identify concrete activities: reading source materials, site visits, stakeholder conversations through appropriate channels, specialist briefings, mentoring or facilitating a board debrief. Review performance through meeting observations and the director's actual contribution, not training hours alone. [ANALYSIS]

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** test independent understanding of complex group reporting and market-sensitive issues; professional development may have an established budget. **Community:** minimum financial, child-safety, safeguarding and regulatory literacy may be more valuable than specialised corporate-finance credentials; avoid burdening unpaid directors with elaborate testing. **Indigenous corporation:** combine general director competencies with local governance knowledge, cultural legitimacy, language accessibility and community-authorised professional development; expert advisers should build local capability rather than displace it.

**Director questions:** What do I understand only because management says it is true? When did I last change my view after hearing a colleague? Are my questions proportionate to the decision's materiality? What would others say I contribute or suppress in debate? How am I building successors rather than protecting my own position?

## 02.05 Intuition, judgement, heuristics, cognitive biases, social psychology, authority and status

### 02.05.1 Intuition has conditions of validity

**Intuition** is rapid judgement based on patterns or associations that may not be consciously articulated. It can be valuable in stable environments where a practitioner has received repeated, timely feedback. It is less reliable for rare, irreversible corporate decisions when the environment is changing and true counterfactuals cannot be observed. Kahneman and Klein's 2009 synthesis concluded that intuitive expertise should be assessed by the predictability of the setting and the decision-maker's opportunity to learn its regularities, not by subjective certainty. This is particularly relevant when a successful executive moves from frequent operating decisions to rare acquisitions, regulatory crises or long-horizon strategy. [RESEARCH] Kahneman and Klein, *Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree*, *American Psychologist* 64(6), 2009, pp. 515-526, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755; [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-25].

**Heuristics** are mental shortcuts that economise on attention. Tversky and Kahneman's 1974 account described availability, representativeness and anchoring/adjustment as recurring forms of judgement under uncertainty. They are not inherently irrational. A shortcut that improves speed in familiar settings can mislead when the evidence base changes. The appropriate response is to design verification and diversity into the decision rather than demand that directors never use intuition. [RESEARCH] Tversky and Kahneman, *Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases*, *Science* 185, 1974, pp. 1124-1131, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124; [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 25-29].

### 02.05.2 Bias catalogue as hypotheses, not personal diagnoses

| Bias or social mechanism | How it may arise at a board | A proportionate countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| Anchoring | Early project cost or expected return becomes the implicit benchmark even when better evidence arrives. | Require independently built ranges and a reset of assumptions. |
| Confirmation/selection bias | Management and directors privilege evidence supporting a favoured plan. | Ask for disconfirming evidence, failed comparators and an alternative paper. |
| Availability | A vivid recent incident drives excessive focus while a quieter recurring exposure is ignored. | Compare base rates, actual exposure and loss severity. |
| Overconfidence and optimism | Expertise in one setting is assumed to transfer to a novel decision; downside is underweighted. | Use outside-view comparisons, sensitivity tests and calibration of forecasts. |
| Status quo/sunk-cost reasoning | The board continues funding to avoid acknowledging past error. | Separate unrecoverable past expenditure from prospective costs and benefits. |
| Framing/loss aversion | A proposal looks different when described as 'avoiding losses' rather than 'creating value'. | Re-express alternatives in comparable net terms, including the do-nothing case. |
| Authority/status pressure | A respected chair, CEO or expert speaks first and suppresses contrary evidence. | Obtain independent initial judgements and hear dissent before the chair's preference. |
| Social identity/in-group preference | A cohesive board discounts information from unfamiliar directors or stakeholders. | Rotate discussion leadership and seek credible outside perspectives. |
| Diffusion of responsibility | Each director assumes a committee or specialist has verified the difficult issue. | Name assurance owners and require every director to test matters central to their duties. |

These are **possible mechanisms**, not assertions about any director or board. A dissenting director may be correct, mistaken or disruptive; a supportive director may have independently reached the same conclusion. Evidence of actual behaviour, not mere alignment, is required before attributing bias. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 26-29, 43-48, 64-65, 81-82].

### 02.05.3 Status, group identity and the ethics of confidence

Senior rank creates information asymmetry and social pressure. A director may feel reluctant to challenge a CEO who appears expert or a chair who controls the agenda. Conversely, a highly respected director can unintentionally make dissent costly without ever explicitly demanding agreement. AICD discusses the risk that organisational identification, moral confidence and good intentions impede principled challenge. A good chair asks, 'Whose informed disagreement have we not heard?' rather than interpreting quietness as consent. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 26-29, 64-65, 81-82].

**Original bias-control protocol [ANALYSIS].** For a high-impact decision: (1) record the initial independent assessment of the issue and alternatives before the presentation; (2) demand an explicit reference class, including unsuccessful comparators; (3) appoint a credible critic with access to the data; (4) separate facts, assumptions and estimates; (5) ask whether the recommendation changes if the first number heard is ignored; (6) state what evidence would reverse the preferred conclusion; and (7) record uncertainty and monitoring triggers. Excessive process can itself slow decisions, so scale this protocol to irreversibility and risk.

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** authority bias often centres on a powerful CEO, sector expert or investment committee; independent valuations and assurance can challenge it. **Community:** founder charisma, donor prominence, long-serving volunteers or a dominant treasurer can exert equal influence even without formal seniority. **Indigenous corporation:** a director must respect legitimate community authority while distinguishing the role of cultural knowledge from legal decision rights and professional expertise; do not treat culturally respectful deference as proof of psychological bias.

**Director questions:** Which assumptions became fixed before this meeting? What would convince us to stop? Is the strongest objection being heard in its strongest form? Are we accepting a confident forecast because we trust the forecaster or because the track record and evidence are sound?

## 02.06 The board as a real team: shared purpose, mutual accountability, role clarity and collective performance

### 02.06.1 The paradox of accomplished individuals and weak collective performance

A **real board team** is a bounded group whose members share a consequential purpose, depend on one another's contributions, follow legitimate decision rules and accept collective responsibility for the work of governance. A board's members may each have impressive careers yet collectively miss a major threat. Individual experience does not automatically become group intelligence. It must be integrated through information sharing, interpretation, debate, decisions and follow-through. Conversely, excessive agreement can make meetings pleasant while preventing the board from discovering that a strategy is failing. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 33-34, 37-42].

The board is legally a collective decision-making organ in the relevant corporate context, but each director also has individual duties and an obligation to exercise independent judgement. **Collective accountability** therefore does not mean a director may defer responsibility to the majority, nor does it authorise a faction to behave as a separate board. The chair can guide how the board works but cannot make every director's contribution for them. The constructive ambition is a group that can challenge hard, decide clearly and then supervise implementation coherently. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 4, 33-34, 39-41]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 52-54].

### 02.06.2 Hackman's enabling conditions, translated into governance practice

J. Richard Hackman's team-effectiveness work provides five *enabling conditions*, adapted to the boardroom in the AICD material. They should be understood as conditions that improve the prospect of effective collective work, not as a certification standard or a promise that all outcomes will be successful. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 33-34], attributing Hackman (2004).

| Enabling condition | What a board should establish | Observable failure mode | Diagnostic evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| A real team | Clear membership, roles, decision rights and stable enough working relationships | A collection of individuals or competing factions | Charter; attendance; conflicts record; decision follow-through |
| Compelling direction | A meaningful organisational purpose and agreed board priorities | Discussions driven by personal preferences, politics or ceremonial routines | Strategy, mission measures, agenda allocation and decision log |
| Enabling structure | Appropriate board size, skills, committees, protocols and candour norms | Expertise that cannot be heard or decisions that cannot be implemented | Skills matrix; agenda and pack review; committee mandates |
| Supportive environment | Adequate time, information, assurance, secretariat and management access | Late packs, inaccessible specialists, untested assertions | Pack-delivery record, information requests, director interviews |
| Expert coaching | Development, peer feedback and appropriately independent facilitation | Repeated unproductive behaviour despite reviews | Debriefs, evaluation findings, training and improvement records |

The first condition concerns **who the team is**; the second, **why it exists**; the third, **how its work is designed**; the fourth, **what resources it has**; and the fifth, **how it learns**. A skills matrix alone deals mainly with team structure. It cannot replace psychologically safe debate, appropriate decision powers or the willingness to address poor performance. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.06.3 Lencioni's five dysfunctions as a reflective model

Patrick Lencioni's *Five Dysfunctions of a Team* (2002), discussed in the course, describes a possible progression from low trust to fear of productive conflict, weak commitment, avoidance of mutual accountability and inattention to collective results. As presented here, this is a **leadership and discussion framework**, not a clinically validated diagnostic instrument for declaring a particular board dysfunctional. The useful question is what concrete behaviours would support or falsify each concern. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 37-42].

**Low trust** may look like directors concealing errors or overstating expertise. **Conflict avoidance** may look like difficult questions being raised only after meetings, when they cannot alter the recommendation. **Weak commitment** may mean no one can restate the decision and its conditions. **Avoidance of accountability** arises when a persistently unprepared or aggressive director is tolerated to preserve relationships. **Loss of results focus** occurs when winning arguments or preserving board reputation eclipses the organisation's actual needs. Each pattern has alternative explanations: confidentiality can be legitimate, a quiet director may be carefully weighing evidence and a delayed decision may be prudent when information is genuinely insufficient. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 38-42]; [ANALYSIS].

**A practical 'team contract' [ANALYSIS]** could include five commitments: disclose conflicts and information gaps; separate criticism of ideas from attacks on people; let every director be heard before closing major matters; clearly record what has been decided and delegated; and revisit whether agreed actions occurred. The board should agree what happens after a serious breach. Rules without enforcement are less useful than a few consistent behaviours.

### 02.06.4 Board-management is an interdependent governance system

The AICD material also encourages thinking about how the board and management work together as a wider governance team. The two groups share an organisational objective but must **not collapse their separate authority**. Directors need management's operating information and expertise; management needs board clarity, judgement and legitimate approval. Reciprocal respect enables candour, while formal delegations prevent blurred accountability. A board that asks for endless extra reports can consume the same management capacity it wants applied to organisational performance. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 33-34]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 52-55, 68-70].

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** the 'team' includes specialised committees and senior management relationships but decision authority remains with the board or its duly authorised delegates; test whether committee expertise actually reaches plenary debate. **Community:** board and volunteers may perform overlapping work, increasing intimacy but also interpersonal disputes; define responsibility clearly and avoid relying on informal agreement alone. **Indigenous corporation:** the board is one governance body among potentially relevant member, family, traditional-owner and native title holder processes; a cohesive board cannot claim legitimacy for decisions legally or culturally requiring a broader authorised process.

**Director questions:** What information did we only discover because someone felt safe to disagree? Which member's expertise is unused? Are actions tracked to closure? Do we spend more energy protecting relationships than challenging important issues? Can management accurately paraphrase our collective direction?

## 02.07 Board composition, diversity, independence, stakeholder representation, skills matrix and succession

### 02.07.1 Composition is a forward-looking portfolio decision

**Board composition** is the selection and continual renewal of the collective skills, judgement, experience, integrity, perspectives and stakeholder knowledge needed to govern the organisation's future risks and opportunities. It is a portfolio question, not a competition to collect prestigious individual résumés. A board fit for a stable domestic organisation may lack capability after international expansion, digital transformation, financial distress or a change in mission. Selection should follow a documented assessment of the entity's purpose, future strategic choices, risks, operating model and existing capabilities. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 25-30, 56-59]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 14-15, 81-88].

The number of directors should allow useful diversity, adequate committee capacity and efficient discussion. AICD governance notes describe common board-size ranges, but there is **no universally optimal number**: a nine-member board without relevant expertise can be worse than a well-functioning five-member board. Complexity, legal requirements, constitution/rule book, financing, member representation and committee demands govern the choice. Smaller boards may act quickly yet lack succession depth; larger boards may pool perspectives yet incur coordination costs and passive participation. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 56-59]; AICD[GAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 17-19].

### 02.07.2 A board skills matrix should anticipate decisions, not merely count credentials

The AICD guide for a board skills matrix provides a way to identify capability gaps and guide succession. An independently useful matrix should distinguish **literacy** (can understand and question), **working capability** (can analyse and supervise) and **deep specialist expertise** (can lead sophisticated analysis). The same person may have deep experience in finance but limited knowledge of the specific entity's financing or social mission. Self-ratings need evidence and challenge: occupation, tenure or prior title is not proof of relevant competence. [CUR] AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 81-88].

| Capability dimension | Evidence to test | Forward question |
|---|---|---|
| Financial literacy and sustainability | Understanding cash, solvency, funding and audit | Who can challenge the next financing decision? |
| Strategy and sector knowledge | Competitors, business/mission model, public policy | Who understands how external change could invalidate the model? |
| Risk, safety and compliance | Material exposure and critical controls | Who can scrutinise the principal non-financial risks? |
| Digital, data and cyber | Oversight of technology investment and vulnerabilities | Who can interrogate digital dependence without managing IT? |
| Workforce, culture and people | Incentives, industrial relations, leadership | Who could identify a defective speak-up or reward system? |
| Stakeholder and community legitimacy | Beneficiary, customer, community or traditional-owner context | Whose knowledge and standing are absent from the process? |
| Board and governance judgement | Proven director conduct, ethical challenge, legal literacy | Who can integrate expert advice into a sound board decision? |

This is an **original illustrative matrix architecture**, not a copy of AICD's rating template. A matrix is useful only if it changes recruitment, induction, committee composition or use of independent advisers. Beware a board comprising a different specialist for every topic: all directors must still understand core duties and the collective consequences. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.07.3 Diversity of perspective, approach and social experience

The curriculum draws on Juliet Bourke's *Which Two Heads Are Better Than One?* (second edition, 2021) to distinguish **diversity of perspective**, which broadens what people notice and how they define a problem, from **diversity of approach**, which broadens methods for solving it. Six illustrative orientations ask first about outcomes, alternatives, process, evidence, people or risk. Every competent director can use all six; the taxonomy is a reflection aid, not a fixed psychometric classification. Consider how a board could technically analyse an acquisition yet overlook the experience of employees who would deliver it, or extensively discuss stakeholder concerns without testing cash requirements. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 43-48], attributing Bourke (2021).

Demographic diversity and lived experience may broaden the questions that get asked. But **representation, perspective and authority are different**. One director cannot be assumed to speak for all people of a particular gender, culture, profession or community. Nor should directors be reduced to identity categories. The empirical link between a particular demographic mix and financial performance depends on context, governance design, selection and inclusion; avoid simplistic claims that diversity mechanically increases profits. The practical aim is a board that both accesses relevant perspectives and uses them. [ANALYSIS]

**ASX governance benchmark [REG].** Fourth-edition ASX Corporate Governance Council Recommendations 1.5, 2.1-2.6 cover diversity policy and objectives, nomination arrangements, disclosed skills matrix, independence, chair/CEO separation and induction/development. Recommendation 2.4 favours a majority of independent directors, and 2.5 an independent chair who is not CEO. These are recommendations within the 'if not, why not' disclosure architecture, subject to the entity's actual listing requirements; they are not automatic statutory prescriptions for every Australian board. Source: ASX Corporate Governance Council, *Corporate Governance Principles and Recommendations*, fourth edition (February 2019), Principles 1-2, https://www.asx.com.au/content/dam/asx/about/cgc-principles-and-recommendations-fourth-edn.pdf; [CUR] AICD[GAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 1-4]. As at 27 October 2026, the proposed fifth edition was a consultation draft, not adopted operative guidance.

### 02.07.4 Independence, nominee status and stakeholder representation

**Formal independence** involves assessment of interests, relationships, tenure and associations that may impair or reasonably appear to impair independent judgement. **Independence of mind** refers to what directors actually do in debate. Both matter, but neither permits a director to disregard legal duties or known conflicts. A nominee director may bring investor or stakeholder knowledge; nomination does not necessarily create an unrestricted right to advocate for the nominating party against the company's interests. Whether the director may participate in a specific decision must be considered under applicable duties, conflicts rules and the governing document. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 18-20, 55-59]; [REG] ASX Recommendation 2.3.

A board should distinguish three possible pathways for stakeholder involvement: **formal director appointment** where legally and constitutionally authorised; **structured advisory and engagement channels** that improve information without changing board membership; and **legally required external decision-making or consent**. The third cannot be replaced by appointing a sympathetic director. For example, RNTBC director/member structures and rights of affected common law native title holders are not interchangeable. [REG] ORIC, *Decision-making in RNTBCs*, https://www.oric.gov.au/for-corporations/decision-making/decision-making-RNTBCs; [ANALYSIS].

### 02.07.5 Succession and renewal as resilience

An effective board maintains an updated **capability-and-succession map** covering likely retirements, key committees, chair continuity, CEO transition and new strategic exposures. Renewal should preserve institutional knowledge without becoming a mechanism for entrenchment. The board should consider whether multiple directors are likely to leave simultaneously, whether highly specialised knowledge has no successor, and whether members or appointing parties can support the needed changes. Tenure limits may assist renewal but are not a substitute for assessing actual contribution. AICD's board-composition checklist and skills-matrix guide can support this process. [CUR] AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 14-15, 81-88]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 56-59].

**NRGworks teaching prompt.** The governance handout also asks participants to modify a skills matrix for the teaching organisation NRGworks and discuss board-wide capability gaps. The displayed prompt is accessible, but the fuller exercise materials are not included among the specified primary PDFs. Its learning function is already captured in the forward-looking skills matrix above; a reconstructed case narrative would be unsupported. [CUR] AICD[GSH, 2025/26 V1, PDF pp. 22-23].

**Costa Group teaching prompt.** The governance slides use *Costa Group Holdings* to explore when a growing organisation should revisit board composition and what new capabilities growth demands. The supplied GSH slides present prompts alongside a case-video segment, not a sufficiently complete primary historical record to assert precisely what Costa's board did or why. The valid lesson is to link board renewal to organisational complexity rather than wait for failure. A full, independently sourced exercise belongs in [File 08](08_Directors_Toolkit_Cases_and_Practical_Applications.md). [CUR] AICD[GSH, 2025/26 V1, PDF pp. 9-10, displayed slides 17-19].

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** use formal nomination processes, public matrix disclosure, succession pipelines, independence assessment and external searches where warranted. **Community:** funder or founder relationships, volunteer scarcity and member election rules constrain recruitment; basic financial, safeguarding and mission expertise may outrank a glossy matrix. **Indigenous corporation:** evaluate skills and community-authorised representation together; recognise language, kinship and relevant traditional-owner knowledge where locally important, but do not invent eligibility or customary selection rules. Check the CATSI rule book, membership arrangements and whether RNTBC decisions require participation of non-member common law holders.

**Director questions:** Which skills would this board need after the strategy changes, not before? Which capabilities exist only on paper? Who is systematically missing from our consultation? Can we replace the chair or a key committee specialist without interruption? Are nominated directors free to exercise independent judgement and manage conflicts?

## 02.08 Inclusive leadership, psychological safety, culturally responsive communication and participation

### 02.08.1 Inclusion is a board process, not a declaration

**Inclusive leadership** creates conditions in which relevant knowledge and dissent can influence the group, including when contributed by people who are less senior, newer, linguistically different, remote or outside the dominant professional network. Inclusion should be judged by **effective participation and impact on consideration**, not just by the diversity categories in the annual report. A director can be invited to speak and still be excluded if the decision was settled before the conversation or the director's evidence is not taken seriously. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 43-59].

The chair has special leverage: set expectations, intervene on interruptions, clarify terms, invite different perspectives, examine who has remained silent, and avoid announcing a personal verdict before hearing others. It is also reasonable to give directors ways to contribute by advance written comment, one-to-one clarification through approved governance channels and targeted technical briefings. Participation must not be confused with unanimity. People can be heard fully while the board ultimately decides against their preference. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 49-51].

### 02.08.2 Psychological safety, candour and responsibility

**Psychological safety**, associated with Amy Edmondson, is a shared expectation that speaking up with a question, concern, error or dissent does not automatically bring interpersonal humiliation or punishment. It enables learning and error reporting but does not excuse bullying, repeated negligence or refusal to accept accountability. It differs from **comfort**: a psychologically safe discussion can be strenuous, emotionally difficult and sharply adversarial on ideas. Boardroom safety means being able to say 'we have not tested that assertion' without retaliation, not being entitled to have one's assertion accepted. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 35-36]; [RESEARCH] Edmondson (1999), *Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams*, *Administrative Science Quarterly* 44(2), pp. 350-383, https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999.

The academic evidence comes largely from workplace-team settings, not randomly assigned corporate boards. It provides plausible mechanisms and useful observations; it does not supply an independently validated boardroom threshold. A board should test behaviours directly: whether late-emerging bad news reaches it; whether junior experts can contradict a senior executive; whether a director can admit confusion; and whether people reporting a problem are treated fairly. A jump in speak-up reports may reflect worsening conduct, improving trust in channels, changing awareness or all three. Numbers require careful denominators and investigation, not simplistic interpretation. [RESEARCH] Edmondson (1999); [ANALYSIS].

### 02.08.3 Communication styles and accessibility

The curriculum contrasts passive, aggressive, passive-aggressive and assertive communication, and discusses practical speaking and listening techniques, constructive feedback and Edward de Bono's **Six Thinking Hats** as a structured way of deliberately approaching the same issue from different perspectives. These are facilitation concepts, not identity labels or validated predictors of directorship. A 'six-hats' discussion may use distinct rounds for facts, concerns, benefits, alternatives, feelings and process; it should not become a substitute for legal compliance or actual evidence. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 53-63].

**Culturally responsive communication** starts from understanding actual participants' communication needs and authorised protocols. Do not assume silence is agreement, that direct eye contact has a single meaning, or that consensus is universally preferred by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander participants. Relevant differences may include language, age, formal authority, disability access, meeting location, time required for consultation and the role of people who are not corporation members. Ask participants or their authorised representatives how information should be presented and who must be included. Provide translation, interpretation, visual explanation or longer deliberation when needed, preserving the required record and legal decision process. AICD itself cautions that practices vary among First Nations communities. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, p. 59]; [REG] Australian Indigenous Governance Institute (AIGI), *Effective Indigenous governance*, https://aigi.org.au/toolkit/effective-indigenous-governance.

### 02.08.4 Feedback, facilitation and asymmetry of power

**Constructive feedback** concerns a specific observed behaviour, its effect on board work, and an actionable request; it does not attribute motives without evidence. For example: 'The paper was introduced as already approved. That prevented examination of alternatives. Please bring material choices before commitment.' A peer who repeatedly interrupts can be asked to let the speaker finish, rather than labelled inherently dominant or aggressive. The AICD materials caution against mechanical feedback rituals, including reflexive praise-criticism-praise sequences that may obscure the actual issue. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 60-63].

A chair can normalise informed challenge by asking each director initially for the strongest uncertainty, obtaining contributions before management's defence, and inviting dissent to be restated accurately. An executive must also be allowed to explain and answer; a culture of interrogation without listening encourages defensive reporting. In-camera board discussion is useful for sensitive evaluation, but it must not become an alternative, undocumented decision forum that excludes people legally entitled to participate. [ANALYSIS]

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** status differences between chair, CEO, international directors, specialist committees and staff can silence experts; anonymous employee channels and independent assurance may provide a second line of sight. **Community:** volunteer relationships can make disagreement personally costly; the chair may need explicit norms to separate friendship from fiduciary judgement and to protect beneficiaries who speak up. **Indigenous corporation:** language access, meeting formats, gender or community-specific arrangements, consultation timing and cultural authority must be established locally; no universal 'cultural practice' can be imposed from this research document.

**Director questions:** Who spoke last and least at our last important meeting? Did contrary evidence change the analysis or merely appear in the minutes? Could the most junior person with relevant information correct us without fear? Have we asked participants what communication format allows them to contribute properly?

## 02.09 Constructive challenge, dissent, conflict, groupthink, uncomfortable debate and poor-information flow

### 02.09.1 Distinguish useful tension from destructive conflict

**Constructive challenge** is rigorous examination of reasoning, assumptions, choices and consequences while respecting individuals and legitimate authority. The AICD distinction between productive **tension** and escalating personal **conflict** is useful: disagreement over the merits of a capital project is necessary; threats, bullying and sustained personal attacks reduce the reliability of information and discussion. Conversely, a polite meeting can conceal substantial unresolved disagreement. An effective chair works to surface tension early and contain damaging behaviour without suppressing dissent. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 35-40, 53-55, 64-70]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, p. 97].

The curriculum draws on the **zone of uncomfortable debate** idea associated with board-dynamics research by Andrew Kakabadse and colleagues. The useful principle is that good governance cannot always feel socially easy. Where discomfort reflects important contrary evidence, suppressing it sacrifices judgement. Yet 'robust debate' is not a licence for aggression. The test is whether challenge produces better shared understanding, defensible choices and clear direction. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 35-36, 53-55]; [ANALYSIS].

### 02.09.2 Groupthink and the boundary between theory and evidence

**Groupthink** is Irving Janis's model of how a cohesive group, under some conditions, may prioritise agreement and identity over examination of alternatives. Possible signals include self-censorship, stereotyping outsiders, apparent unanimity when objections are untested, and pressure on people who question the preferred view. It is one explanation for some collective errors, **not** a reliable diagnosis whenever a board reaches consensus or makes a poor decision. Cohesion and agreement can be consistent with thorough independent analysis; weak evidence and bad incentives can also cause failures without groupthink. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 64-65], citing Janis; [ANALYSIS].

A better audit asks for behaviour and information flows. Was the option set narrowed before board scrutiny? Did the meeting record a credible rejection rationale? Was a material expert concern escalated? Did anyone know adverse facts that failed to reach directors? Were dissenters sanctioned or merely disagreed with on the evidence? These tests can be investigated without speculating about directors' psychological traits. [ANALYSIS]

**Historical regulatory example.** In its **2018** Prudential Inquiry into Commonwealth Bank of Australia, the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) identified serious governance, risk and culture weaknesses, including complacency, insufficient challenge and non-financial risk oversight shortcomings. It provides a regulator-supported example of why seemingly successful organisations require sceptical information flow and clear non-financial accountability. It is not evidence that those findings necessarily describe the institution in October 2026, nor proof that any particular contemporary board suffers from groupthink. [REG] APRA, *Prudential Inquiry into the Commonwealth Bank of Australia (CBA): Final Report* (April 2018), https://www.apra.gov.au/sites/default/files/CBA-Prudential-Inquiry_Final-Report_30042018.pdf; [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 64-66, 87-88].

### 02.09.3 Decision paralysis, information clogs and performative discussion

The course draws on Ram Charan's account of organisational indecision and discusses dysfunctional discussion patterns: **dangling dialogue** (issues raised but not resolved), fragmented or **piecemeal perspectives** (each person sees only a fraction of the problem), and **information clogs** (too much low-value material obstructs insight). Other practical failures include the false impression that a committee has settled a matter the full board has not reviewed, and repeatedly reopening a resolved issue without new evidence. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 66-68].

These problems arise for different reasons and require different remedies. Information overload calls for issue-led papers and a small set of material options, not less disclosure of serious risks. Repeated indecision calls for clearer decision rights, deadlines and explicit requests for missing evidence, not arbitrary speed. Fragmented perspectives call for joint framing before functional presentations. Unresolved dissent may call for documented conditions or independent review. An effective chair distinguishes **constructive delay** from **avoidance**. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.09.4 Uncomfortable debate protocol and handling a 'difficult' director

AICD's *Managing uncomfortable debate* tool (TOOL p. 97) can be translated into an original protocol: identify the substantive disagreement; restate each position accurately; specify evidence that could change it; test consequences against purpose and law; then decide, defer with an information request, or escalate through a legitimate process. Avoid private negotiation that bypasses the board where the issue must be collectively decided. If discussion becomes personal, interrupt and return to the question. [CUR] AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, p. 97]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 69-70].

The director viewed as 'difficult' may be the only person noticing a real problem, may have an ineffective delivery style, or both. Separate **content** from **conduct**. A chair should not suppress legitimate substantive concerns in the name of collegiality; equally, directors are entitled to be protected from bullying. Formal performance feedback, an agreed code of conduct, skilled facilitation, conflict management and, in severe cases, legal advice on removal/eligibility may be needed. Removal powers differ by entity, constitutional rules and appointing authority; a chair does not simply 'dismiss' another director. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 69-70]; [LAW] relevant entity law and rules.

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** watch for collective deference to a highly successful CEO, an investment thesis or a specialised committee; seek independent assurance and board access to relevant experts. **Community:** uncomfortable debate may concern funder influence, related-party contracts or volunteer conduct; set conflict rules in advance and use independent chairs or advisers for particularly personal matters. **Indigenous corporation:** disputes may have corporate, member, native title and community dimensions; a formal vote cannot resolve a consultation obligation or relational legitimacy concern by assertion. Use locally authorised conflict-resolution pathways where appropriate, while observing legal duties and recording valid decisions.

**Director questions:** What is the strongest argument against the current proposal? Is 'consensus' real or merely silence? Which significant issue has been repeatedly deferred without a named next step? What relevant warning might management hesitate to bring us? Who can correct the chair?

## 02.10 Decision quality: decision rights, alternatives, evidence, ethics, framing and structured process

### 02.10.1 Distinguish a good outcome from a good decision

**Decision quality** is the robustness of the decision process given the knowledge and options reasonably available **when the decision was made**, rather than the favourability of the eventual outcome. An attractive return can arise despite unjustified risks; a reasonable proposal can fail because of external shocks. Boards need both an *ex-ante* test of process and an *ex-post* review of what was learned. This is especially important for infrequent decisions with uncertain outcomes, where a single result does not validate a forecasting method. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 72-80, 89-94]; [ANALYSIS].

Every material board decision should be framed by a **decision statement**: the specific action, authorised decision-maker, practical alternatives, deadline, intended organisational outcome, material obligations, downside exposure, and conditions for implementation and later reconsideration. Confusing a request to *discuss* with authority to *approve* increases both operational ambiguity and legal risk. A paper should say clearly whether the item is for decision, discussion or noting. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 62-71]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 34-38, 121-123].

### 02.10.2 Define the decision rights before analysing the options

**Decision rights** identify who may propose, recommend, scrutinise, authorise, execute and monitor an action. Board-reserved matters and management delegations are different; committee recommendations may not be final approvals unless a valid delegation allows it. For some decisions, shareholders, members, regulators or holders of particular rights must authorise or consent. The threshold may be a budget limit, legal requirement, risk profile or special matter under governing documents. Document the legal source and approval route before asking whether the business case is attractive. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 52-55, 75-78]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 36-38]; [LAW] governing statute, constitution/rule book and applicable regulation.

**RNTBC distinction.** Ordinary CATSI corporation operating resolutions are not automatically the correct authority for native title decisions. For certain actions relating to native title rights and interests or compensation, the RNTBC must consult and obtain consent from the **affected common law holders**, including those who are not corporate members, under the relevant native title laws and regulations. A board's commercial enthusiasm or even unanimous director vote cannot replace that process. [REG] Office of the Registrar of Indigenous Corporations (ORIC), *Decision-making in RNTBCs*, https://www.oric.gov.au/for-corporations/decision-making/decision-making-RNTBCs.

### 02.10.3 Stages of a sound board decision

The AICD *Decision stages framework* and *Processes to aid decision making* tools encourage disciplined problem framing, criteria, alternatives, evaluation, choice and review. An original board adaptation below adds explicit authority and post-decision assurance. These stages may recur rather than follow a rigid linear order. [CUR] AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 34-35, 100-101]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 72-80].

| Stage | Essential director test | Minimum record for material decisions |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Authority and purpose | Is this our decision, and what authorised outcome matters? | Decision rights, purpose, required consent and constraints |
| 2. Problem definition | What problem is being solved, and what happens if we do nothing? | Problem statement, baseline and counterfactual |
| 3. Criteria and risk | What constitutes success and what must not be breached? | Evaluation criteria, critical risks, ethical/legal limits |
| 4. Options | What are credible alternatives, including stop, stage or defer? | Options and rationale for excluding others |
| 5. Evidence and challenge | Which facts are verified, assumed or estimated? | Source quality, dissent, sensitivity, independent review |
| 6. Choice and conditions | Is the preferred option proportionate and implementable? | Resolution, rationale, conditions and delegated authority |
| 7. Monitoring and learning | What changes our view and when will we review? | Action owner, indicators, triggers and review date |

Do not mistake a long options table for true alternatives. A board paper comparing three minor versions of the same acquisition may omit the strategically important option of improving the current business or preserving cash. Likewise, an apparently cheaper operating decision may externalise cost to beneficiaries, staff or community relationships. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.10.4 Evidence quality, ethics and the board's obligation to challenge

**Evidence** should be distinguished from expert opinion, market expectation, management estimate, stakeholder preference and unresolved claim. Financial models need traceable assumptions and comparative economics. Culture reporting needs qualitative and quantitative sources, including contrary evidence. Safety decisions need relevant operational and legal expertise. A reputable outside adviser improves information but cannot transfer the board's ultimate accountability. Directors should request specialist advice when warranted and remain capable of understanding the central question. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 72-87]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 68-70].

The AICD curriculum addresses ethics as an active decision discipline rather than treating legal permission as full moral justification. It discusses the Ethics Centre's approach in terms of **framing the issue, shaping options, evaluating choices, refining judgements and acting**. The point is to identify affected people, competing values, foreseeable harms and the difference between a defensible course and a merely profitable or permissible one. An ethics review can conclude that the board should refuse an otherwise lawful option, but must still respect statutory duties and decision rights. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 81-87], including the Ethics Centre model.

**Ethical question set [ANALYSIS].** What would be objectionable even if no law expressly prohibits it? Who benefits, who bears the downside and who cannot leave the relationship? Would we defend the decision and the assumptions publicly? Does the action contradict stated values, charitable purpose or community-authorised obligations? Are we confusing an influential stakeholder's preference with a legal right to direct the decision? Who can remedy harm if the decision turns out poorly?

### 02.10.5 Decision techniques: matched to the problem, not used as theatre

Decision trees and probability ranges help where outcomes and contingent choices can be specified. A weighted criteria grid makes competing objectives visible but cannot turn subjective weights into objective truth. Cost-benefit analysis and financial modelling help compare resources and trade-offs but can miss unpriced dignity, safety or legitimacy. *Five Whys* can reveal a process failure but may oversimplify interacting causes. Force-field analysis can clarify implementation barriers. Sensitivity analysis reveals fragile assumptions; a premortem asks participants to imagine failure and explain how it occurred. Six Thinking Hats structures perspective-taking but does not establish the reliability of the underlying evidence. Select tools for the decision's uncertainty and consequence, document their limitations and avoid scoring theatre. [CUR] AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 34-35, 100-101]; [ANALYSIS].

### 02.10.6 Worked micro-example: an unfunded expansion

A charity board is asked to approve a two-year service expansion because a donor is likely to provide additional funding. The paper assumes the donation but lacks a binding commitment. A competent director reframes the problem from 'Should we expand?' to 'Which scale and timing preserve services if the new funding does not arrive?' The board could test a conditional rollout, a capped pilot, or deferral; distinguish donated restricted funds from unrestricted cash; seek evidence of demand and beneficiary outcomes; and set minimum cash and safeguarding conditions. An approval might authorise a pilot only when documented funding and staffing gates are satisfied, with management to report before any larger commitment. This original illustration is not a curriculum case or statement of charity law. [ANALYSIS]

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** value, capital allocation, continuous disclosure, regulatory exposures and delegated financial authority often dominate decision design; quantify financial and non-financial downside. **Community:** funds may be restricted, volunteers scarce and safeguarding non-negotiable; simpler papers can still compare alternatives rigorously. **Indigenous corporation:** establish whether the choice is a corporate commercial matter or a native title decision requiring common law holder consultation and consent; financial benefit alone does not grant authority.

**Director questions:** Who is legally entitled to decide? What is the genuine no-action alternative? Have we distinguished facts, forecasts and wishes? What was the dissenting case? Which ethical constraint would rule out a financially attractive option? What would cause us to reverse or stop the commitment?

## 02.11 Complex and uncertain decisions: scenario thinking, minority views, reversibility and review triggers

### 02.11.1 The distinction between technical problems and adaptive challenges

A **technical problem** has a reasonably definable solution that suitable expertise, data and established methods can address. An **adaptive challenge** requires people to reconsider competing values, established roles, incentives or established ways of operating; the problem itself may be disputed. Ronald Heifetz's leadership distinction is used in the AICD course to show why boards need different approaches to different situations. An accounting treatment may require specialist technical analysis. Whether to abandon a long-established business model or change a service mission despite community resistance requires technical evidence **and** adaptive leadership. Many real decisions combine both. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 75-77], attributing Heifetz and Laurie (2001).

The distinction is not an excuse to disregard specialist advice. Instead, it guides the questions: *What is knowable by technical analysis? Which aspects are negotiations among affected values or interests? Whose behaviour must change?* A director who treats adaptive tensions as a spreadsheet error risks false certainty. A director who treats technical evidence as a matter of feelings risks avoidable error. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.11.2 Complexity requires a different decision posture

**Complex decisions** feature interacting causes, uncertain responses, incomplete evidence, multiple legitimate interests or consequences that change the situation itself. They may also be subject to time pressure. The board should distinguish a **known risk** with usable probability evidence from **deep uncertainty**, where reliable probabilities may not be available. In the latter case, a single weighted-average forecast can be misleading, and the sensible strategy may be resilience across several plausible futures rather than optimisation for one. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 75-80]; [ANALYSIS].

The original practical method is to use three connected tests:

1. **Robustness:** Would the strategy remain viable across several distinct scenarios, including an adverse but plausible state? Do not assign precise probabilities without supporting evidence.
2. **Reversibility:** Which commitments can be stopped, staged, piloted or contracted conditionally? Which are irreversible because of cash expenditure, reputation, lost trust, safety or destruction of cultural heritage?
3. **Learning:** Which observed developments would justify increasing, pausing or reversing commitment, and who is empowered to act when a threshold is reached?

The 'best' option need not maximise the expected outcome in every forecast. A board may legitimately prefer a lower apparent return that preserves solvency, community legitimacy or strategic choices. But **reversibility is not the same as risk-free experimentation**: a short pilot can still harm a beneficiary, release confidential information or trigger a native title decision process. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.11.3 Scenario dialogue without pretending scenarios are forecasts

A **scenario** is a coherent description of a possible future and its causal conditions, used to test strategic assumptions. It is not an endorsement that the future is likely. Construct a few genuinely different states, such as severe funding loss, weaker-than-expected demand, cost escalation, new legal restrictions or workforce shortage. Compare each alternative for financial headroom, mission continuity, compliance and the capacity to adapt. Follow with explicit early indicators: unrestricted cash, client outcomes, contract renewals, absenteeism, control exceptions or regulatory events. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 76-80]; cross-reference [File 03](03_Strategy_Risk_and_Organisational_Resilience.md) for integrated strategy, risk appetite and resilience.

A useful decision record can state: *Approve a staged commitment, subject to two conditions. The risk committee will review the critical exposure each quarter; the board will revisit the decision if the leading indicator crosses the agreed threshold.* The level of review must reflect materiality; an over-engineered traffic-light system for routine purchases wastes board time. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.11.4 Protect minority information and define the decision to be made

A **minority view** is valuable because it may reveal a missing assumption, an overlooked stakeholder or a genuine legal obstacle. It is not necessarily correct. The chair should enable the minority position to be expressed in its strongest reasonable form, test evidence fairly and then bring the authorised body to a decision. Where dissent concerns legality, safety, an unaddressed conflict or required consent, voting for a commercial majority does not cure the underlying defect. Where dissent concerns uncertain commercial judgement, a defensible majority decision may proceed with the dissent recorded appropriately and without stigmatising the director. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 39-40, 69-70, 77-80]; [ANALYSIS].

**Indigenous-governance qualification.** The AICD discussion of First Nations consensus processes (BE pp. 79-80) references particular Indigenous scholarship and examples. It should **not** be reinterpreted as a universal statement that every Aboriginal corporation requires unanimity or follows one cultural protocol. The relevant rule book, specific law, affected holders' rights and community-authorised process must determine what is appropriate. Where a native title consent process legally applies, the decision cannot be converted into an ordinary corporate board majority vote. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 79-80]; [REG] ORIC, *Decision-making in RNTBCs*.

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** material project acquisitions and restructuring can be tested against scenario ranges, capital discipline and defined stop/continue decisions. **Community:** scenario tests should prioritise funding withdrawal, loss of volunteers, safeguarding, lease or grant dependence, and ability to honour ongoing commitments. **Indigenous corporation:** scenarios must include consequences for native title rights, members and affected common law holders where applicable, with consultation methods and cultural legitimacy established locally, not inferred by outsiders.

**Director questions:** What part of this is technical, what part adaptive? Is the proposal resilient if one key assumption is wrong? Can we stage the decision? Who has a veto or consent right? What evidence would trigger a new board decision rather than simply another progress report?

## 02.12 Board operating rhythm: annual calendar, agenda, meeting packs, director preparation, minutes, actions and escalation

### 02.12.1 The annual calendar is a risk-based governance work plan

An **annual board calendar** specifies when recurring statutory, constitutional and governance decisions, oversight reviews, committee work and development activities will occur. It should begin with known obligations and reporting deadlines, then reserve attention for strategy, capital or funding decisions, risk, culture, CEO performance, succession and evaluation. A calendar is not simply meeting dates. It determines whether directors have enough time for the work that matters and whether management has enough notice to prepare useful evidence. The AICD Director tool provides both candidate subjects and a monthly grid; neither is a universal frequency requirement. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 62-65]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 3-11].

An original operating rhythm has four complementary loops:

| Loop | Principal board question | Illustrative cadence, not a statutory rule |
|---|---|---|
| Every meeting | What changed, what must we decide, and what is beyond appetite? | At each scheduled meeting and when material events arise |
| Quarterly or periodic deep dive | Are principal risks, execution and non-financial leading indicators deteriorating? | As appropriate to exposure and management reporting capacity |
| Annual strategic and governance reset | Are purpose, strategy, risk appetite, CEO objectives and board capability aligned? | At least within the board's annual planning cycle |
| Event-triggered escalation | Has an incident or new fact invalidated a delegated decision or assurance? | Immediately or as required by law and severity; not delayed for the next calendar slot |

These are **illustrative governance practices** [ANALYSIS]. A small volunteer association might combine reviews into fewer meetings; a listed or prudentially regulated entity may require substantially more frequent controls, additional reports and time-critical disclosure.

### 02.12.2 The agenda is an attention-allocation device

A good **board agenda** identifies decisions, discussions and items for noting; orders important matters while directors are attentive; identifies conflicts and quorum; specifies presenters and intended time; and creates opportunities for in-camera discussion, emerging issues and review of outstanding actions. Prior minutes, audit findings and risk indicators should not disappear into 'noting' merely because they are recurring. The chair should invite directors' agenda suggestions, collaborate with the company secretary and CEO and adapt the timetable when new issues appear. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 64-66]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 24-26].

A board can review **agenda time** without turning it into a rigid productivity measure. If most meeting time is spent on historical operating information, while the CEO succession plan is always postponed, the process is likely misaligned. But unusually heavy compliance time can be justified by a crisis, litigation or significant financial reporting event. The diagnostic is the *opportunity cost* of attention, not the achievement of a prescribed allocation. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.12.3 Board papers should make choices legible

The AICD *Sample board paper* and governance notes emphasise relevant background, a clear recommendation, significant options, risks, financial consequences and a decision request. A **decision paper** should not read as a promotional investment pitch or bury its uncertainties in lengthy attachments. A practical structure is: decision sought; why now; facts and assumptions; viable options; cost and funding; risk and compliance; affected people and stakeholders; recommendation and alternative case; proposed resolution and monitoring conditions. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 68-70]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 121-123].

Separate **reported fact** from **management estimate**, **expert advice**, **scenario** and **unresolved matter**. Show historical comparisons and the original baseline; make principal exposures conspicuous. A short paper with a clear annex of supporting detail may work better than a huge pack. However, shortness cannot excuse concealing an adverse audit view or legal uncertainty. When a sensitive matter involves conflicts or litigation, obtain suitable advice on access, privilege, privacy, secure storage and procedural fairness. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.12.4 Preparation and information rights

A diligent director should read the pack, examine prior decisions, compare reported performance against strategy and identify material gaps before the meeting. A useful **preparation sheet** asks: what is the decision? what was last agreed? what evidence has changed? which assumptions are weak? what advice is missing? which conflict or stakeholder right may affect participation? what question would materially improve the decision? Directors should request information through the agreed chair, CEO or secretary process rather than privately directing employees. The AICD *Board meeting preparation: observer instructions* is relevant for identifying conduct, attention and information flow, although an observer cannot replace independent legal judgement. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 65-70]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, p. 29].

**In-camera sessions** of non-executive directors can discuss CEO performance, board dynamics or sensitive issues without management present. They can improve candour where properly used and handled in accordance with legal and constitutional requirements, but should not become an unrecorded alternative board. The chair should ensure the CEO receives appropriate feedback afterwards; blanket exclusion of management can harm implementation. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 65-66]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 6-8].

### 02.12.5 Minutes, decision records and actions

**Minutes** record the formal business of a meeting and support proof of how decisions were made. They should accurately record attendance, quorum, declared conflicts and how managed, resolutions, material conditions, actions and, where appropriate, significant reasons or dissent. They are **not** a verbatim transcript. The appropriate depth depends on context, sensitivity, legal obligations and the need to demonstrate informed deliberation. Do not routinely record privileged advice or confidential details without guidance on correct handling. The *Sample board minutes* director tool and GOV chapter provide models to adapt rather than forms whose contents guarantee statutory compliance. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 70-72]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 118-120].

**Law distinction [LAW].** For companies subject to the *Corporations Act 2001 (Cth)*, section 251A imposes particular obligations concerning minutes and recording them within **one month**. It does **not** follow that the identical section or timing automatically governs every WA incorporated association or CATSI corporation. The relevant statutory regime and governing instrument must be checked. Timely drafting and review are sensible practice, but a governance suggestion to circulate draft minutes quickly is not identical to the law's deadline. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 70-72]; [LAW] *Corporations Act 2001 (Cth)* s 251A, https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00818/latest/text.

A separate **action register** should show owner, deadline, evidence of completion and any escalation trigger. Mark an action complete when the underlying board requirement is satisfied, not merely when an email was sent. Resolutions and management delegations should be consistent with the minutes. For a particularly material decision, maintain a compact **decision record** that retains alternatives, key assumptions, dissent, evidence, conditions and review date. That improves later learning and reduces hindsight bias. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.12.6 Escalation and digital governance

**Escalation** is the pathway by which significant new information reaches the board or another authorised decision-maker fast enough to change the response. It should identify material safety events, cash distress, misconduct allegations, breaches of delegated limits, threatened loss of funding, failures of critical controls and events affecting disclosure or consent. Trigger definitions should include both numerical and qualitative thresholds, allowing judgement when an event is novel. Digital board portals and remote meetings improve access but create confidentiality, cybersecurity, device security and information-control issues. The board should know how urgent communications and valid resolutions are handled between meetings. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 72-74]; [ANALYSIS].

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** align calendar and packs with market reporting, assurance, material capital commitments and committee timetables; ensure urgent market-sensitive information is handled under applicable disclosure law. **Community:** a brief annual workplan, digestible cash forecasts and concise decisions may be more reliable than enterprise-scale board portals; retain formal minutes and incident escalation even when meetings are informal. **Indigenous corporation:** set meeting notifications, quorum, language and access consistent with the rule book and law; distinguish ordinary board business from community consultation and affected-holder consent processes. Digital tools must not exclude people without access or authority to participate.

**Director questions:** Does the agenda reflect actual risks and future decisions? Could I explain the paper's alternative in one sentence? Do the minutes match what we really decided? Which issues should reach us between meetings? Can we trace an important decision from proposal to approval, action and review?

## 02.13 Board committees, chairing, charters and proportionate structures for smaller organisations

### 02.13.1 Committees extend board capacity but do not create a second board

A **board committee** is a formally constituted group of directors, and sometimes appropriately authorised participants, tasked under a charter to examine or exercise specified functions. It can increase time, expertise and scrutiny devoted to a complex topic. It also creates risks of fragmented information, misplaced reliance and decisions drifting beyond authority. A committee may recommend to the board, or may have defined delegated power, according to law and the governing documents. **Committee formation does not remove the board's collective governance responsibilities or each director's applicable duties.** [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 75-78]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 6-8].

A committee is useful when the issue requires specialised attention, repeated oversight or independent discussion with auditors and other assurance providers. It may be unhelpful where the full board is already small, committee meetings duplicate discussion and directors cannot attend. The function is what matters: an audit function must be substantively performed even if the appropriate entity does not need or choose a separate committee. **Advisory groups** providing expertise without board authority must also be distinguished from committees that exercise formal board powers. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 75-78]; [ANALYSIS].

### 02.13.2 Core charter elements

The AICD collection includes sample **audit, risk, remuneration and sustainability committee charters**. Each is a starting template and must be adapted to purpose, legal form, materiality and board capacity. A robust charter should answer: who appoints members and chair? what expertise and independence are required? what is the committee's purpose? which matters may it decide, and which does it recommend? how does it access management, auditors, information and independent advice? what are quorum and conflicts rules? how will it report to the board? how are performance and charter scope reviewed? [CUR] AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 17-23, 109-117, 125-127].

| Committee or equivalent function | Board-level value | Key boundary or failure risk |
|---|---|---|
| Audit | Integrity of financial reporting, audit, control and independent challenge | Assuming the audit committee has discharged every other director's financial responsibilities |
| Risk | Deep review of major exposures, risk appetite, controls and resilience | Treating risk as an isolated register, detached from strategy and investment |
| Remuneration/people | CEO and executive rewards, succession, workforce incentives and conduct | Incentivising target achievement at the expense of risk, safety or values |
| Nomination/governance | Skills, succession, appointment, independence and board evaluation | Choosing familiar colleagues rather than needed capability or legitimate representation |
| Sustainability/mission | Oversight of material environmental, social, stakeholder or mission priorities | Creating a symbolic silo that leaves strategy and operational accountability untouched |

For listed entities the ASX fourth edition provides specific recommendations concerning nomination, audit, risk and remuneration committee composition and disclosure. For example, Recommendation 4.1 sets out a preferred audit committee structure or requires disclosure of alternative verification arrangements under the recommendations framework. That should not be presented as the universal lawful minimum for a small WA charity or CATSI corporation. [REG] ASX fourth edition, Recommendations 2.1, 4.1, 7.1 and 8.1; [CUR] AICD[GAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 3-9].

### 02.13.3 Reports that bring insight back to the full board

A useful committee report should identify: significant conclusions, alternative positions, unresolved evidence, conditions, decisions taken within authority, matters referred to the board, and implementation or assurance work outstanding. It should not simply state 'the committee considered the matter'. Where the full board must approve, it needs sufficient primary understanding to challenge the proposal. When disagreement emerges in committee, the report should not erase it for the sake of displaying alignment. [ANALYSIS]

The committee chair should manage participation, ensure the right professional advisers are heard and avoid treating a specialised portfolio as personal territory. Regular board-committee handoffs improve coordination. Audit and risk committees may require cross-attendance or joint reporting on financial controls, operational risk or major projects; nevertheless, overlapping functions must not create ambiguity about who follows up a risk. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 75-78]; [ANALYSIS].

### 02.13.4 A proportionate smaller-organisation design

For a small volunteer-governed organisation, sensible alternatives to multiple permanent committees include (a) a full-board agenda that allocates clear time to audit, risk, culture and succession; (b) one combined finance/audit/risk committee where legally appropriate; (c) a temporary working group with a fixed scope and expiry date; and (d) independent external review for a genuinely specialist matter. Document who reviews the financial controls and who is accountable for safeguarding even if no committee exists. A working group may research and recommend but should not unknowingly usurp the board's authority. [ANALYSIS]

For a CATSI corporation or RNTBC, committee design must reflect its rule book, member representation, statutory processes and local governance arrangements. A corporate committee cannot assume authority to conduct or certify a native title consent process merely because the committee is labelled 'community' or 'land'. Where affected common law holders have separate legal rights, those rights must be addressed through the applicable process. [REG] ORIC, *Rule books*; ORIC, *Decision-making in RNTBCs*.

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** committee independence, financial expertise, regulated reporting and board-charter coherence are material; avoid 'boards within boards'. **Community:** simplicity reduces volunteer workload, but do not dilute oversight of cash, safeguarding or grants. **Indigenous corporation:** combine functional oversight with the distinction between corporation decision-making, members, cultural processes and affected common law holders; seek locally authorised advice before changing representation arrangements.

**Director questions:** Which decisions can this committee legally make? Which information did the full board not see? Does the committee work add independent scrutiny or duplicate reporting? Who is responsible when audit, risk and culture overlap? Could the function be performed better with a simpler structure?

## 02.14 Organisational culture: values, incentives, risk behaviour, speak-up, misconduct, safety and culture metrics

### 02.14.1 Culture is what the organisation repeatedly does

**Organisational culture** is the shared pattern of assumptions, norms, incentives, decisions and observed behaviour through which work is actually carried out. A values statement describes the desired culture; it does not prove that the culture exists. Boards should look for what is rewarded, what is tolerated, whose concerns are heard, whether staff can stop unsafe work, and what happens when objectives conflict. The AICD governance curriculum connects culture to performance, risk and compliance, safety and quality, integrity, remuneration and stakeholder trust. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 89-99]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 100-103].

Culture is neither wholly designed from the top nor a phenomenon entirely outside board control. Boards influence it by choosing the CEO, articulating values, defining acceptable risks, setting incentives, allocating resources, supervising critical controls and modelling candour. Management translates direction into employment, purchasing, operating and reporting practices. Staff, contractors, volunteers and external stakeholders observe those practices and respond. The board's task is to test whether **espoused values** and **lived norms** are aligned, including in units distant from head office. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 89-99]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 68-80, 104].

### 02.14.2 The AICD three-part governance approach and four lenses

The AICD material on *Governing organisational culture*, attributed in the course to Jennifer MacCormick (2020), distinguishes three responsibilities: **set cultural expectations**, **align structures and incentives**, and **test lived reality**. Its associated director tool also examines culture through formal and informal dimensions: board processes and agenda; board reporting and decision methods; director behaviours in the boardroom; and direct observation of conduct outside the boardroom. These are complementary approaches, not one algorithmic score. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 100-102]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 68-80].

An original way to operationalise the model is:

| Culture question | Board-controlled lever | Independent or contrary evidence to request |
|---|---|---|
| What is expected? | Values, code, strategic objectives, risk appetite | Staff understanding; inconsistent informal instructions |
| What gets rewarded? | Executive performance objectives, incentive and promotion oversight | Payouts after misconduct, turnover in high-performing but unsafe teams |
| What is tolerated? | Incident escalation, investigations, consequence management | Repeat incidents, overdue corrective actions, reports of retaliation |
| What reaches the top? | Whistleblowing architecture, assurance, site exposure | Issues discovered externally before internal escalation |
| What improves? | Corrective action, learning and management accountability | Recurrence of control failures, quality outcomes and stakeholder experience |

No single measure establishes culture. A survey may show broad employee satisfaction while a high-risk team suppresses errors. A high volume of complaints may indicate increased harm, improved willingness to report, increased awareness of channels or a combination. Comparisons require credible definitions, stable denominators and the ability to examine qualitative detail safely. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.14.3 Performance and risk culture: incentives have causal consequences

**Performance culture** is the set of behaviours that pursues valid objectives while respecting values and constraints. It is not simply high targets. An organisation may meet revenue goals by underinvesting in maintenance, pressuring customers, overlooking safeguarding or relying on unrealistic volunteer contributions. A board should test both **what** leaders achieve and **how** they achieve it. Incentives that reward near-term volume while ignoring losses, control breaches or community consequences can promote conduct inconsistent with risk appetite and purpose. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 91-92, 95-97]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 68-80].

**Risk culture** describes how people understand, discuss, accept, manage and escalate exposures in everyday decisions. A documented risk appetite statement is useful only when embedded in operational delegations, business cases, reward systems, risk reporting and training. The board should question whether management can report deteriorating performance without career penalty and whether reported risks reflect observations rather than a quarterly 'tick-box' exercise. Avoid treating all risk-taking as cultural failure: an effective organisation must take **authorised and understood risks** to achieve purpose. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 92-93]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 104-105]; cross-reference [File 03](03_Strategy_Risk_and_Organisational_Resilience.md).

### 02.14.4 Speak-up and integrity: reporting is only the beginning

A **speak-up system** allows relevant concerns to be raised, assessed, investigated and corrected without improper retaliation. This includes confidential channels where appropriate, trained investigators, clear escalation and procedural fairness to people accused of wrongdoing. The board should receive aggregated information and direct escalation of serious matters according to policy and applicable law, while protecting privacy and preventing individuals from being unfairly prejudged. A mechanism that encourages reporting but leaves cases unresolved may reduce trust. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 92, 94-95, 97-99]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 68-80, 104].

**Integrity** is demonstrated when an organisation applies principles even where doing so is inconvenient, commercially costly or uncomfortable for senior leaders. A code of conduct is necessary but insufficient. Examine actual decisions on conflicts, related-party dealings, gifts, whistleblower reports, procurement and senior-leader disciplinary outcomes. The board's treatment of adverse findings sends a stronger signal than policy language. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 94-95].

**Legal/regulatory distinction.** ASX fourth-edition Recommendations 3.1-3.4 address values, codes, whistleblower and anti-bribery policies, with board visibility of material breaches or reports. They are part of listed governance recommendations, not the only possible legal obligations. The Australian Human Rights Commission (AHRC) explains the **positive duty** under the *Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth)* for relevant duty holders to take reasonable and proportionate measures to eliminate specified discriminatory and harmful conduct so far as possible. Workplace health and safety duties also require entity-specific attention. These obligations should not be treated as satisfied merely by a board policy or annual training. [REG] ASX fourth edition, Principle 3; AHRC, *Positive duty*, https://humanrights.gov.au/our-work/sex-discrimination/positive-duty-sex-discrimination-act; [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 92-94]. Precise application depends on entity, employment arrangements and current law.

### 02.14.5 Safety, quality and the shadow side of high performance

A **safety and quality culture** is observable when people have authority and practical resources to prevent harm, report near misses, maintain controls and correct defects. High performance claims are less meaningful if they exclude injury, misconduct, poor service or the cost of remediation. Boards should require a coherent view of leading indicators (critical control verification, training and competence, corrective action closures, staffing and workload) as well as lagging outcomes (incidents, injuries, service failure and complaints). A low reported incident count is not necessarily proof of safety when reporting incentives are weak. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 92-94].

There is a particularly important distinction between **zero tolerance for unlawful or harmful conduct** as a leadership commitment and claiming that an organisation can guarantee zero incidents. The latter may be impossible and can perversely encourage underreporting. Effective oversight focuses on prevention capability, timely detection, procedural fairness, support for affected people and demonstrable correction. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.14.6 A culture dashboard that does not pretend to measure the unmeasurable

A defensible culture dashboard has multiple independent dimensions and clearly describes the inference limits. Consider the number and confidentiality of settlement or non-disclosure arrangements where available and legally appropriate; the raw dollar total alone cannot establish improper conduct. [CUR] AICD[GSH, 2025/26 V1, PDF p. 22]. Illustrative indicators include: staff or volunteer turnover, regretted departures, vacancy persistence, short-notice absence, reports and substantiated findings of misconduct, critical control testing, safety near misses, survey trust scores, grievance resolution times, repeat control findings, customer or beneficiary complaints, and post-incident remedial closure. Stratify by business unit and cohort where ethically, legally and statistically appropriate, rather than rely on an average hiding a serious pocket of risk. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 97-99]; [ANALYSIS].

The board should explicitly ask management for **contradictory evidence**: one example in which the stated values were costly to uphold, one in which an important concern was raised early, and one where an executive decision was changed following safety or ethical challenge. Site visits and interactions beyond the CEO can broaden the board's line of sight, provided access protocols and privacy are respected. Avoid directors conducting informal investigations or collecting identifiable staff complaints without an authorised safe channel. [CUR] AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 68-80, 104]; [ANALYSIS].

### 02.14.7 Historical organisational-culture teaching cases

The AICD 2025/26 materials refer to the **2018 CBA Prudential Inquiry**, the Australian financial-services Royal Commission, historical **HIH Insurance** governance failures, and examples involving casinos and professional-services misconduct. Their teaching value is the connection between incentives, weak challenge, information failure, leadership conduct and institutional reputation. They are not interchangeable examples and should not be reduced to a statement that 'bad culture caused everything'. A proper case analysis examines the factual finding, responsible parties, legal regime, time period, competing causes and what control actually failed. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 64-66, 81-82, 87-88]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 89-99]; AICD[GAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 26-31].

The governance slide handout also uses the **aged-care, disability and financial-services Royal Commissions** as historical prompts about institutional power imbalances, vulnerable stakeholders and transactional conduct. The relevant director lesson is to examine whether an apparently productive organisational practice imposes disproportionate harm or prevents affected people being heard. These slides are an educational synthesis, not independently verified findings about any specific present-day organisation. [CUR] AICD[GSH, 2025/26 V1, PDF pp. 19-21].

The **HIH** material concerns an earlier corporate collapse and failures associated with governance and control; the **CBA** inquiry concerns a 2018 prudential review and non-financial risk weaknesses. **Slater & Gordon's historical UK acquisition** appears in the governance appendices as an example for reviewing strategy, diligence and governance, not as evidence of a current management culture. For named contemporary individuals or organisations mentioned in the curriculum, allegations must not be treated as established misconduct without authoritative findings, and the case's 2025 teaching vintage must be distinguished from later developments. Full sourced exercises are allocated to [File 08](08_Directors_Toolkit_Cases_and_Practical_Applications.md). [CUR] AICD[GAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 20-31]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 52, 81-82].

**Positive Impact Centre Limited (PIC), a fictional governance and culture exercise.** GOV's 2025/26 case describes a growing not-for-profit organisation whose volunteer board includes two executives, has undertaken governance reviews and faces concern about excessive board involvement in management. Following an overseas conference, directors seek reimbursement of a dinner attended by spouses despite a policy requiring family-related costs to be personally borne; the CFO resists. The issue is not primarily the absolute amount of roughly **A$2,500**. It is whether directors can make a self-interested exception, undermine management for enforcing policy, create a conflict of interest, and set a damaging culture example while claiming governance improvement. Directors should examine authority, proper process, conflicts, policy, documentary evidence and consequences, rather than use collective status to prevail. This is an **AICD hypothetical**, not an allegation concerning a real charity. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 103-104]; AICD[GSH, 2025/26 V1, PDF p. 18]. Detailed role-play and decisions belong in File 08.

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** culture may vary considerably across divisions, geography and contractors; board incentives, assured non-financial data and market conduct are consequential. **Community:** volunteers are part of culture; attention to beneficiary safety, donations, harassment, informal family dynamics and realistic workloads is essential. **Indigenous corporation:** culture includes both corporate conduct and the locally legitimate ways people understand authority and accountability; conventional staff surveys do not measure community cultural legitimacy, and culturally sensitive evidence should be designed and interpreted with authorisation.

**Director questions:** Which behaviours are rewarded despite contradicting our values? What bad news reached us from an external party first? Could an employee, volunteer or community participant safely raise a concern? What does the CEO do when a high performer breaches the rules? What would an independent site visit or community-authorised review reveal that the dashboard cannot?

## 02.15 CEO appointment, performance evaluation, incentives, succession and emergency continuity

### 02.15.1 CEO selection is a governance and cultural decision

**CEO appointment and oversight** are among a board's most consequential controls because the CEO shapes strategy execution, resource allocation, information flow and behaviour throughout the organisation. The board should define the leadership problem before selecting the candidate: sustaining an established model, managing a crisis, scaling operations, rebuilding trust or changing an operating model require different capabilities. Candidate evaluation should cover ethics, judgement, performance history, culture, ability to build a management team, relevant sector constraints and willingness to accept challenge. The most persuasive presenter may not be the best steward. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 52-55, 89-99]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 7-8, 49-52, 101-102].

A robust appointment process defines the CEO's delegation, authority limits, reporting obligations, performance expectations, contract terms and escalation duties. References and checks should test decision quality under pressure as well as claims of achievement. For small organisations without a paid CEO, the analogous question is which executive coordinator, president or volunteer leader carries management responsibility and how the board oversees that role without inventing a job title. In a CATSI corporation, the rule book and statutory provisions govern appointments and lines of authority; local legitimacy considerations may materially affect leadership success. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 52-55]; [ANALYSIS].

### 02.15.2 Performance must integrate outcomes, conduct and organisational capacity

**CEO evaluation** should assess agreed outcomes and leading indicators, including strategy execution, quality of management information, financial stewardship, safety, employee and stakeholder trust, risk appetite, succession depth, and compliance. Performance should be reviewed against conditions known when targets were established, while avoiding blanket exculpation for adverse external events. A leader can meet financial targets yet undermine controls or create a fragile culture; a leader navigating an adverse market may protect long-term value despite weak short-term metrics. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 95-97]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 7-8, 101-102].

The chair should convey feedback clearly, but the board owns evaluation criteria and significant employment decisions. A sensible annual cycle is: agree strategic and behavioural objectives; review mid-period with evidence; collect appropriate management and stakeholder insights; evaluate performance and conduct; decide remuneration and development; update the succession assessment. Ensure the CEO knows how disagreements will be resolved and that sensitive performance information is handled confidentially. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.15.3 Incentive design, conflicts and unintended behaviour

**Executive incentives** should not reward results obtained by taking unauthorised risk, suppressing incidents or weakening controls. Boards should examine leading and lagging performance measures, payout discretion, risk adjustments, malus or clawback where legally and contractually applicable, and whether rewards reflect sustainable outcomes rather than temporary financial gains. A bonus formula cannot capture every ethical problem. It can, however, create incentives for behaviour the board later condemns. [CUR] AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 95-97]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 109-112].

For community organisations, incentives may be largely non-monetary: status, access to resources, grant discretion, public recognition or reappointment can influence behaviour. For CATSI corporations and RNTBCs, the same need for conflicts and fair performance appraisal applies, with added attention to whether management incentives are aligned with corporation purpose and legitimately authorised native title or community objectives. The board must not measure cultural legitimacy through an outsider's unapproved indicator alone. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.15.4 Planned and emergency succession

**CEO succession** is an organisational resilience system. The planned process identifies emerging leaders, capability gaps, required experiences, interim coverage and whether an external search may be needed. The **emergency continuity plan** addresses sudden incapacity, resignation, death, misconduct allegations or loss of confidence. It names the interim decision-maker, delegations, communication protocol, access to systems, critical signing authorities and safeguards against unilateral board interference. The board should rehearse the scenario and review it when business complexity changes. [CUR] AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 3-11]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 7-8]; [ANALYSIS].

A director should distinguish **removing a CEO from employment or executive office** from **removing a director**, if the CEO is also a board member. Contractual, constitutional, statutory, procedural and disclosure requirements may differ. A sudden leadership problem can affect investors, beneficiaries, staff and community legitimacy differently. Seek applicable advice rather than assume the chair alone may terminate every office. [LAW] entity-specific law, governing documents and contracts.

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** formal nomination/remuneration processes, market-sensitive disclosure and leadership bench strength are material. **Community:** a 'key person' dependency on one volunteer or founder may be the chief continuity risk; a simple list of interim responsibilities is valuable. **Indigenous corporation:** succession must respect corporate appointments, rule-book powers and community-authorised legitimacy, without presuming cultural authority transfers with a management title.

**Director questions:** If the CEO left tomorrow, who could sign, pay staff and manage immediate risk? Do our performance measures reveal risk-taking quality? Is our information flow dependent on one person? How would we act if the most commercially successful executive behaved contrary to our values?

## 02.16 Director, chair, committee and whole-board evaluations and improvement plans

### 02.16.1 Evaluation is a governance learning cycle

**Board evaluation** is the structured review of how the board, its members, chair and committees fulfil their functions, interact and improve organisational outcomes. An evaluation is not made effective by the existence of a survey or the publication of a statement that it occurred. It becomes effective when it identifies material weaknesses, assigns credible corrective action, protects candour, and checks whether subsequent behaviour and decisions improve. Feedback is best considered both immediately after consequential events and periodically as a broader strategic assessment. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 89-104].

The AICD material recommends **brief meeting debriefs** and more formal evaluation as complementary mechanisms. A debrief should ask not only what task was completed but how the team worked: preparation, candour, information exchange, decision clarity and support. The chair should hear other directors' observations before supplying a personal diagnosis; a brief record of the one or two agreed improvements and the next review date is more useful than a defensive narrative. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 90-94]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 27-28].

### 02.16.2 Evaluate distinct levels, not an undifferentiated 'board'

| Evaluation level | The real question | Useful methods | Typical action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole board | Is our work aligned with purpose, risk and decision needs? | Charter/agenda review, interviews, outcome tracing | Rebalance agenda or revisit information flow |
| Chair | Does the chair elicit judgement, manage relationships and challenge appropriately? | Confidential director and CEO feedback, observation | Coaching or revised chair protocols |
| Individual directors | Are directors informed, ethical, engaged and constructive? | Self-review, confidential peer feedback, preparation and attendance evidence | Development or succession plan |
| Committees | Does specialist oversight genuinely support the full board? | Charter comparison, committee reports, auditor/management feedback | Change membership, scope or reporting |
| Board-management relationship | Does challenge produce clarity without operational takeover? | CEO/executive interviews, decision/action review | Improve board-paper instructions or delegation |
| Cultural and stakeholder legitimacy | Are relevant voices heard through legally and locally appropriate channels? | Authorised stakeholder feedback and evidence of decisions | Redesign consultation or communication protocols |

The table is an original evaluation architecture [ANALYSIS] informed by the separate subjects discussed in AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 94-103]. Personal reviews should be handled with privacy and procedural fairness, particularly where results influence reappointment or removal. A sensitive board may benefit from an independent facilitator, but independence alone does not cure weak terms of reference or lack of follow-up.

### 02.16.3 Evaluation design and independence

A formal review can combine self-assessment, peer feedback, observation of meetings, interviews with management and selected stakeholders, analysis of actual board decisions, and review of documentary evidence. Survey answers measure perceptions; they do not necessarily establish competence or causality. External facilitation can help where there is a dominant chair, entrenched interpersonal conflict, major strategic failure or a need for confidential challenge. It also creates confidentiality, cost and trust considerations. The appropriate method depends on organisational size, maturity and the sensitivity of findings. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 94-100].

**Listed governance recommendation [REG].** ASX fourth-edition Recommendation 1.6 calls for a listed entity to disclose a process for periodically evaluating the performance of the board, committees and individual directors, and whether an evaluation occurred in or for the reporting period. Recommendation 1.7 separately concerns senior executive evaluation. Neither tells a board that one simple annual survey necessarily produces effective improvement. AICD discusses the potential value of external evaluations on a multi-year cycle, but the recommended frequency should not be silently converted into a statutory rule applicable to all entity forms. [REG] ASX fourth edition, Recommendations 1.6-1.7; [CUR] AICD[GAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 2-3]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 94-100].

### 02.16.4 From observations to a credible improvement plan

A **board improvement plan** should be short enough to use, with specific changes, owner, deadline and evidence. The curriculum suggests a small set of board priorities and considers functional tasks, particular improvement actions, stakeholder relationships and collective performance as distinct evaluation dimensions. A board that lists 30 low-impact actions and returns to normal behaviour has not achieved improvement. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 102-103].

An original improvement register might identify: (1) reduce time on retrospective reporting to create room for a biannual strategy challenge; (2) require an alternative in every material investment paper; (3) give all directors a protected first contribution before the chair announces a preference; (4) introduce a direct escalation report on material safeguarding concerns; and (5) conduct a confidential CEO succession tabletop review. Each has an owner and evidence of behaviour change. These examples are proposals, not measured best-practice targets. [ANALYSIS]

**Evaluation trap: the halo of good results.** A board with strong financial or service outcomes should still challenge its information and decision process. Conversely, weak outcomes do not necessarily demonstrate negligent governance. A useful review follows two or three consequential decisions from initial briefing to recorded alternatives, chosen assumptions, implementation and learning. This approach avoids judging all capability from a satisfaction score or an isolated outcome. [ANALYSIS]

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** use formal periodic disclosure, independent facilitation where material and clear committee/CEO feedback. **Community:** brief peer conversations, an annual self-review and a small action register may be sufficient, but personal familiarity must not prevent confronting serious weaknesses. **Indigenous corporation:** the process should be accessible and culturally legitimate, with locally authorised feedback from relevant stakeholders and clear separation of board performance from native title consent and community satisfaction; never let an external survey substitute for required consultations.

**Director questions:** What actually changed after the last evaluation? Does the chair receive genuinely independent feedback? Are we measuring participation and decision quality or merely meeting attendance? How do we know management benefits from the board's work? Which difficult finding has no action owner?

## 02.17 Indigenous cultural governance considerations: legitimacy, inclusive deliberation, authority and community-designed processes

### 02.17.1 Distinguish formal compliance from legitimate authority

A **CATSI corporation** is an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander corporation registered under the *Corporations (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander) Act 2006 (Cth)* and regulated by the Office of the Registrar of Indigenous Corporations (ORIC). A **registered native title body corporate (RNTBC)** has particular functions in holding or managing native title rights and interests under native title legislation and regulations. The same organisation may have corporate directors, members, employees, community relationships and affected **common law native title holders** who are not all the same people. **Corporate governance legitimacy** concerns whether formal authority is legally valid; **cultural legitimacy** concerns whether arrangements are credible to the relevant people in their own setting. A system can be formally compliant yet lack acceptance, or be locally credible while requiring legal changes to become valid. Neither form of legitimacy should be presumed to cancel the other. [REG] ORIC, *Decision-making in RNTBCs*, https://www.oric.gov.au/for-corporations/decision-making/decision-making-RNTBCs; [RESEARCH/INDIGENOUS GOVERNANCE] AIGI, *Effective Indigenous governance*, https://aigi.org.au/toolkit/effective-indigenous-governance.

The **Australian Indigenous Governance Institute (AIGI)** identifies four high-level principles of effective Indigenous governance: **customised**, **culturally legitimate**, **two-way accountable** and **evolving**. *Customised* means tailored to the people, objectives and circumstances rather than borrowed unaltered. *Culturally legitimate* requires credibility under relevant local norms, institutions and expectations. *Two-way accountability* recognises both Indigenous governance relationships and external legal or funding responsibilities. *Evolving* means governance must adapt as priorities and conditions change. These principles are attributable to AIGI; they do **not** authorise an outside researcher or director to specify a community's cultural rules. [INDIGENOUS GOVERNANCE] AIGI, *Effective Indigenous governance*, cited above, accessed 27 October 2026.

### 02.17.2 Three distinct decision classes for a RNTBC

| Decision class | Illustrative subject | Critical authority test |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary corporate operations | Staff management, operational budgets, routine contracts | Does the CATSI Act, rule book or lawful delegation give the board/CEO authority? |
| Member or constitutional matters | Rule-book changes, director election or membership structure | Does the rule book and CATSI Act require member decision, special resolution or particular representation? |
| Native title or compensation decisions | Certain agreements, actions affecting native title rights or compensation applications | Which affected common law holders must be consulted and consent, under native title laws and relevant regulations? |

ORIC's guidance identifies both **native title decisions** and applications for compensation as requiring consultation and consent from relevant affected common law holders, including those who are **not corporation members**, and explains certification requirements. The relevant process is governed by native title legislation, the *Native Title (Prescribed Bodies Corporate) Regulations 1999* and applicable RNTBC arrangements. A unanimous director resolution is not, of itself, a substitute for affected-holder consent. Equally, not every routine corporate purchasing decision requires a native title consent meeting. Precise classification and legal procedures must be assessed for the actual transaction. [REG] ORIC, *Decision-making in RNTBCs*; [LAW] *Native Title (Prescribed Bodies Corporate) Regulations 1999* and CATSI Act, as applicable.

### 02.17.3 Inclusion cannot be designed from stereotypes

A legitimate deliberative process is **community-designed or community-authorised**, not inferred from broad descriptions of Aboriginal custom. The use of Elders, representative groups, kinship-based discussion, language interpretation, gender-sensitive arrangements or extended consultation periods may be appropriate in particular places where the people concerned support them, but none should be asserted as universal. AICD BE pp. 79-80 discusses consensus-oriented decision traditions through attributed research; that discussion should be read as illustrative, not as a rule for all First Nations peoples or corporations. Some organisations may use deliberation towards consensus and then undertake a legally required formal vote. Both stages need clear records and legitimacy, but their methods must be authorised locally. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 59, 79-80]; [INDIGENOUS GOVERNANCE] AIGI, *Effective Indigenous governance*.

AIGI describes an example in which the Arnhem Land Progress Aboriginal Corporation has used cultural liaison and board communication support as part of its own two-way governance. The appropriate lesson is that **some groups design additional communication roles that help translate governance concepts meaningfully**; it is not a mandate to replicate ALPA's arrangements elsewhere. [INDIGENOUS GOVERNANCE] AIGI, *Effective Indigenous governance*, ALPA example. Cultural information may be confidential, collective, restricted to authorised audiences or otherwise unsuitable for unrestricted board packs and external analytics. Ask what may be recorded, by whom, stored where and communicated to whom, and obtain relevant community authority and legal advice before circulation. [ANALYSIS]

### 02.17.4 An original legitimacy-and-decision-rights gate

Before a consequential RNTBC matter is tabled, a board can require a short **authority and legitimacy note**: (1) precisely describe the proposed decision and affected rights; (2) classify it as corporate, member or native title/compensation, including any overlapping approvals; (3) identify the applicable statute, regulations, rule-book provision and proposed resolution; (4) identify affected people and how lawful, appropriate consultation will be established; (5) document who designed or authorised the communication process; (6) identify conflicts, language and access needs; (7) obtain evidence that required consent and certification processes are satisfied; and (8) set reporting back to holders and implementation controls. This is a **proposed governance checklist**, not a replacement for ORIC guidance, native title legal advice or community-authorised rules. [ANALYSIS]

**Across contexts.** **Listed:** legitimacy often concerns investor trust, lawful approval and affected stakeholder expectations, with some external consent rights in projects. **Community:** charities and associations may need legitimate member, beneficiary and local stakeholder engagement beyond bare formal voting. **Indigenous corporation:** potential overlapping corporate, member, traditional and native title authority is a first-order governance design issue. Never import ASX independence rules or volunteer-association customs as an automatic replacement for CATSI rules or locally authorised processes.

**Director questions:** Which people have a formal right to decide, which must consent, and which should be heard? Are non-member common law holders affected? Who authorised our engagement design? What cultural information may we record? What evidence shows that a resolution is both formally valid and legitimately understood?

## 02.18 Original practical diagnostics and director/board self-assessment questions

### 02.18.1 The board-effectiveness diagnostic: observe evidence before scoring

The following **original diagnostic** converts the preceding frameworks into a repeatable governance review. It is **not** a validated psychometric instrument, an AICD grading rubric or a legal compliance opinion. Use a qualitative assessment, supporting examples and an action plan. A high self-rating should count for little without evidence; a low rating may reflect temporary crisis conditions rather than permanent dysfunction. [ANALYSIS]

| Dimension | Evidence to inspect | Strong board test | A plausible warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose and performance | Strategy, mission results, annual priorities | Board attention reflects the actual value at risk | Agenda driven entirely by history and habit |
| Authority and delegation | Charter, rules, reserved powers, committee mandates | Everyone knows who may decide and on what conditions | Committees or executives assume unwritten powers |
| Personal competence | Induction, preparation, peer review | Every director understands core duties and challenges material assumptions | Specialist status substitutes for basic scrutiny |
| Information and assurance | Packs, audits, incident reports, escalations | Material adverse news arrives early and with context | Surprises emerge first from media, funders or regulators |
| Teamwork and challenge | Observation, debrief, dissent records | Strong disagreement can occur respectfully and affect decisions | Silence, factions, personal attacks or predetermined approvals |
| Decision architecture | Options, assumptions, resolutions, review triggers | Major commitments have alternatives and conditions | No-action option omitted; sunk costs drive continued funding |
| Board composition | Skills matrix, appointment record, succession | Capabilities match coming decisions | Famous credentials conceal missing relevant skills |
| CEO and culture | Targets, incentive outcomes, speak-up evidence | CEO is accountable for conduct as well as results | High performer protected despite serious misconduct |
| Stakeholder and cultural legitimacy | Consultation records, governing rules, authorised feedback | Relevant rights and local decision processes respected | 'Consultation' held after binding commitment |
| Learning and evaluation | Actions from debrief and independent review | Behaviour changes after findings | Repeated annual survey without corrective action |

### 02.18.2 Evidence-based self-assessment, not personality labelling

For each dimension, participants can use four **descriptive states**, not invented statistical thresholds: **unexamined** (no reliable evidence); **partly established** (process exists but is inconsistently applied); **demonstrated** (recent decisions and records support the practice); and **tested under pressure** (the practice held when incentives, time or status discouraged it). Document at least one counterexample and the date of the latest evidence. This deliberately avoids numerical precision that a small self-review cannot justify. [ANALYSIS]

**Individual director prompts:** Can I explain the entity's purpose, duties, cash position and principal risks without reading the CEO's presentation? Which view of mine changed recently and why? When did I last acknowledge an error? Have I kept conflicts and confidential information appropriately managed? Am I being silent to preserve relationships or dominating to win? Do I understand which Indigenous governance or member rights lie outside board discretion in this entity?

**Chair prompts:** Whose contribution did I actively invite? Have I indicated the preferred decision too early? Does every significant dissent receive a fair hearing? Do I respond to poorly presented concerns by testing their content or simply shutting them down? Has the CEO received clear feedback from in-camera sessions? Can another director challenge my judgement without personal consequence?

**Whole-board prompts:** Which three decisions of the past year deserve re-examination of process? What was the genuine alternative? Which board paper contained material information we could not independently validate? Have committees made the board more competent or more dependent? Which cultural indicator looks reassuring only because the reporting channel may be broken? What has the board stopped doing after an evaluation?

### 02.18.3 Three-context stress tests

**Test A: listed-company irreversible investment.** Management proposes a large acquisition based on a high forecast growth rate. Before approval, ask for external comparators, downside cash exposure, capital opportunity cost, alternative uses of capital, decision rights, conflicts, financing conditions and triggers for reevaluation. Observe whether directors challenge both the numbers and the strategic narrative. This tests authority, bias, risk, strategy and capital discipline together. [ANALYSIS]

**Test B: volunteer charity under funding stress.** A long-serving founder recommends an expanded program despite falling unrestricted cash and rising safeguarding complaints. Ask for cash commitments, donor restrictions, beneficiary risk, complaints pathway, conflict and board-management boundaries. Compare conditional pilot, scaled continuation and cancellation. Observe whether volunteer status or personal relationships prevent challenge. This tests purpose, solvency, speak-up and proportionality. [ANALYSIS]

**Test C: RNTBC commercial opportunity involving native title.** A commercial counterparty wants rapid agreement with an attractive upfront payment. Classify the decision and affected rights *before* comparing financial terms. Determine consultation, consent, rule-book and certification requirements and ask who has authorised the engagement process. The board must not substitute director enthusiasm for affected common law holder decisions, or allow commercial time pressure to erase lawful rights. This tests legal authority, cultural legitimacy and decision quality. [ANALYSIS]; [REG] ORIC, *Decision-making in RNTBCs*.

### 02.18.4 A compact 90-day improvement method

**Days 1-30: establish evidence.** Identify two recent consequential decisions, observe one meeting, review the action register and collect confidential feedback from directors and management through suitable channels. Examine one meaningful culture warning and verify how it was escalated. For a culturally governed entity, establish permission before collecting community-specific evidence.

**Days 31-60: select changes.** Agree a maximum of three substantive priorities based on risks and outcomes, for example better alternatives in board papers, clear committee delegations, or lawful stakeholder/holder consultation. Appoint an owner and identify how effectiveness will be observed rather than simply whether a policy was drafted.

**Days 61-90: test and adapt.** Apply the revised process to a real decision; conduct a short debrief; ask management whether clarity improved; record unresolved concerns; and either embed the change or revise it. Escalate immediately if the review reveals a potential legal breach, safety risk or invalid decision. Ninety days is an **illustrative governance improvement cycle**, not a statutory period or a guarantee of cultural change. [ANALYSIS]

**Practical closing question:** If a regulator, auditor, beneficiary, member, affected common law holder or future director reviewed the underlying record, would they see an informed, authorised, ethical and carefully challenged decision, or merely a confident conclusion?

**Early Gifts Limited, in brief.** The AICD fictional charity scenario places a board in a serious ethical and operational dilemma about whether to continue assisting vulnerable people in a conflict-affected setting following political upheaval, amid safety risks, mission obligations and important funder relationships. The teaching purpose is to examine what the board owes to beneficiaries, personnel, donors and mission, how it obtains reliable information under uncertainty, and whether staged or alternative approaches could reduce avoidable harm. Neither an automatic 'stay' nor an automatic 'leave' answer follows from the scenario. Do not confuse the named fictional organisation or location in the case with a verified present-day international operating incident. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 107-108].

**XYZ Sport, in brief.** The AICD fictional sporting organisation faces substantial financial and going-concern warning signs alongside a proposed sponsorship arrangement that raises reputational, environmental and stakeholder issues. The case refers to approximately **$5.2 million in losses over two years**, around **$3 million of debt**, and a possible **$12 million sponsorship over two years**; those figures are *fictional scenario inputs*, not evidence of an actual sports body. The board needs an independently understood cash forecast, financing and solvency assessment, careful examination of sponsorship conditions and stakeholder consequences, and an appropriately challenged CEO response. An auditor's going-concern warning is serious but should not automatically be equated with a final legal finding of cash-flow insolvency. [CUR] AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 109-111].
