### Fast decision gateway

1. **Who is acting?** A CATSI corporation? A Corporations Act company? An association? A trustee? A registered charity? An RNTBC? Identify registration numbers and legal capacity.
2. **What is the decision about?** Routine business, corporate rule-book change, membership/representation, land or cultural heritage, native title rights, agreement, compensation application, or restricted money?
3. **Whose rights and assets are engaged?** The corporation's own property; native title held for common law holders; a third-party trust; grant funds; an Indigenous Land Use Agreement (ILUA) benefits trust; or a mixture?
4. **Which legal pathway controls?** CATSI governance alone, PBC Regulations 8/8A/8B and 9, ILUA registration law, trust instrument, state heritage law, charity law, or several concurrent pathways?
5. **Does the evidence establish authority?** Check the determination, affected holder population, required decision-making process, signed/current rule book, consents, conditions, meeting evidence, independent advice and regulatory certificates.
6. **Only then decide and sign.** Where an agreement crosses several decision classes, complete *every* applicable authorisation path. The order and evidence requirements are detailed at **07.11** and **07.18**.

## 07.01 Diversity of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, organisations and governance systems; limits of universal claims

### 07.01.1 Distinct peoples, sources of authority and histories

Aboriginal peoples and Torres Strait Islander peoples comprise many distinct nations, island communities, language groups, families and other communities. Relationships to Country, Sea Country, traditional laws and customs, knowledge, authority, kinship, representation and methods of reaching decisions vary. They may also differ *within* a particular group, across generations and according to the question in issue. Colonial dispossession, displacement, administrative intervention and contemporary urban, regional and remote experience have produced different institutional conditions. An RNTBC's recognition of native title is one legal expression of continuing rights, not a complete description of identity, culture, authority or self-government. The High Court recognised the common law's capacity to acknowledge rights arising under traditional laws and customs in *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* [1992] HCA 23. The Commonwealth Attorney-General emphasises that the content of native title necessarily varies by community. [CASE-MABO] [GOV-AG-NATIVE]

A board must therefore reject purported national templates for who may speak on Country, whether Elders have a single final say, whether every decision requires unanimity, or which family may represent all others. Consent cannot be established simply by finding the most visible spokesperson or a person with seniority. The relevant peoples themselves determine their authority arrangements, subject to the law governing the particular decision. The legal threshold for identifying holders may be established by a Federal Court determination; operational arrangements can be expressed in a rule book; other cultural governance may operate outside both. [LAW-NTA ss 55–59; LAW-PBC r 8(3)–(4)]

**AICD curriculum:** GOV pp. 46–49 describes Aboriginal governance as substantive, longstanding and diverse, and introduces Aunty Dr Mary Graham, Aunty Dr Lilla Watson and Gaala Watson's accounts of lateral/knowledge-based authority and consensus. GOV p. 47 expressly disclaims a singular overarching Aboriginal Law or method. BE p. 79 presents an account of consensus decision making, but it must be read as attributed teaching, *not* an empirical assertion that all First Nations decisions everywhere are unanimous. RISK pp. 16–17 links the assessment of risk to Country, future generations and relational accountability, illustrating one particular example about Karajarri water management. That AICD example is not authority to infer a Karajarri protocol for other peoples. [AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 46–49]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, p. 79]; AICD[RISK, 2025/26 V1, pp. 16–18]]

### 07.01.2 Questions and evidence for directors

**Establish:** which peoples and legal holders are connected with the land, program or decision; whether there are distinct affected classes or overlapping claims; whether the decision involves cultural knowledge, family obligations, governance of an organisation, or legally held native title. **Request:** publicly available determination/map, registered rule book, group-authorised statement of relevant decision arrangements, current engagement protocol, explanation of differing views and independent anthropological or native title advice where needed. **Authority:** the people, legal holders and formally authorised actors appropriate *to this decision*, not a generic stakeholder list. **Record:** whose authority was relied on, why, who was not reached, limits of the evidence and the follow-up. **Monitor:** exclusion, fatigue, intergenerational effects and the adequacy of community feedback.

An outsider should never decide that a person is an Elder, Traditional Owner, knowledge holder, common law holder or family representative based only on title, appearance, attendance or a proponent's preference. Cultural authority and membership can overlap, but neither proves the other. Also, not every Aboriginal corporation's central purpose is land or native title. There are health, housing, art, education, media, commercial, advocacy, service-delivery and other enterprises with their own different constituents and legal obligations. [AIGI-TOOLKIT] [AIGI-EFFECTIVE]

## 07.02 Available legal structures, CATSI corporations, charities, associations, trusts and PBC/RNTBC roles

### 07.02.1 Start with the actual legal vehicle

| Organisation / capacity | Primary law and public regulator | What it means and what it does **not** prove |
|---|---|---|
| Aboriginal/Torres Strait Islander-managed proprietary/public company or company limited by guarantee | *Corporations Act 2001*; Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) | A company need not be CATSI-registered; shareholder/member rights and duties arise under its company law/constitution. |
| Incorporated association, e.g. in Western Australia | Relevant state/territory statute, such as *Associations Incorporation Act 2015* (WA); state regulator | Association committee duties and permitted operations derive from that statute and rules, not from CATSI merely because the members are Indigenous. |
| CATSI corporation, evidenced by an Indigenous Corporation Number (ICN) | *Corporations (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander) Act 2006* (CATSI Act); Office of the Registrar of Indigenous Corporations (ORIC) | A Commonwealth legal person under a distinct corporate regime. Neither charity status nor native title status follows automatically. |
| Registered charity | *Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission Act 2012* and *Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission Regulations 2022*; ACNC | Charity status overlays a legal vehicle where conditions for registration are met. It is not a legal form on its own. |
| Trust / corporate trustee | Particular trust deed, equitable obligations, applicable trust legislation, any specific land/native title law | Assets may be held for beneficiaries rather than beneficially owned by the corporation; separate fiduciary duties and indemnity limits apply. |
| Prescribed body corporate (PBC), after registration an RNTBC | CATSI Act **plus** *Native Title Act 1993*, Federal Court determination and PBC Regulations | Exists to hold native title on trust or act as agent for common law holders. Not every CATSI corporation qualifies. |
| Native title representative body (NTRB) or recognised native title service provider | Native Title Act Part 11 and relevant recognition/funding arrangements | Provides representation, facilitation or services; it is not necessarily the RNTBC or the native title holder itself. |
| Statutory Aboriginal land council or Torres Strait authority | Specific Commonwealth/state/territory land-rights or statutory legislation | Legal rights and approval requirements may be materially different from native title/RNTBC powers. |

The registration gateway is decisive. Obtain the ICN and ORIC register record; where relevant also check the Australian Business Number, ASIC register, ACNC Charity Register, state associations register, National Native Title Register and the actual trust deed. A group's public name, its use of “corporation”, or a project partner's reference to “Traditional Owners” is insufficient to classify it. Different legal entities may sit in one economic group, employ the same people and share premises. [LAW-CATSI Chs 2–3; LAW-NTA ss 56–59; LAW-PBC r 4; REG-ORIC-DM]

### 07.02.2 Why structure matters

The structure fixes who can own assets, sue and contract, select directors, receive grants, distribute any surplus, lodge reports and be wound up. It also affects liability for trading as a trustee, charitable purpose restrictions, taxation status and community control. A trading subsidiary might legitimately undertake tourism, construction or service delivery, whereas a PBC's functions remain tied to the native title determination and other valid objectives. A trust may receive agreement benefits while a separate corporation pays staff. Treat related entities as a **legal map**, not a single treasury account. [LAW-CATSI ss 96-1, 99-5, 271-1; LAW-PBC rr 6–7]

**RNTBC threshold:** PBC Regulations r 4 requires a qualifying CATSI corporation, appropriate objects, native title-holder membership or holder-consented non-holder membership, and the CATSI Indigeneity requirement. It is only an RNTBC following the relevant statutory determination and registration pathway. Where non-native-title holders seek RNTBC membership, r 4 and the definition of *high level native title decision* require special attention. The corporation cannot decide alone to expand membership eligibility beyond holders' consent. [LAW-PBC rr 3–4, 8; LAW-NTA ss 56–59]

**AICD curriculum:** GOV pp. 9–20 surveys corporate structures and separate legal personality; LEG pp. 11–17 treats constitutions, contracts and delegations as the internal legal framework; LAPP pp. 11–14 distinguishes registered charity, incorporated association and unincorporated arrangement. The comparative state/CATSI table at LAPP p. 5 says **March 2024**, and is a *dated overview*: verify each provision before applying it in 2026. [AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 9–20]; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 11–17]; AICD[LAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 5, 11–14]]

**Director practice:** Commission a one-page legal-entity chart specifying incorporation number, governing law, charitable status, relevant determination(s), capacity (principal, agent or trustee), fund owners, directors, signatories, reporting regulator and intercompany contracts. Separately map all material assets and beneficial owners. Before board approval of a related-entity transfer or contract, ask whose money it is and whether the signing company is permitted to act in that capacity. Reconcile the chart annually and whenever a new grant, ILUA, trust or subsidiary arises.

## 07.03 CATSI statutory architecture: registration, rule books, corporate powers, meetings, members, records and ORIC

### 07.03.1 Incorporation and internal governance

The CATSI Act is not simply a renamed Corporations Act. Its Chapter 2 regulates CATSI registration; s 29-5 sets an Indigeneity requirement with details prescribed in the CATSI Regulations, and ss 29-10–29-25 address other registration conditions. Under s 29-5, if there are at least five members, the prescribed percentage applies; the special treatment of smaller member counts is set out separately. Do not state an Indigeneity percentage without checking the **operative regulation and any applicable exemption**. Under ss 96-1 and 99-5, the corporation is a legal person with corporate capacity and prescribed execution methods. However, legal capacity is distinct from **internal authorisation**, fiduciary limits, land rights and statutory native title consent. A signature that complies with s 99-5 does not prove a native title agreement was validly authorised. [LAW-CATSI ss 26-1, 29-5, 96-1, 99-5]

A CATSI corporation's constitution and internal governance rules are commonly called its **rule book**. Chapter 3 and ss 60-1 onwards deal with replaceable rules and the constitution. The actual registered rule book decides which permitted defaults have been displaced, whether membership is direct/representative, board composition, meetings, conflicts beyond statutory minima, election/termination, dispute handling and delegations. Not every CATSI statutory provision is replaceable. A rule book cannot override mandatory CATSI rules, the Native Title Act, PBC Regulations or third-party rights. A draft or board policy is not necessarily the registered operative rule book; obtain the filed version and subsequent valid changes. [LAW-CATSI Ch 3, ss 60-1, 69-1–69-5; REG-ORIC-RULE]

### 07.03.2 Members, meetings and board powers

Chapter 4 controls membership, records and member protections, notably ss 144-1–144-10 (admission and entry on register), ss 150-1 onwards (ending membership), s 153-1 (classes) and ss 163-1, 166-1–166-10 (rights and oppressive conduct remedies). Chapter 5 regulates general meetings and director meetings, including member-requested meetings (ss 201-5–201-15), meeting notice, voting, quorum, annual general meetings (s 201-150), directors' meetings (ss 212-1 onwards), and minutes (s 220-5). Chapter 6 deals with eligibility, appointment and duties of directors (ss 243-1, 246-1 onwards, 265-1 onwards). Section 274-1 is the default board-management power, subject to the Act and constitution; s 274-10 permits delegation subject to conditions; s 265-50 preserves accountability for improper delegation and reliance. [LAW-CATSI Chs 4–6]

A corporation should verify its annual general meeting (AGM) and reporting dates and not assume an ASX company calendar. ORIC currently explains the usual financial year as 1 July–30 June, with an AGM by **30 November** and reporting by **31 December**, subject to applicable first-year rules and extensions. The company secretary/contact person should maintain one compliance calendar tying together AGM, directorship notifications, register updates, filings, charitable requirements and native title certificates. [LAW-CATSI ss 201-150–201-155, 330-1–330-10; REG-ORIC-ANNUAL; REG-ORIC-FIRST]

### 07.03.3 Records and ORIC's role

The board should maintain an accurate register of members and directors, financial records, minutes of *corporate* proceedings, executed contracts, conflict register, authority/delegation register, grant acquittals and statutory reports. RNTBCs additionally maintain PBC Regulations r 9 consultation certificates and supporting consent evidence, access-controlled cultural records and trust administration records. ORIC administers registration, monitors lodgements and compliance, provides rule-book and dispute assistance, and has statutory regulatory and enforcement powers. The Registrar does **not** certify every cultural claim or substitute for native title-holder authorisation. The National Native Title Tribunal, Federal Court and native title representative bodies play distinct roles. [LAW-CATSI Chs 7, 10, 14, ss 220-5, 322-1 onward; LAW-PBC rr 9–10; REG-ORIC-DISPUTES]

**Director's minimum evidence pack:** (1) current register extract, (2) stamped rule book and variation history, (3) board/member register with eligibility evidence where lawfully retainable, (4) signed director consents and director identification compliance, (5) previous AGM and board minutes, (6) statutory reports and audit letters, (7) material agreements, trusts and grant terms, (8) compliance and dispute register. Record which document governed each decision and the quorum, notice, voters, conflicts and delegation used. Escalate missing/contradictory records rather than cure them with a retrospective assertion. [LAW-CATSI ss 201-20–201-35, 212-20–212-25, 220-5, 246-10, 308-1 onwards]

## 07.04 CATSI director and officer duties, proper purpose, care, conflicts, transactions, solvency, accountability and remedies

### 07.04.1 Statutory duties are entity-specific

A CATSI director or officer owes the **CATSI** duties: care/diligence (s 265-1(1)); good faith in the corporation's best interests and proper purpose (s 265-5); no improper use of position (s 265-10) or information (s 265-15); criminal offences for dishonest or reckless misconduct (s 265-25); disclosure of material personal interests (s 268-1); and restrictions on interested directors' participation (s 268-20). Common law and equitable obligations may also apply (s 265-30). Unlike a general-purpose company director, an RNTBC director has special statutory recognition of native title obligations: ss **265-20** and **265-25(2)** protect certain acts done in good faith with the belief that they are needed to comply with native title legislation. This is a tailored protection, **not** blanket immunity from poor financial management or a licence to bypass holders. [LAW-CATSI Divs 265, 268]

A corporation's purposes include its registered objects and any required charitable or PBC purposes. A person appointed to represent a family or region is still a director of the **whole corporation**. They should not act as an uninstructed conduit for one family benefit where that conflicts with statutory duties. Conversely, acting on lawful native title holder directions may be necessary to discharge the RNTBC's obligations and is not a breach merely because an ordinary commercial board would have chosen differently. Distinguish *corporate interest*, *beneficiaries' interests*, *statutory native title holder rights* and *personal economic interest* before making a finding about loyalty. [LAW-CATSI ss 265-5, 265-20; LAW-PBC rr 6–8]

### 07.04.2 Care, business judgment, delegation and cultural expertise

Section 265-1(2) provides a qualified business judgment rule for the duty of care, requiring good faith/proper purpose, absence of material personal interest, appropriate information and a rational belief in the corporation's best interests. It applies to **business judgments**, not as a universal defence for illegal native title authorisation, trust breaches or other mandatory procedures. Under s 265-45 reliance on advice is assessed on competence and context; the Act expressly recognises expertise in traditional laws and customs for this purpose. Section 265-50 addresses responsibility for delegates. Directors should obtain both culturally appropriate knowledge and independent specialist legal/financial advice, testing the advice's assumptions and conflicts. [LAW-CATSI ss 265-1(2)–(3), 265-45, 265-50]

**AICD curriculum:** LEG pp. 23–54 teaches fiduciary duties, informed inquiry, reliance, business judgment and solvency principally through the Corporations Act. Its principles are useful, but CATSI provisions must be cited separately. BE pp. 72–81 emphasises deliberation, bias and reflection, relevant to ensuring directors are heard and decisions are not dominated by a funder, chair, lawyer or project proponent. TOOL pp. 60–66 offers a general director duties checklist to *adapt*, not mechanically adopt. [AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23–54]; AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 72–81]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 60–66]]

### 07.04.3 Conflicts: two different questions

First, determine whether the interest is one that **must be disclosed**, is permitted to be held and whether the director may participate under CATSI ss 268-1 and 268-20. Section **268-5** specifically removes a notice obligation for an RNTBC director's interest *merely as a common law holder* of the native title managed or held by the corporation. It does not exempt personal ownership of a contracting business, a director's employment by the counterparty, or special financial advantage to a relative. Secondly, assess the related-party financial-benefit regime in **Part 6-6**, especially s 284-1: member approval is generally required unless a statutory exception in Division 287 applies, with procedure in Division 290. Disclosure and abstention alone do not necessarily satisfy Part 6-6. [LAW-CATSI ss 268-1, 268-5, 268-20, 284-1; Divs 287, 290]

**Applied illustration, hypothetical:** A director's sibling offers earthmoving services for $180,000 to the corporation. The board should obtain conflict details and a related-party assessment, competitive procurement evidence or credible alternatives, budget and funding permission, pricing verification, a legal opinion on any Part 6-6 exception and approval path, and properly constituted independent deliberation. If the transaction affects a native title asset or land authority, additionally classify the native title decision. Minute the disinterested decision, tested options, price, interested-director treatment and monitoring of delivery. Do not assume that culturally significant family obligations themselves constitute misconduct, nor that family proximity removes statutory conflict rules. [LAW-CATSI Divs 265, 268, 284–293]

### 07.04.4 Trust debts, insolvency and enforcement

**CATSI s 271-1 is unusually important**: directors may personally bear certain liabilities incurred by a CATSI corporation acting as trustee where the corporation cannot discharge the debt and its right to be indemnified from trust assets is impaired because of breach of trust, acting beyond trustee powers, or restrictive trust terms. Mere lack of trust assets is **not by itself** the s 271-1 trigger. Under s 531-1, relevant Corporations Act insolvency provisions, including s 588G, are applied with modifications. Analyse actual cash-flow insolvency, debts when incurred, available protections and any formal restructuring advice. Do not automatically extrapolate Corporations Act safe-harbour wording without checking the CATSI application provision and transitional rules. A fully funded grant budget does not prove that unrestricted cash can pay wages, superannuation or suppliers as debts fall due. [LAW-CATSI ss 271-1, 531-1; LAW-CORP s 588G]

Civil consequences can include declarations, pecuniary penalties, compensation, injunctions and disqualification, depending on provision and elements (CATSI Chapters 8, 10 and 14). Specific criminal liability is different from civil duties; monetary penalties may change with legislation. Avoid historical dollar maxima. Directors should request a rolling 13-week cash forecast, forecast unrestricted reserves, liabilities and creditors, trust reconciliation, audit issues and related-party register at each material financing decision. Record dissent and urgent advice when solvency is doubtful. [LAW-CATSI ss 265-25, 279-1 onwards, 386-1 onwards, 531-1, 576-25; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 47–54]]

## 07.05 Membership, representation, eligibility, election/removal, dispute rights and the significance of the actual rule book

### 07.05.1 Membership is a statutory status, not a synonym for identity

Membership permits particular corporate voting, meeting and inspection rights. It is distinct from being an Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander person, a Traditional Owner, an RNTBC common law holder, a trust beneficiary, a resident or an authorised cultural spokesperson. CATSI ss 144-1–144-10 require eligibility, a valid application/acceptance pathway and entry on the membership register. Corporate member classes and rights must be checked against s 153-1 and the rule book. For an RNTBC, PBC Regulations r 4 and specific CATSI provisions impose additional constraints on admission and removal. [LAW-CATSI ss 144-1–144-10, 150-22, 153-1; LAW-PBC r 4]

ORIC describes two common RNTBC rule-book models: **direct representation**, where eligible individual holders may join, and **indirect representation**, where specified people represent holder subgroups. In the indirect model, many common law holders may **never themselves become corporation members**; this does not extinguish their native title rights. The registered rule book must enable representation of all common law holders directly or indirectly and include additional dispute provisions. A membership list is useful for a corporate meeting but generally incomplete evidence of those entitled to native title decision-making. [REG-ORIC-RULE; REG-ORIC-HOLD]

### 07.05.2 Admission, elections, removal and electoral legitimacy

For each vote verify the meeting type, eligible voter list at the proper record date, nomination method, directors' term and eligibility, permitted proxies, quorum, special-resolution thresholds and any subgroup allocations. Under CATSI s 246-15, members can appoint directors in general meeting subject to the law and rule book; ss 249-10 and 249-15 control relevant removal routes. A director cannot simply substitute a family meeting for a statutory director-election process; conversely a lawful rule book may incorporate group nomination and representation choices. Voter information must be understandable and available in time to make the right meaningful. [LAW-CATSI ss 201-20 onwards, 243-1, 246-1–246-25, 249-1–249-15]

For an RNTBC, ORIC explains that a corporation's **membership model** or board composition rule-book change is a corporate governance decision requiring member processes, ordinarily a special resolution. But allowing **non-common-law-holders to be eligible members** is separately a high-level native title decision requiring affected holders' consent under the PBC Regulations. Treat both conditions as cumulative where relevant. The holder consent should precede and inform the rule-book vote, with registration of valid amendments as required. [REG-ORIC-DM; LAW-PBC rr 3(1), 4, 8; LAW-CATSI Ch 3]

### 07.05.3 Removal, grievances and exclusion risks

RNTBC membership-cancellation grounds are constrained by the CATSI Act, particularly s 150-22 and related provisions; a rule book cannot create a convenient new ground to expel a dissenter. Consider notice, procedural fairness, evidence and review rights. A contested membership decision and a disputed claim to be a native title holder are **different disputes**. The first may concern statutory membership eligibility or corporate procedure. The second concerns native title identity, determination terms and potentially anthropology, and must not be concluded merely by a majority board vote. [LAW-CATSI ss 150-1–150-35, 163-1, 166-1; REG-ORIC-RULE; REG-ORIC-DISPUTES]

**Director action:** establish who is in/out of the register and *why*, identify groups with sparse representation, request a legally constrained explanation for any rejected application or cancellation, and seek independent advice where eligibility is contested. Record eligibility criteria, evidence used, reasons given, procedural steps and internal review. Monitor applications by subgroup and location, not just total membership growth. For RNTBCs report separately on **common law holder engagement**, which corporate membership statistics cannot measure.

## 07.06 AIGI's four principles: customised, culturally legitimate, two-way accountability and evolving governance

### 07.06.1 Effective and legitimate are different tests

The **Australian Indigenous Governance Institute (AIGI)** distinguishes governance that works effectively (capable of achieving agreed objectives) from governance that is legitimate (the governed regard its authority and methods as credible). This is more demanding than judging a board by compliance, attendance and clean accounts alone. A community may experience a formally compliant institution as unrepresentative or imposed; a popular representative system may lack the records, controls or legal procedures needed to protect rights and resources. Sustained governance needs both. AIGI's *Indigenous Governance Toolkit*, “Effective Indigenous governance”, develops four specific principles. These are **recommended governance principles grounded in Indigenous governance research**, not universal rules issued by a court or legislature. [AIGI-EFFECTIVE, source accessed 27 October 2026]

1. **Customised.** A governance structure should be developed for the particular people's objectives, relationships, resources and operating context. This calls for actual community design, not relabelling a standard company constitution or copying another nation's selection rules. The critical test is whether the arrangements work for those governed while meeting legal requirements.
2. **Culturally legitimate.** Authority and decisions should reflect the group's contemporary understandings of legitimate leadership, ways of exercising power and its traditions, laws, rules and values. AIGI emphasises that such arrangements should be generated through the people's own efforts and have their support. It is not an outsider's prerogative to identify one universal cultural authority structure.
3. **Two-way accountability.** Governance must address accountabilities to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples' own laws, expectations and relationships **and** to the wider external system: statutory law, employees, funders, regulators, commercial counterparties and other legally recognised interests. Two-way governance is an institutional design and translation challenge. It is neither blind submission to funders nor a claim that law can be disregarded.
4. **Evolving.** Rules and institutions require review as community aspirations, leadership, demographics, law, external pressures, funding and organisational capabilities change. A rule book can be amended through lawful procedures without assuming that tradition must be static. Equally, evolution is not permission for directors to unilaterally rewrite recognised cultural authority or holder consultation processes.

**AICD curriculum:** GOV pp. 48–49 introduces these same four principles using AIGI as the source. It places them alongside a particular account of Aboriginal governance at GOV pp. 46–47. The AICD does not provide an independently sufficient operational test for a proposed native title ILUA; the legal decision procedure comes from the PBC Regulations and the Native Title Act. [AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 46–49]; AIGI-EFFECTIVE]

### 07.06.2 Turn principles into governance evidence

| AIGI principle | Board question | Evidence of progress | Warning signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customised | Who designed our membership, representation, meetings and services, and for what locally defined purpose? | Group-developed options, explicit choices, feedback and accessible rule book. | Outside adviser delivers an unmodified template and calls it community consent. |
| Culturally legitimate | Who is recognised as speaking about different issues? Who disagrees, and how can they be heard? | Locally endorsed protocol, independent validation where contested, roles explained without disclosing restricted knowledge. | Board chair or proponent assumes all cultural authority. |
| Two-way accountability | Can we explain a decision both to the community and in a lawful regulator/funder record? | Dual report: community-facing explanation and statutory/financial evidence; clear exception escalations. | Minutes show only a legal signature, or only a cultural assertion with no statutory test. |
| Evolving | What is no longer working? How can it be revised lawfully? | Scheduled rule-book review, accessible evaluation and compliant amendment process. | Governance arrangements cannot be challenged or changed; or changes are imposed at speed. |

**Practice cycle:** (i) ask relevant constituents what outcomes matter; (ii) map legal non-negotiables; (iii) co-design possible structures; (iv) test conflicts and exclusions; (v) formalise within the rule book where needed; (vi) resource translation and training; (vii) monitor outcomes and revise. Record disagreements and who will revisit them. An organisation might adopt a community council with advisory, appointment or cultural roles, but must specify whether it has legal decision power, and cannot simply delegate PBC-regulated holder decisions to it without authority. [AIGI-EFFECTIVE] [AIGI-GOV] [LAW-PBC rr 8–9]

## 07.07 Cultural authority, Elders, Traditional Owners, local governance arrangements, community relationships and external obligations

### 07.07.1 Different kinds of authority

**Cultural authority** concerns the standing, knowledge, responsibility and legitimacy a group recognises in relation to Country, ceremonies, stories, relationships, land and collective affairs. **Corporate authority** concerns who may act for the legal entity under statute, rule book, meeting rules and delegations. **Native title authority** concerns the specific people and procedures recognised by the determination, Native Title Act and PBC Regulations. **Trust authority** concerns beneficiaries, trustee powers and any protector or decision role in a deed. **External authority** may arise from a government grant, land access licence, heritage statute or regulatory approval. A person can hold several kinds; they do not merge automatically. [AIGI-GOV] [LAW-CATSI Chs 3–6] [LAW-PBC rr 6–9]

The title “Elder” is not legally equivalent to director, common law holder, shareholder or a statutory native title decision-maker. Some communities may choose to recognise senior cultural advisers formally, as in the attributed AIGI Kanyirninpa Jukurrpa example at 07.17. Others may use different arrangements. The board should ask rather than prescribe. If a traditional decision process applies for giving consent under PBC r 8(3), the legal requirement is to use **that actual process**; if it does not, r 8(4) directs attention to a process agreed to or adopted by the relevant affected holders. Do not assume a show of hands, one-vote-per-person ballot, nominated Elders' approval or unanimous consensus is legally adequate in every instance. [LAW-PBC r 8(3)–(4); AIGI-CULTURE]

### 07.07.2 Relationships are governance infrastructure

Community meetings, on-Country engagement, different languages, travel, youth participation, women and men's culturally restricted domains where applicable, and appropriate remuneration for specialist knowledge may materially affect inclusion, cost and timing. These are design questions whose answers should come from the relevant people. Directors need a budget for authentic participation and time between information being delivered and authority being sought. Confidentiality requirements must be balanced with affected holders' right to receive enough information about alternatives, risks and consequences. An urgent project timetable does not create an exception to PBC r 8. [LAW-PBC rr 8–9] [AIGI-EFFECTIVE]

**Two-way accountability applies to external parties as well.** Governments should not make a grant conditional on the corporation sidelining lawful community authority; project proponents should not insist on discussing benefits solely with management while bypassing affected native title holders; the corporation should not tell a funder that a cultural council's endorsement resolves corporate signing or grant acquittal. If the arrangements conflict, identify the provisions and renegotiate the schedule, delegate, scope or grant condition where lawful. [LAW-CATSI s 265-5; LAW-PBC r 8] [AIGI-EFFECTIVE]

**Board evidence:** authority map by topic and place (not an indiscriminate list of “leaders”); the basis on which voices were selected; engagement funding and participation barriers; independent translation/anthropology/legal advice as appropriate; records of opposition or unresolved matters; clarification of which body is *advisory* and which is *deciding*. **Monitoring:** who participates, whether returns to Country/community occur, whether concerns result in revised outcomes, complaints of exclusion, and respect for restricted knowledge. No public document should disclose confidential cultural or genealogical details merely to demonstrate diligence.

### 07.07.3 Reconciliation of apparent conflicts

**Hypothetical:** A culturally respected advisory group asks a board to divert a legally restricted service grant to an urgent community ceremony. Both concerns may be real and important. The board should acknowledge and document the request and the cultural significance as expressed by the group, check the executed grant's permissible purposes, review lawful variation/reallocation possibilities with the funder, identify unrestricted revenue or a lawful donation route, and consider the corporation's purposes. It must not secretly reclassify the grant. If the request is part of a native title benefits trust, obtain the deed and holder/beneficiary instructions as applicable. The culturally legitimate course need not be the legally prohibited one; a credible alternative can respect both. [LAW-CATSI ss 265-1, 265-5; FUNDER actual agreement; LAW-PBC rr 6–7 where applicable]

## 07.08 Country, cultural heritage, data and knowledge governance, confidentiality, intellectual property and Indigenous data sovereignty

### 07.08.1 Country and heritage are substantive, not reputational, matters

Country may encompass land, waters, living relationships, stories, responsibilities, sacred/restricted places, resources and generations. Corporate actions can affect these differently from the asset categories in financial statements. The Native Title Act protects rights recognised in a native title determination, but it does not itself exhaust the relevant laws. Commonwealth and state/territory cultural heritage, land access, planning, environmental, biodiversity and Aboriginal land-rights legislation may impose overlapping restrictions and procedures. A lawfully approved native title agreement is not necessarily a cultural-heritage permit, and a heritage permit is not necessarily holder consent to a surrender of native title. Identify the jurisdiction and site before advising on any specific approval. [LAW-NTA s 223, Parts 2–3; GOV-AG-NATIVE]

An entity holding freehold land might have broad statutory/corporate power to sell it; an RNTBC holding native title on trust does *not* have equivalent free-sale discretion. When a proposal concerns a cultural survey, tourism venture, renewable project, water access or a traditional knowledge licence, first classify property and rights. Distinguish heritage management services provided commercially by a corporation from native title consents conferred by common law holders. For overlapping tenure, map who holds freehold, lease, native title, cultural-heritage decision rights and financial benefit entitlements. [LAW-CATSI s 274-1; LAW-NTA ss 56–59; LAW-PBC rr 6–8]

### 07.08.2 Indigenous cultural and intellectual property

Australian copyright, confidence, contracts, trade marks and other intellectual property laws protect some expressions and uses, but do not automatically protect every collective, intergenerational or culturally restricted form of knowledge on the same terms. A photograph, map, recording or research dataset may carry conventional copyright plus contractual, cultural and ethical restrictions. The legally recognised copyright owner is not necessarily the person or people with cultural authority to approve disclosure. Directors should ask separate questions: Who can consent to collection? Who owns copyright? Who controls access? What permissions exist for reuse, digitisation, training an artificial intelligence model, machine translation or offshore storage? Can consent be withdrawn or limited? [AIGI-CULTURE] [AIATSIS-CODE] [GIDA-CARE]

A practical **knowledge and data schedule** attached to a project agreement should address purpose and sensitivity; classification by the relevant knowledge holders; access rules; named custodians; duration and storage location; copies and deletion; publication consent; copyright and licence limits; commercial benefit; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander attribution preferences; data security; breach notification; audit; re-use and secondary uses; dispute process; and return or culturally appropriate disposal. A developer's “perpetual, worldwide, transferable” licence to all cultural material should be treated as a material negotiation risk, not mere boilerplate. [ANALYSIS; AIATSIS-CODE; GIDA-CARE]

### 07.08.3 Indigenous data sovereignty and research ethics

The Global Indigenous Data Alliance's **CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance** (collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, ethics; announced 2019 and articulated in 2020 research) identify people and authority as central to data stewardship. They complement, rather than simply repeat, technical FAIR principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable). The **AIATSIS Code of Ethics for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Research**, published **October 2020**, addresses self-determination, Indigenous leadership, impact/value, and sustainability/accountability. These are ethical/research governance standards. Whether binding on a particular organisation depends on its contracts, funding, project, professional obligations or other law. They should not be described as an independently enacted privacy law. [GIDA-CARE; AIATSIS-CODE; ARDC-CARE]

**Director practice:** implement a cultural information register with a separate restricted appendix; verify collection authority and data pathways; require purpose-limited consent, controlled contractor access and no artificial-intelligence training by default without appropriate permissions; commission cybersecurity and research-ethics advice for high-risk projects; return results in a community-authorised form. Publish only nonsensitive governance process evidence. Do not put sacred location coordinates or cultural eligibility data into a public board paper to satisfy generic transparency demands. When legal disclosure duties arise, obtain advice on minimisation, protective orders and restricted access. [LAW-CATSI ss 265-15, 322-1 onwards; LAW-PBC r 9(5); AIATSIS-CODE]

## 07.09 Native title, prescribed bodies corporate, RNTBCs, trustee versus agent PBCs and relationships with common law holders

### 07.09.1 The determination, not the corporation's aspirations, starts the analysis

The Native Title Act defines native title through rights and interests possessed under traditional laws and customs, maintained connection and recognition by the common law (s **223**). A Federal Court determination specifies whether native title exists, the holders and the rights/interests (ss **55–57**, **225**). It can distinguish rights by geographic area, exclusive/non-exclusive character or classes of holders. This is different from the *registered native title claim group* pending a final determination; an RNTBC usually arises after an approved determination, while applications and negotiations before that stage can involve registered native title claimants and representative bodies. [LAW-NTA ss 55–59, 61, 223, 225, 253]

Once native title is determined to exist, the Federal Court also addresses governance under ss 56–57. If a suitable PBC is nominated with its consent to hold native title on trust, **s 56** provides for a trustee PBC; otherwise, the holders retain title and **s 57** provides for a PBC to undertake statutory functions on their behalf. On the relevant registration, the PBC becomes an RNTBC for statutory purposes. Section **58** enables detailed PBC regulations; **r 4** imposes eligibility and objects requirements. The same corporation may serve multiple groups/determinations if statutory conditions are satisfied (PBC Regulations r 5). This does *not* collapse the distinct holder groups or their decision rights. [LAW-NTA ss 56–59, 59A; LAW-PBC rr 4–5]

### 07.09.2 Trustee and agent compared

| Question | Trustee PBC | Agent PBC |
|---|---|---|
| How established? | Federal Court determination under Native Title Act s 56; registered PBC. | Court pathway under ss 56–57 where title is not held in trust by the PBC. |
| Who holds underlying native title? | PBC holds rights and interests **on trust** for common law holders. | Common law holders hold title; the PBC acts as their agent/representative for prescribed functions. |
| Core functions | Manage rights; hold associated money on trust; invest/apply that money **as directed** by holders; consult under rr 8–8B (r 6). | Act as agent; manage as authorised; may **also hold money on trust**; invest/apply as directed; consult under rr 8–8B (r 7). |
| Whose consent for native title decision? | Affected common law holders under r 8, subject to valid standing instructions/low-level pathway. | Same r 8 decision rule. Agent status is not a board discretion exemption. |
| Key financial risk | Trust asset segregation, powers/indemnity, correct holder directions. | Agent authority **plus** trust money duties where held; money does not automatically become general revenue. |
| Key evidence | Determination, rule book, trusts, holder directions, consent certificate. | Determination, agency authority, rule book, holder directions, consent certificate. |

A PBC can be trustee of **native title rights** while a different trustee holds **agreement benefits**, or can be an agent PBC that holds certain **compensation money in trust** under PBC r 7. Never conclude “agent means no trust” or “trustee PBC owns benefits beneficially”. Investments and payments directed by holders do not eliminate statutory finance, charity or trustee obligations; they specify additional legal authority for a particular class of property. [LAW-PBC rr 6(1)–(1A), 7(1)–(1A)]

### 07.09.3 PBC functions beyond legal title

An RNTBC may negotiate agreements, accept statutory notices, exercise future-act procedural rights, administer cultural heritage services or run businesses. Some activities fall within its corporate powers and ordinary governance, some within regulated native title functions, and some within another land/heritage law. RNTBCs may charge prescribed fees for certain services; Native Title Act s 60AB and PBC Regulations Part 4 regulate aspects and the Registrar's opinion function. Revenue from a service is not ipso facto native title compensation, and a payment described as a “royalty” in commercial material is not automatically a royalty arising from a mineral title held by the corporation. Trace the **legal source of the payment**, payer, payee and beneficial entitlement. [LAW-NTA s 60AB; LAW-PBC rr 19–26; REG-ORIC-DM]

**Evidence and monitoring:** retrieve the sealed determination and schedule, Native Title Register entry, map in an appropriately controlled system, naming/registration of PBC, trust-versus-agent order, holder descriptions and any classes, a legal opinion on overlaps, rule book, fee schedule, delegation and bank account/trust reconciliation. Monitor changes in laws, determinations, claim boundary, additional holdings or beneficiary claims. Independent native title advice is essential if the relevant group, rights or geographic extent is disputed.

## 07.10 Critical distinction between corporate membership and native title rights and interests held by common law holders

### 07.10.1 Two non-equivalent populations

**Corporate members** acquire legal rights through application, eligibility, entry on the corporation's register and the CATSI Act/rule book. **Common law holders** are the persons in whom native title is recognised under the determination, by reference to traditional laws and customs. An RNTBC is a legal instrument for managing these rights; it does not create native title status by admitting someone or extinguish native title status by refusing membership. Some holders may be minors, live away from Country, decline membership, lack necessary paperwork or be represented indirectly. PBC r 4 and ORIC guidance expressly contemplate structures where holders are not identical to the membership register. [LAW-CATSI s 144-1; LAW-NTA ss 56–57, 223, 225; LAW-PBC r 4; REG-ORIC-HOLD]

The **affected common law holders** for a decision may also be a subset of everyone recognised by a determination. For a determination identifying holder classes, PBC r 8(3)–(4) focuses on classes on whom the proposed decision **would have an effect**. For a compensation application, r 8B applies its own identification conditions for **specified persons**, including geography and whether relevant classes are identified. An RNTBC with multiple determinations must not casually assume that a whole-of-corporation vote substitutes for decision-making by affected classes. [LAW-PBC rr 8(3)–(4), 8B(2)–(4)]

### 07.10.2 Why non-member holders still have enforceable participation rights

PBC **regulation 8(1)** places the requirement on a trustee RNTBC or registered agent PBC **before making a native title decision**: consult and obtain consent from relevant common law holders using the required mechanism. The definition in r 3 specifically includes ILUAs, section 31 agreements, surrender, admission of non-holder membership classes and other actions affecting native title. PBC **regulation 8B** similarly governs the decision to make compensation applications. These duties do **not** say “members voting at the AGM”, and their beneficiaries are not restricted to the corporation's formal membership. ORIC explicitly states that non-member common law holders retain the same native-title decision rights as member holders. [LAW-PBC rr 3, 8, 8B; REG-ORIC-HOLD; REG-ORIC-DM]

A required consent process may follow a particular decision process under the holders' traditional laws and customs if one must be followed for that consent (**r 8(3)**); otherwise it follows a process agreed to or adopted by the relevant holders for that decision or kind of decision (**r 8(4)**). **Low-level** decisions may use a properly holder-consented rule-book consultation process (**r 8A**). Certain specified **standing instructions decisions** may be covered by previously obtained approval, within any imposed conditions (**r 8(2), (8)–(9)**). None of these mechanisms is permission to fabricate a cultural method or infer continuing blanket consent from the corporation's original registration. [LAW-PBC rr 8–8A]

### 07.10.3 The non-substitution test

**Hypothetical, legally decisive:** An RNTBC has 300 recognised common law holders. Forty are corporation members; thirty attend a valid general meeting and approve a proposed ILUA. The directors also pass a valid resolution to sign it. The corporate votes may be valid **as corporate decisions**, but the facts do *not* show that the common law holders affected by the ILUA were identified, consulted and consented under r 8. If holders belong to different recognised classes, approval by one subset cannot automatically bind the others. The legal team must establish the applicable holder procedure and evidence; a compliant PBC r 9 certificate is required after the native title decision, with r 9's evidentiary limits. **Conclusion: do not execute or represent authority as complete on that evidence.** [LAW-PBC rr 3, 8, 9; REG-ORIC-DM]

The reverse also matters: a valid native title-holder decision does not necessarily elect directors, approve an unrelated corporate financial benefit to a related party, change a rule book or authorise a separate trustee to distribute assets. Several layers of approval may be necessary. **Director records:** maintain a *corporate decision ledger* and a separate access-controlled *native title consent ledger*, linked by a transaction ID. Include holder population and mapping, process used, invitations, information supplied, meetings, material opposition, outcome, conditions, certificate and advice. Do not populate the member register with sensitive genealogical details solely to make it stand in for holder evidence.

## 07.11 Decision taxonomy: routine corporate matters, native title decisions, compensation, agreements, consultation, consent, certification and records

### 07.11.1 Classify the decision before debating its merits

A decision should not be described simply as an “RNTBC decision”. It may be **(A) a corporate decision**, **(B) a native title decision**, **(C) a compensation-application decision**, **(D) a trust/fiduciary decision**, **(E) a cultural-heritage or other statutory decision**, or a combination. The controlling documents may therefore require different actors, separate processes and cumulative approvals. This taxonomy is a director's analytical instrument; it is not an additional legislative category. The legally defined native title decision is in **PBC r 3**. [LAW-PBC rr 3, 6–10; REG-ORIC-DM]

**A. Corporate decisions.** Approving an ordinary operating budget, appointing a chief executive, approving a payroll system, renewing insurance, hiring employees, acquiring office equipment and deciding on a corporation-owned freehold property ordinarily sit with directors or properly appointed delegates under CATSI **s 274-1**, their rule book, reserved-matters schedule and any restrictions. Member approval may apply to changing rules, electing directors or particular related-party benefits. A board must distinguish the ordinary **decision to include a transaction in a budget** from any further consent required before it is carried out. [LAW-CATSI ss 69-5 onward, 274-1, 284-1; REG-ORIC-DM]

**B. Native title decisions.** A *high-level* native title decision under **PBC r 3** includes a proposed **surrender**, entry into an **Indigenous Land Use Agreement (ILUA)**, entry into a **right-to-negotiate agreement under Native Title Act s 31**, a decision about eligibility of non-holders for PBC membership, and an alternative consultation process in the constitution. A *low-level* decision is another action affecting the common law holders' native title rights and interests (other than a decision to make a compensation application). These defined categories do not depend on deal size. A small access permission may be low-level but still require r 8 consultation/consent or a valid r 8A/standing-instructions pathway. [LAW-PBC rr 3, 8–8A]

**C. Compensation applications.** The **decision to make a compensation application** is deliberately treated separately under **r 8B**, including the identification of relevant determinations, specified persons and applicable decision process. Negotiating **settlement terms**, making promises concerning **benefit allocation**, or investing **compensation money** can raise additional distinct authority questions. Do not treat an r 8B approval to file an application as advance, unlimited approval of all settlements, fees, distributions or investments. [LAW-PBC rr 6–8B; LAW-NTA ss 51, 61]

**D. Trust decisions.** Before investing or distributing native-title money held in trust, identify the legal holder and beneficial owners, the terms of the deed, any statutory holder direction requirement, investment policy, charity purpose and contractual restrictions. A group authorisation to enter an ILUA does not automatically rewrite the benefit trust deed. PBC r 6(1) and r 7(1) require application or investment of relevant money **as directed** by the entitled common law holders; other trustees must follow their own instruments and the general law. [LAW-PBC rr 6–7; REG-ORIC-DM]

**E. Other decisions.** Aboriginal heritage permissions, statutory approvals, land access, intellectual property licensing and government agreements have jurisdiction-specific pathways. A heritage consent may involve a different statutory definition of Aboriginal parties or knowledge holders from a native title determination. An RNTBC may exercise a contractual cultural services role without the action itself being a native title decision; conversely a proposed project may trigger both. Obtain local legal advice before assuming equivalence. [LAW-NTA Part 2 Division 3; AIATSIS-CODE]

### 07.11.4 Evidence design: two decision records and an authority map

Create a **corporate approvals register** and a protected **holder authority record** for each transaction. Cross-reference but do not merge data indiscriminately. The corporate record includes exact CATSI/rule-book quorum, resolution, conflicts, delegated signing capacity and funding source. The holder record includes determination/area/classes, identified participants, procedure legal basis, accessible information, authority for representatives, deliberation and consent result, conditions, r 9 certificate and r 10 access handling. The engagement record includes third-party parties, contractual conditions, NNTT notices, any objection and final registration. Restricted names, cultural mapping and sensitive knowledge belong in permission-controlled annexes. [LAW-PBC rr 8–10; LAW-CATSI ss 99-5, 220-5, 274-1]

**Stopping questions:** Does this affect rights of non-member holders? Is the decision high-level, low-level or compensation under precise regulation? Could an Elders' endorsement be culturally significant but not legally sufficient under r 8? Could a legally valid holder vote coexist with a failure under trust deed or member approval? Have we checked the *final* negotiated version, rather than a draft approved weeks earlier? If any is unresolved, the board should adopt an investigation resolution, **not a final transaction authorisation**.

## 07.12 Indigenous Land Use Agreements, future acts, section 31 agreements, royalties, benefits, compensation, trusts and project relationships

### 07.12.1 Agreements with native title impact have distinct statutory routes

An **Indigenous Land Use Agreement** is an agreement under the Native Title Act framework about the use and management of land and waters, native title and future acts. The Act provides **body corporate agreements** (ss 24BA–24BI), **area agreements** (ss 24CA–24CL) and **alternative procedure agreements** (ss 24DA–24DM). Each has particular parties, subject matter, registration requirements and legal consequences. The Native Title Tribunal's registration guidance is the current process reference; do not select a form based solely on which has the shortest timetable. [LAW-NTA ss 24BA–24DM; LAW-ILUAREG; REG-NNTT-ILUA]

A registered ILUA can bind persons holding or claiming native title in the agreement area, including non-signatories, by operation of **NTA ss 24EA–24EB**, subject to the relevant agreement type and statutory conditions. The reach of those effects is why negotiating authority, information quality, eligible holder groups and registration evidence deserve separate review. The public **Register of Indigenous Land Use Agreements** is evidence of registration and recorded terms, not proof that every commercially important obligation appears in the public extract. Agreements may contain confidential payment schedules or deeds. [LAW-NTA ss 24EA–24EB; REG-NNTT-REGISTER; REG-NNTT-SEARCH]

A **section 31 agreement** arises in the distinct **right to negotiate** process for covered future acts. A section 29 notice identifies the proposed act, grantee and native title parties. Section **31** requires negotiations in good faith, and parties may reach agreement on doing the act and conditions. An RNTBC party still needs its PBC r 8 high-level native title process for entry into a section 31 agreement. A deal can be technically available under a future-act pathway but unacceptable to the affected holders, and a project proponent cannot transform a consultation statement into statutory holder consent by calling it a memorandum of understanding. [LAW-NTA ss 29–31; LAW-PBC rr 3, 8; REG-NNTT-RTN]

### 07.12.2 Authorisation and certification are not interchangeable

Use precise terms:

- **PBC consultation/consent under r 8**: RNTBC must obtain affected common law holder approval before its defined native title decision, with conditional lawful processes and exceptions.
- **PBC r 9 certificate**: corporation prepares a document recording compliance after the decision; it is evidence and has prescribed form/content, not the original source of the consent.
- **Native Title Act s 251A authorisation**: a definition of authorisation by an Aboriginal/Torres Strait Islander group for an ILUA in cases where that statutory test applies, particularly area agreements. It has a statutory decision-process structure that should not be casually treated as identical to r 8.
- **NNTT registration**: statutory application by parties with required documents, notice and possible objections; the NNTT's current matrix identifies when a **PBC r 9 certificate** is an attachment, including body corporate ILUAs and relevant area ILUAs where an RNTBC is a party.
- **Representative-body certification**: where applicable, native title representative body certifications follow the separate Native Title Act representative-body and ILUA provisions. The decision-maker must confirm the correct certifier and required evidence rather than assume the RNTBC can certify everything itself.

[LAW-NTA ss 24BA–24DM, 203BE, 251A; LAW-PBC rr 8–9; REG-NNTT-REGISTER]

**Registration sequence:** negotiate term sheet; determine ILUA type/parties; map land and rights; establish holder process; obtain consent to the exact version; authorise corporate execution; complete r 9 certificate; assemble registration application; observe notice/objection conditions; obtain registration; then apply the agreement's specific conditions precedent and monitor performance. If the final agreement materially changes the surrender, access, compensation, sharing or residual liability terms after consent, seek advice on **renewed holder approval** before treating the changed form as authorised. [LAW-PBC rr 8–9; LAW-NTA ss 24BA–24EB]

### 07.12.3 Deal value: calculate more than the headline payment

A proposed agreement might promise a lump sum, annual access payments, volume-based payments, employment and training expenditure, contracting opportunities, infrastructure, cultural services, environmental management, joint governance, data protection and economic development. These are different economic claims. Report them separately rather than declaring their undiscounted sum to be the “deal value”. Determine whether payments are fixed, indexed, conditional on production, discretionary, subject to minimums or capped, and whether gross figures include the project proponent's own operating costs. For project-related “royalties”, specify whether the term is **contractual** rather than a statutory royalty interest; identify production measurement, payment waterfall, audit rights, transfer/change-of-control rights and termination. [ANALYSIS; LAW-NTA Part 2 Division 3]

**Original illustration, not a public deal:** One proposal offers $2 million at signing, $400,000 annually for 15 years from production, and a discretionary “up to $5 million” employment programme. At a **7% real discount rate** and assuming annual payments arrive at each year-end from year 1 (not delayed), fixed payment present value is approximately **$3.64 million**, making nominally promised fixed payments worth approximately **$5.64 million** including the signing payment. The discretionary employment expenditure is **not a $5 million cash benefit to holders**. If commercial production begins five years later, the annual stream's present value falls further. This demonstrates sensitivity to timing, not a basis for imposing 7% as a community discount rate. Directors must ask holders what values, distribution preferences, intergenerational priorities and risk trade-offs matter; financial appraisal is one input, not the sole criterion. [ANALYSIS]

### 07.12.4 Native title compensation and beneficiaries

The Native Title Act allows compensation in specified cases for extinguishment or impairment of native title rights and interests, with questions of entitlement, acts, valuation, interest and appropriate non-economic/cultural loss. **Northern Territory v Griffiths [2019] HCA 7** (the *Timber Creek* compensation appeal) demonstrates that non-economic cultural loss may be legally material alongside economic loss and interest. Its particular findings about Ngaliwurru and Nungali people and the acts in Timber Creek cannot be extrapolated as a uniform per-hectare schedule or a general monetary benchmark. [LAW-NTA ss 51–53; CASE-GRIFFITHS]

Who can commence an application is governed by **NTA s 61** and the Native Title Act's procedural structure; an RNTBC's **r 8B** duty to consult specified persons is a further requirement. For areas of historic extinguishment, r 8B(2) expressly accommodates relevant determinations with an external boundary embracing the area. Class identification depends on the regulation's conditions, not the number of financial beneficiaries on a present-day corporation register. Proposed settlement can affect people not currently members, and any distribution requires an independent entitlement/benefit instrument analysis. [LAW-NTA ss 51, 61; LAW-PBC rr 7(1A), 8B]

### 07.12.5 Project relationships, obligations and economic dependence

A native title agreement is a long-duration governance relationship, not merely a legal instrument. The RNTBC should track the proponent's financial security, parent guarantees, future ownership, environmental and rehabilitation exposure, heritage reporting, site access, complaints, workforce targets, payment verification, intellectual property, confidentiality, inflation, renegotiation and dispute resolution. A 30-year agreement can be impaired by personnel turnover, production shutdown, a sale of the project or a change in the community's administrative capacity. [ANALYSIS; AICD RISK 16–18, 63–86]

**Director questions and monitoring:** What rights may be irrevocably surrendered? What alternative route would apply without agreement? What future-act process does the project use? Which common law holders are affected? Which costs and obligations may outlive production? What funds pay for RNTBC governance and legal monitoring throughout the contract? What audit rights and information will allow independent verification of volume-dependent payments? Could an apparent community benefit force service expenditure inconsistent with a trust deed? Prepare **an obligation-by-obligation register**, with owner, counterparty, entitlement, condition, due date, evidence, escalation and dispute window. Review it quarterly for major projects, and independently when material renegotiation is proposed.

## 07.13 Strategy, risk, financial stewardship, grant funding, procurement, assets, conflicts and accountable investment

### 07.13.1 Strategy must reconcile purpose, capability and obligations

An Indigenous-controlled corporation can pursue social, cultural, environmental and economic objectives together. An RNTBC may also have duties and community expectations that are not reducible to corporate earnings. The strategy test is therefore **what legally and culturally legitimate value is delivered, to whom, with what resources and under whose authority**. A surplus may be necessary to sustain services but does not by itself show that affected holders' rights, benefits or Country are protected. Similarly, a culturally compelling project can fail financially or breach a grant, trust or employment duty if it is not funded competently. [AICD RISK 11–18, 19–37, 87–110; AIGI-EFFECTIVE]

Directors should translate purpose into distinct workstreams: native title representation and decision support; corporate services and employment; cultural heritage/Country activities; programme delivery; economic development; financial reserves; member/holder communications. For each identify *beneficiaries*, *source of authority*, *money source*, *performance evidence* and *obligations after funding expires*. The board should approve a risk appetite containing non-negotiable constraints: legal holder consent, safety, integrity of trust assets, cultural-information permissions and no false representation of authority. Choices within those constraints can then involve explicit financial and operational risk tolerances. [AICD RISK 14–18, 97–110; TOOL pp 30, 89–92]

**An original “four-capital” review:** (1) Financial: unrestricted liquidity, trust and restricted balances, contingent liabilities, payment timing and solvency. (2) People: governance capacity, staff succession, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander workforce pathways, independent advisors and interpreter access. (3) Rights/Country: holder decision authority, healthy cultural relationships, heritage obligations, data sovereignty and future generations. (4) Systems: financial controls, records, cybersecurity, contract performance, complaints and assurance. These are analytical dimensions, not AIGI's published four governance principles. Board decisions should document trade-offs without asserting that every community ranks them identically. [ANALYSIS; AICD FIN 14–22; AIGI-TOOLKIT]

### 07.13.2 Grant-funding and service-delivery example

**Hypothetical:** A CATSI corporation receives a $1.2 million, two-year grant for regional housing support. The chief executive proposes charging the grant $180,000 for a new four-wheel-drive vehicle and central office refurbishment. Some spending might reasonably support field delivery, but a grant officer's verbal agreement and the existence of available cash do not establish eligibility. The board must obtain the executed grant, eligible-cost schedule, budget variation provisions, output/outcome requirements, capital asset vesting conditions, procurement terms, GST/accrual treatment and acquittal timetable. Set up a grant-specific accounting cost centre and transaction-level evidence. [LAW-CATSI ss 265-1, 265-5, 327-1 onward; grant contract]

Separate three decisions: **lawful eligibility** of the spending under the grant; **corporate approval** by an authorised delegate/board; and **quality of service delivery** to the intended community. If a director owns the proposed vehicle supplier, an additional CATSI conflict and possible related-party benefit process applies. A funder-approved expense may still be poorly priced or conflicted; a board-approved capital purchase may still be grant-ineligible. [LAW-CATSI ss 265, 268, 284-1; ANALYSIS]

Ask management for a quarterly dashboard showing: restricted cash opening/closing, funding received versus deferred, spent versus eligible amounts, cost per participant/service, outputs and outcomes, late deliverables, obligations remaining at grant expiry, audit exceptions and reimbursement risk. Do not treat grant income as guaranteed continuation or count undrawn funds as unrestricted reserves. **Monitor** whether temporary-funded permanent staff and leases create a recurring unfunded cost base. [AICD FIN 14–22, 50–79; TOOL pp 53–59]

### 07.13.3 Restricted funds are not excess liquidity

For every incoming receipt, record: payer, governing instrument, bank account, corporate/trust legal owner, beneficial interest, permitted uses, decision-maker, investment authority, accounting recognition, beneficiary eligibility, audit rights, repayment/termination conditions and whether funding can be pooled. Cash in a bank account can have different **beneficial ownership** or restrictions from its accounting location. A consolidated balance sheet may combine controlled entities for financial reporting while the laws and deeds that control *spending* remain separate. Intercompany balances do not transfer trust entitlements. [LAW-PBC rr 6–7; LAW-CATSI ss 265, 327–342; AICD FIN 76–78]

**Hypothetical:** An RNTBC reports $9 million cash, comprising $6 million native title compensation held as trustee, $2 million unspent grants and $1 million unrestricted operations. A four-month corporate cash burn of $1.4 million implies an apparent surplus if all cash is pooled, but potential unrestricted funding shortfall of about **$0.4 million**, before receivables and committed cash flows. Trustees may only use the compensation within governing powers and holder directions; grant money cannot fund an unrelated deficit absent lawful variation. Directors should request at once a **13-week entity-by-entity cash forecast**, bank-to-ledger-to-restriction reconciliation, aged liabilities and restructuring options. Do not borrow restricted money temporarily on the assumption that it will be replaced later without establishing legal power and consent. [ANALYSIS; LAW-PBC rr 6–7; LAW-CATSI s 531-1; AICD FIN 90–100]

### 07.13.4 Procurement in close relationships: rules and fairness without assumptions

In a regional community, directors may have personal, family or social relationships with suppliers, applicants and employees. This fact alone does not establish misconduct, disqualify all local suppliers or mean a related-party process is optional. The legal questions are the nature of interest, potential personal benefit, statutory disclosure, participation restrictions, any member approval requirement, value-for-money and any additional funder or charity rule. Directors should obtain legal advice where the relationship's statutory treatment is unclear. [LAW-CATSI ss 265-5, 265-10, 268-1, 268-20, 284-1 and Div 287]

**Hypothetical:** A director's sibling owns the only readily available transport firm. The corporation needs urgent medical transport. The board may have sound reasons to contract locally, but should not simply waive governance controls on grounds of community benefit. Record the relationship, give the unconflicted decision-makers information on urgency, safety and alternatives, obtain independent comparative pricing (including reasonable remote-market allowances), test the legally applicable member approval/exception, and minute why chosen terms are reasonable. If an emergency exception is relied upon, preserve the exact statute/deed basis rather than use “emergency” as a generic waiver. [LAW-CATSI ss 268, 284–287; ANALYSIS]

A realistic conflict policy distinguishes **actual, potential and perceived** conflicts as a risk-management technique but tests **material personal interests**, improper purpose and related-party financial benefits against their *legal* definitions. Regular standing declarations help, but a transaction-specific disclosure, recusal and approval trail is still necessary. In small boards, conflicted directors leaving may undermine quorum; seek ORIC/legal advice on a valid path, such as member approval or appointment, before trying to force a vote. Never invent a blanket permission for an interested director to participate because the transaction appears “culturally normal”. [LAW-CATSI ss 268-1–268-30, 284-1; AICD LEG 25–37]

### 07.13.5 Investments and benefit funds

Corporate investment of genuinely unrestricted reserves can be evaluated for liquidity, capital preservation, concentration, counterparty risk, costs, access, and alignment with mission. **Native title money in trust** adds specific holder-direction duties under PBC rr 6–7, and a separate benefit trust adds trustee duties and deed investment powers. “Long-term investment” is not itself evidence that a risky, leveraged property development is appropriate. Directors must examine whether returns benefit the right people, whether losses would impair distributions, whether a project creates conflicted jobs or an uncontrolled subsidiary, and whether exit is feasible. [LAW-PBC rr 6–7; LAW-CATSI s 265-1; ANALYSIS]

**Original analytical test:** For a proposed $4 million regional enterprise, compare a base case, downturn case and no-investment alternative. The board might estimate a 60% probability of a $1.5 million net present value and 40% probability of a **$2 million loss**, giving a simple probability-weighted value of **+$0.1 million**. That arithmetic does not prove the project should proceed: the capital may be restricted, probabilities uncertain, benefits uneven, financial risk concentrated or cultural criteria decisive. Use stress tests on insolvency, permanent employment, independent oversight, and strategic non-financial returns; do not label speculative forecasts as reported data. [ANALYSIS; AICD RISK 38–86]

**Director action package:** approve a transaction approval policy distinguishing funds and beneficiaries; obtain three-way cash forecasts and project-specific downside economics; establish independent valuation/procurement and conflict rules; conduct periodic trust and grant assurance; test programme outcomes with intended participants; and publish an accessible accountability report that does **not** disclose confidential cultural material. Report exceptions, missed payments, breaches and corrective actions to the board. [TOOL pp 36–38, 47–59, 118–123; AIGI-EFFECTIVE]

## 07.14 ORIC financial and annual reporting, corporation classification, consolidated gross operating income and charity overlap

### 07.14.1 Two separate ORIC tests, not one revenue band

The CATSI Act Part 7-1/7-2 and **CATSI Regulations rr 8–9 and 17–28, 42** establish a framework for classification and reporting. Official ORIC guidance describes **corporation size** by at least **two of three** indicators in a financial year: consolidated gross operating income (CGOI), consolidated gross assets, and employee numbers. The size is recorded on the ORIC public register. It is **not** solely an income classification and can differ from the corporation's charity status. [LAW-CATSI ss 327–333; LAW-CATSIREG rr 8–9; REG-ORIC-SIZE]

| Registered CATSI size | At least two criteria in applicable band (for a financial year) |
|---|---|
| **Small** | CGOI **under $100,000**; consolidated gross assets **under $100,000**; employees **fewer than 5**. |
| **Medium** | CGOI **$100,000 to less than $5 million**; consolidated gross assets **$100,000 to less than $2.5 million**; employees **5–24**. |
| **Large** | CGOI **$5 million or more**; consolidated gross assets **$2.5 million or more**; employees **25 or more**. |

“Two of three” means a corporation with CGOI over $5 million may still have registered medium or small status if **two other indicators** meet those bands, subject to the exact regulatory test and ORIC registration. **Do not assume $5 million CGOI necessarily equals a large corporation.** Conversely a corporation with income under $100,000 can be classified large on assets and staff. Directors must check the register and classification period; a significant new subsidiary, workforce expansion or asset receipt may move the indicators. [REG-ORIC-SIZE; LAW-CATSIREG rr 8–9]

**CGOI** is the corporation's gross operating income consolidated with entities it controls, as defined for CATSI purposes and described by ORIC. It is not identical to: cash receipts, free cash flow, net surplus, beneficially owned trust assets, unconsolidated turnover, taxable income, ACNC “revenue”, or **money available for unrestricted spending**. Seek an accountant's documented reconciliation for entities with investment, trust and benefit structures. Where accounting versus CATSI aggregation of a particular trust, grant or payment is unclear, obtain a formal accounting/statutory interpretation rather than presume that all bank deposits count, or that all restricted receipts do not. [LAW-CATSIREG rr 8, 19–28; REG-ORIC-SIZE; REG-ORIC-ANNUAL]

### 07.14.2 Annual reports are a second test: size **plus** CGOI

Even after size is established, ORIC's published reporting matrix uses **registered size** and **actual CGOI in the financial year**. Annual reporting is not simply “small = no financial report, medium = audit, large = four reports”. The alternative report route for qualifying public funding must be checked against the actual statutory text, not treated as a generic waiver. [REG-ORIC-REPORT; LAW-CATSIREG rr 17–28]

| Registered size | Annual CGOI | Ordinary ORIC reports required (subject to lawful exemptions/variations) |
|---|---|---|
| **Small** | Under **$100,000** | **General report** only. |
| **Small** | **$100,000 to under $5m** per ORIC guidance; see exact-boundary note below | **General + financial + audit** according to ORIC; statutory r 25 says **more than** $100,000; starred alternative only if all r 27 conditions are met. |
| **Small** | **$5m or more** | **General + financial + audit + directors' report**. |
| **Medium** | Under **$5m** (including under $100k) | **General + financial + audit**; starred conditional grant alternative may apply. |
| **Medium** | **$5m or more** | **General + financial + audit + directors' report**. |
| **Large** | Any CGOI | **General + financial + audit + directors' report**. |

**Exact statutory alternative, not a blanket grant exemption:** The short ORIC reporting tool refers to **more than 90% of total income from government grants**. The **operative CATSI Regulations 2017 r 27(2)** is more precise: for a corporation falling within **r 25**, an alternative set of financial-report materials is permitted only if **(a)** Australian Accounting Standards do **not** require it to prepare consolidated financial statements for that year; **(b)** **at least 90%** of the corporation's **gross operating income** for the year consists of funding from one or more **'funding bodies' as legally defined in r 5**, a category not simply the ordinary-language label 'government grants'; and **(c)** a condition of funding requires annual reports to those bodies accounting for expenditure over the **entire financial year**. When each condition is met, **r 27(3)** requires copies of the funder annual reports and any related audit reports, statements for otherwise-unreported income/expenditure and assets/liabilities, plus a directors' cash-flow solvency declaration. The full normal financial statement and audit requirements are not simply dispensed with without substitute evidence. If r 27(1) instead applies, **r 28** imposes an audit; the r 27(2) reporting option is distinct. Obtain accounting and ORIC confirmation, particularly when the corporation controls subsidiaries. [LAW-CATSIREG rr 5, 25, 27(1)–(3), 28; REG-ORIC-REPORT; REG-ORIC-FIN]

**Exact $100,000 boundary warning:** ORIC's reporting table labels small-corporation CGOI **$100,000 and above** as requiring financial and audit reports. However, the text of CATSI Regulations **r 25(1)(a)(ii)** applies its small-corporation financial-report subdivision where CGOI is **'more than $100,000'** and below $5 million; **r 19** covers the other small-corporation group only from **$5 million**. This leaves an apparent drafting/guidance discrepancy at **exactly $100,000**, not a broad exemption. Do **not** infer exemption or suppress reporting based on this handbook. Obtain the Registrar's written interpretation and current counsel/accountant advice in that narrow case. At all other threshold values, use the prescribed definitions, registered size and year-specific CGOI. [LAW-CATSIREG rr 19, 25; REG-ORIC-REPORT]

**Worked size-and-reporting tests:**

1. **Corporation A:** CGOI $80,000, assets $60,000, four employees. It meets all three small-size tests and, absent specific directions, ordinarily lodges **general report only**.
2. **Corporation B:** CGOI $6 million, assets $90,000, four employees. Despite high income, it meets **two small-size criteria**. The reporting matrix nevertheless places a small corporation with **CGOI $5 million or more** in the **four-report** category. This deliberately illustrates the *two-stage* nature of ORIC's tests.
3. **Corporation C:** CGOI $1.8 million, assets $1 million, nine employees. It meets three medium criteria; ordinarily the general, financial and audit reports apply. If potentially eligible under **all three conditions in r 27(2)**, it should obtain written ORIC/accountant confirmation of the alternative materials under r 27(3) before changing its reporting plan.
4. **Corporation D:** CGOI $70,000, assets $3 million, thirty employees. It meets **two large criteria**. The reporting obligation is **all four reports**, despite low CGOI.

These are constructed examples of the classification rules, not verified corporate data. They also show why an accountant should review definitions and year-specific classification rather than estimate reporting from the board's ordinary income statement alone. [LAW-CATSIREG rr 8–9, 19–28; REG-ORIC-SIZE; REG-ORIC-REPORT]

### 07.14.3 Content, timing and responsibilities

**General report:** CATSI **ss 330-1 and 330-5** cover the annual report about members, directors, officer/contact arrangements, addresses and prescribed information. RNTBC member registers must not be falsely represented as an exhaustive list of common law holders. **Financial report:** include statements, notes and directors' declaration where required (CATSI **ss 333-10/15**; CATSI Regulations Part 6); test accounting treatment, consolidation, restrictions, related parties and going concern. **Audit report:** required where applicable under CATSI **s 333-20**, regulations and any Registrar direction. **Directors' report:** required for specified categories, with content under the regulations. The Registrar has power to increase reporting for particular entities or classes (CATSI **ss 336-1, 336-5**) and can grant exemptions under relevant CATSI Part 7-4 provisions. [LAW-CATSI ss 327–342, 348; LAW-CATSIREG rr 17–28; REG-ORIC-FIN]

ORIC states that the usual financial year is **1 July–30 June**, annual reports for that year ordinarily are lodged **by 31 December**, and an AGM is ordinarily held **by 30 November**, unless a lawful exemption/extension or special first-year rule applies. The statutory general-report time in CATSI s **330-10(2)** is modified by CATSI Regulations **r 18**, so quoting s 330-10's raw three-month period without the prescribed extension would misstate the standard deadline. New corporations' **first financial year** requires careful application of CATSI rules and ORIC's updated **1 October 2026** guidance; directors should not assume the ordinary anniversary date. [LAW-CATSI ss 201, 330-10, 348; LAW-CATSIREG rr 18, 42; REG-ORIC-ANNUAL; REG-ORIC-FIRST]

Operational controls: calendar the AGM and every report; maintain separate member and common law holder records; reconcile corporate/subsidiary accounts; understand any trust and restricted liabilities; require a report-ready set of management accounts; commission audit sufficiently early; obtain director declarations and signed minutes; verify the submitted documents against the approved versions; and view the published ORIC register extract for any overdue return or administrative direction. Annual external filing is only the floor. The board needs timely management information throughout the year. [REG-ORIC-ANNUAL; AICD FIN 11–44; TOOL pp 3–11, 47–59]

### 07.14.4 ACNC charities: overlapping governance, generally separate reporting route

**CATSI registration does not create charity registration.** A corporation must separately hold charitable purpose and obtain registration with the Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission (ACNC) to be an ACNC-registered charity. If both CATSI and ACNC registered, the legal corporate obligations under CATSI continue. ACNC **Governance Standard 5** in the **ACNC Regulations 2022 s 45.25** requires the registered charity to take reasonable steps to ensure its Responsible People fulfil specified duties. This is an **entity-level charity standard** in addition to directly applicable CATSI duties; it is not a wholesale replacement of CATSI law or an automatic personal action under Corporations Act s 180. Other ACNC governance/external conduct requirements and charity purposes may apply. [LAW-ACNC; LAW-ACNCREG s 45.25; REG-ACNC-REPORT]

ACNC's ordinary charity size categories are **annual revenue**, not ORIC's two-of-three CGOI/assets/staff tests:

| ACNC charity size (2022 and later reporting periods) | Revenue band | Ordinary non-ORIC ACNC reporting (subject to applicable exceptions) |
|---|---|---|
| Small | **Under $500,000** | Annual Information Statement; financial report generally optional. |
| Medium | **$500,000 to under $3m** | Annual Information Statement + financial report; review **or audit**. |
| Large | **$3m or more** | Annual Information Statement + financial report; **audit**. |

The ACNC expressly states that charities **registered with ORIC** are **not required to submit an ACNC Annual Information Statement or annual financial report**: they report to ORIC instead. ORIC passes specified registry information to the ACNC, which is displayed on the Charity Register. This reporting alignment does **not** exempt them from ACNC charity registration requirements, governance standards, record-keeping, notifications not covered by information sharing, tax or fundraising obligations, or from funder and trustee obligations. The charity size bands remain useful for comparing charitable enterprises, but directors must not instruct an ORIC charity to duplicate an ACNC filing simply because its revenue exceeds $3 million. [REG-ACNC-REPORT; REG-ACNC-ORIC; REG-ACNC-SIZE]

**Hypothetical overlap:** A CATSI RNTBC operates a registered charitable youth programme with $900,000 annual revenue and manages $4 million of restricted native title funds via a separate trust. Its ACNC revenue could place it in the **medium charity band** if tested on those revenues, while its **ORIC classification and reporting** must be calculated from CATSI definitions, controlled entities, assets and staff. The restricted native title fund is not automatically charity programme money; the charity's eligibility, income recognition, financial aggregation and trust entitlement require precise entity-specific analysis. It ordinarily reports to ORIC rather than duplicating ACNC annual returns. [ANALYSIS; REG-ACNC-REPORT; REG-ORIC-SIZE]

### 07.14.5 Director's annual reporting control sheet

| Control | Evidence to request | Escalation signal |
|---|---|---|
| Corporation identity and size | Current ORIC extract; three size-test workings; controlled-entity list | Size assumed from CGOI alone; changed staff/assets not reflected |
| Annual CGOI | Reconciliation of gross income by controlled entity and accounting period | Unexplained project receipts, inter-entity transfers, trust attribution |
| Required return types | Size + CGOI matrix; Registrar direction/exemption; grant alternative decision | Audit omitted because “we are small” or “mostly grant funded” |
| Governance records | Updated member/director records; AGM notice/quorum/minutes | Holder registry incorrectly treated as member list or vice versa |
| Financial position | Restriction ledger, cash flow forecast, debt and grant commitments, related-party disclosures | Cash available only because of trust/grant funds; solvency uncertainty |
| ACNC status | Charity Register record, purposes, Governance Standard 5 controls, reporting exemption | Presumed charity solely from CATSI incorporation; missed purpose restriction |
| Approvals and publication | Board-approved signed reports, auditor opinion, ORIC receipt, public extract | Filed version differs from directors' declaration; overdue flags |

Board oversight is not satisfied by a compliance dashboard marked green without the **underlying size calculation and exemption basis**. The board should obtain specialist accountant/ORIC advice when close to thresholds, where controlled trusts or subsidiaries change consolidation, or when a late report could affect funding or community confidence. [AICD FIN 14–44; REG-ORIC-ANNUAL]

## 07.15 Effective meetings, accessible information, translation, independent advice, consent records and culturally legitimate director practice

### 07.15.1 Design the meeting for the authority it must exercise

A **directors' meeting** under CATSI, a **general meeting of members** under CATSI, a **native title common law holder consent process** under PBC r 8 or r 8B, and a **cultural meeting or consultation** may involve overlapping people but do **not** automatically have the same purpose, legal quorum, procedures or authority. Agenda notices must say which process is being convened, the decision proposed, who can participate and decide, and what evidence will show its outcome. A combined event may be practical but requires separate, legally compliant agendas and records. An AGM notice to members will not reach all non-member common law holders or supply native title consent by itself. [LAW-CATSI Ch 5; LAW-PBC rr 8–10; REG-ORIC-DM]

**Legal baseline:** Corporate meeting notices, member rights, voting and directors' proceedings are governed by the CATSI Act and the registered rule book. Native title consent must meet the applicable r 8(3) traditional-law process **where it must be followed**, otherwise the relevant holders' agreed or adopted process under r 8(4), or a qualifying r 8A/standing-instructions path. There is no universally prescribed community-wide consensus rule, one-person-one-vote rule, automatic elder veto, default headcount quorum or single “traditional meeting” format. The AICD's treatment of First Nations consensus and respectful deliberation is useful teaching, but is not a determination of how any particular rights-holding group must decide. [LAW-PBC rr 8–8B; AICD BE 79; AICD RISK 16–18]

### 07.15.2 Accessibility is decision assurance

The practical standard is **a meaningful opportunity to understand and exercise actual authority**. Depending on the group's own preferences and participation barriers, evidence may support local-language interpretation, plain-language summaries, visual maps, separate women/men or other culturally appropriate sessions **only where requested or locally justified**, travel support, digital participation alternatives, accessible venues, disability accommodations, childcare, advance delivery of documents and enough time for internal discussion. These are possible practices, not manufactured universal customs or necessarily universal statutory entitlements. Independent advisers should be accountable to the holders they advise and disclose who pays them. [AIGI-EFFECTIVE; AIGI-CULTURE; LAW-PBC r 8]

The board should commission a **decision-access plan** identifying (a) target decision-maker population and any affected classes, (b) communication channels tested with the community, (c) delivery evidence, (d) translation quality/control and version numbering, (e) conflicts and advocacy support, (f) sensitive material limits, (g) meeting methods the holders accept, and (h) independent verification. When large transaction documents are confidential, use legally robust confidentiality undertakings or controlled access rather than withhold material risks from those required to decide. A summary of benefits that omits surrender clauses, releases, environmental obligations or costs is not adequate decision information. [LAW-PBC rr 8–9; AIATSIS-CODE; ANALYSIS]

### 07.15.3 Notes and records that protect both process and sensitive information

**Corporate minutes:** date, place/channel, attendees, quorum, material interests and participation restrictions, resolutions, vote and dissent where necessary, relied-upon papers, legal advice and assigned actions. **Holder process record:** relevant area and classes, how participants' authority was established, meetings and other consultations, information provided, legally operative decision procedure, actual consent and conditions, evidence of standing instructions if used, material challenges, r 9 certificate and access procedures under r 10. **Cultural record:** record only what community-authorised people agree can be recorded and circulated; do not reproduce restricted knowledge to make an audit file appear complete. [LAW-CATSI s 220-5; LAW-PBC rr 8–10; AIATSIS-CODE; TOOL pp 118–123]

PBC **r 9(3)** requires details including names of participants in the certificate; the regulation also expressly permits limited collection of personal information for this purpose under **r 9(5)**. This is not permission to publish all participants' personal, family or cultural information freely. Give a certificate to eligible written requesters under **r 10**, while preserving appropriate protections for underlying records and seeking advice if public disclosure, privacy or cultural confidentiality duties collide. An effective secretariat maintains a confidential evidence chain and a public-facing account of the process at a level the holders authorise. [LAW-PBC rr 9(3), 9(5)–(7), 10; LAW-CATSIREG r 55A]

### 07.15.4 Example: legitimate input, insufficient authorisation

**Hypothetical:** Elders give a project negotiator permission to start exploratory talks. A director records “Elders approved project” and the proponent assumes a land-use agreement can be signed. The problem is not whether Elders have authority in that community. It is that **the scope and legal source of the instruction have not been established**: exploratory contact may be permitted without authorising final native title consequences, and the relevant holders' r 8 decision process may not have occurred. The corrective decision is to document the preliminary mandate, ask those with locally recognised authority how the legally required process operates, obtain legal advice, and separate negotiation from execution. It is inappropriate to reinterpret an ambiguous discussion as consent after the event. [LAW-PBC r 8; AIGI-CULTURE; ANALYSIS]

**Practical director self-test:** Can each intended decision-maker explain, in an accessible way, what can happen without the agreement, what is irrevocable, how money will be held, who will benefit, who can challenge and what the corporation is authorised to sign? Could a holder outside membership challenge the completeness of the decision record? Are legal criteria satisfied even if consensus cannot be achieved? Have directors sought specialised advice rather than deciding what the community's customary protocol “must have been”? [LAW-PBC rr 8–10]

## 07.16 Disputes, procedural fairness, rule-book mechanisms, community relationships and relevant legal remedies

### 07.16.1 Identify which relationship has broken down

Disputes may concern corporate membership or elections, director conduct, family relationships, native title entitlement, validity of holder consultation, benefit distribution, cultural heritage advice, project contracts or regulator compliance. The response depends on the **right at issue** and the **relationship** involved. A dispute among corporation members is not automatically a determination of native title identity; a native title decision challenge is not resolved simply by recounting votes at an AGM. Family/community differences may be longstanding and legitimate. Directors should not label all opposition “disgruntled members” or discredit a claimant because they live away from Country. [LAW-CATSI Chs 4–5; LAW-PBC rr 4, 8–10; REG-ORIC-DISPUTES]

**CATSI rule books must contain dispute resolution rules**. ORIC's public guidance differentiates governance **disputes** concerning compliance with CATSI/rule book from broader interpersonal **conflicts** for which ORIC's role is generally limited. For an RNTBC, the rule book must also provide for disputes with a common law holder or person claiming that status about the person's status or the corporation's native title functions. These rights can matter particularly where a complainant is **not a corporation member**. The person ordinarily activates the relevant rule-book process first, but urgent court relief may be appropriate where rights are at immediate risk. [REG-ORIC-DISPUTES; REG-ORIC-RULE; LAW-CATSI required-rules provisions]

### 07.16.2 Procedural fairness and preservation

On receiving a serious challenge, the board should: identify the issue without prejudging it; disclose the correct process and time limits; appoint an unconflicted case officer or external facilitator; give the person a fair opportunity to provide relevant information; protect against retaliation; seek consent for use of sensitive evidence; give an intelligible response with reasons to the extent legally appropriate; and document options for appeal/review. Procedural fairness as a legal obligation depends on the decision type and applicable public/private law, but these are robust governance practices. A corporate membership rejection should be checked against exact eligibility and rules; an oral claim of cultural authority should be treated respectfully but tested through the recognised process rather than simply accepted or dismissed. [AIGI-EFFECTIVE; REG-ORIC-DISPUTES; LAW-CATSI Ch 4]

If a dispute challenges a proposed ILUA, mark the transaction **amber/red** immediately, preserve draft/version histories, holder notices, consultation records and objections, and ask counsel whether to defer execution, registration or an impending statutory deadline. Record the concerns factually; do not erase comments from minutes or retrospectively reconstruct a consent certificate. One participant's objection does not automatically invalidate a lawful group decision, but significant evidence that a required holder class was omitted or the wrong decision process applied is a substantial legal risk. [LAW-PBC rr 8–10; LAW-NTA ILUA provisions; REG-NNTT-REGISTER]

### 07.16.3 Remedies and regulators: capacities are limited

**Rule-book remedies** may include facilitated discussion, internal review, independent mediation or another locally designed pathway. **ORIC** may examine alleged CATSI/rule-book noncompliance, issue compliance notices, call meetings or exercise other statutory regulatory functions. ORIC does **not** automatically determine disputed native title holders or adjudicate all contractual or cultural disagreements. Under **CATSI Regulations r 55A**, the Registrar can give an opinion concerning a PBC r 9 certificate's compliance when properly requested by relevant persons; such an opinion is **not itself a court declaration of holder rights**. [REG-ORIC-DISPUTES; LAW-CATSIREG r 55A; LAW-PBC rr 9–10]

**Courts** may address statutory validity, injunctions, declarations, oppressive conduct or other civil remedies depending on the pleaded cause, standing, factual evidence and relevant law. CATSI **s 166-1** contains a statutory oppressive-conduct route, with applicable remedies/standing in the relevant Part. Trust beneficiaries may have trustee remedies for breach of deed or general law. The **Federal Court** has central roles under the Native Title Act including determinations, compensation and some agreement issues; the NNTT has statutory registration, mediation and future-act functions. None should be presented as a universal, cost-free complaints forum. Legal advice should assess standing, limitation periods, urgency, injunction criteria, costs and alternative dispute resolution. [LAW-CATSI s 166-1 onward; LAW-NTA Parts 2–3; REG-NNTT-ILUA]

**Conflict escalation tool:**

```text
Dispute received
 -> Classify membership / corporate / native title / trust / heritage / contract.
 -> Identify persons entitled to use the appropriate rule-book pathway.
 -> Preserve evidence; control conflicted decision-makers and confidentiality.
 -> Test urgent risk: impending signing, registration, expenditure or heritage harm?
      -> YES: stop or obtain urgent independent legal advice and consider protective relief.
      -> NO: run the locally applicable rule-book process fairly and promptly.
 -> If unresolved, select ORIC / NNTT / mediator / court / funder or trustee process
    based on legal subject matter and regulator jurisdiction.
 -> Record outcome and systemic governance improvements; verify acceptance
    without misrepresenting the outcome as universal community consensus.
```

The board should monitor patterns rather than the raw number of complaints alone: repeated membership denial, unexplained travel barriers, inaccessible accounts, exclusion of non-member holders, conflicts that recur in procurement, persistent grievances over benefit distribution and staff turnover can indicate structural legitimacy issues. A board that maintains legal paperwork but loses broad community confidence needs to evaluate its governance model, while respecting the independently enforceable rights of specific persons. [AIGI-EFFECTIVE; REG-ORIC-DISPUTES]

## 07.17 Real and properly attributed public examples; clearly hypothetical illustrations where necessary

### 07.17.1 AIGI's publicly described examples, not universal templates

**Kanyirninpa Jukurrpa (KJ), Martu Country, Western Australia.** AIGI's *KJ governance story*, most recently updated **December 2024**, describes KJ's purpose as strengthening and sustaining Martu communities through cultural, land-management and social programmes. AIGI identifies KJ as a **company limited by guarantee incorporated under ASIC**, not simply as a CATSI corporation. Its *Culture-smart governance* Toolkit describes a board of Martu directors inviting Senior Cultural Advisors/Elders into board deliberations. The governance lesson is **institutional design can acknowledge culturally knowledgeable authority without claiming every advisor is the corporation's statutory director**. It does **not** establish how a different group should choose Elders or approve an ILUA. [AIGI-KJ, Dec 2024; AIGI-CULTURE]

**The Arnhem Land Progress Aboriginal Corporation (ALPA).** AIGI's *Effective Indigenous governance* Toolkit describes ALPA embedding representation in its constitution, with an elected representative identified as a traditional landowner and a community representative, and using culturally attentive board communication and liaison. The published ALPA “hat” teaching exercise distinguished duties to the entity from a director's other personal obligations. It is a reported **ALPA-specific** example, not a legal power to disregard kin relationships, nor evidence of a uniform representation formula across First Nations. Verify ALPA's current constitution and any changes before adopting its specific arrangements. [AIGI-EFFECTIVE, public Toolkit page accessed 27 Oct 2026]

**Miriuwung Gajerrong (MG) Corporation.** In AIGI's *Defining governance* Toolkit, **Helen Gerrard, a director speaking in 2012**, described an arrangement involving Garralyel, Dawang Council and a board, with differentiated cultural, strategic and corporate roles. This is a **historically dated, publicly attributed account** of how that community organisation explained governance at the time. It does not purport to be a 2026 rule-book extract, a current list of powers or a template that outsiders may apply elsewhere. The lesson is to **map who holds what authority in the actual governance system** rather than assume a board is the only source of legitimate leadership. [AIGI-GOV, MG Corporation 2012 account]

**Northern Territory v Griffiths [2019] HCA 7** is a judicial, not a community protocol, example. The High Court's approach to compensation recognises distinct economic, interest and cultural components in the facts of the Timber Creek matter. It supports examining the nature of loss before allocating compensation. It does not prove a standard benefit formula or amount for other communities. [CASE-GRIFFITHS, High Court 13 March 2019]

**Northern Land Council v Quall [2020] HCA 33** arose in the separate statutory system governing a native title representative body's certification functions and whether they could be delegated to the body's chief executive under the relevant Native Title Act provisions. It is a valuable warning against treating “the CEO can sign” as an unexamined general rule. It is **not a holding that an RNTBC CEO may replace common law holder consent under PBC r 8**. Always identify the institution, statutory power and decision being delegated. [CASE-QUALL, High Court 7 Oct 2020]

## 07.18 Full RNTBC due-diligence and decision procedure for a proposed native title agreement

### 07.18.1 Purpose, standard of assurance and first decision

This procedure is intended for a director, company secretary, external legal reviewer or holder-authorised advisory group assessing a proposed ILUA, section 31 agreement or comparable agreement affecting native title. It must be tailored to the specific determination, decision type, parties and law. Its objective is **not to maximise the number of approvals**; it is to establish that the **right people** exercised the **right authority**, on the **right information**, through the **required process**, and that the resulting financial and other commitments can actually be administered. [LAW-NTA Parts 2–3; LAW-PBC rr 3, 8–10; REG-ORIC-DM]

At intake, the board should choose one of four limited mandates: **(1) decline to engage**, **(2) authorise preliminary information exchange**, **(3) authorise negotiation subject to reserved consent**, or **(4) approve final corporate execution after all legal preconditions are evidenced**. These are distinct decisions. An early negotiating mandate should state expressly that **no final native title holder consent has yet been represented or obtained**, unless that is independently established. A chief executive's or negotiator's authority to discuss price is not authority to agree a surrender. [LAW-CATSI ss 265, 274-1; LAW-PBC r 8]

### 07.18.2 Stage-gate due-diligence checklist

The following **nineteen gates** should be recorded in a transaction ledger. “Must” refers to a legal requirement when cited; otherwise the item is expressly a proposed governance control. Any unresolved legal gate should block an unconditional board commitment.

**Gate 01 — Verify the legal entity.** Obtain the current ORIC extract, registered name, Indigenous Corporation Number (ICN), incorporation status, actual rule book and all amendments, directors and representatives, objects, delegation, pending ORIC notices and any ACNC charity registration. Identify each legal entity in a corporate group separately. Check whether the corporation is actually the RNTBC for **this** determination; never infer it from a related group name or officeholders. **Evidence:** authenticated register extract, rule book, board/secretariat certificate. **Actors:** corporate board and legal counsel. [LAW-CATSI Chs 2–5; REG-ORIC-DM]

**Gate 02 — Verify determination and tenure.** Obtain the sealed Federal Court determination, maps/schedules, National Native Title Register extract and PBC trustee/agent orders. Record recognised rights, exclusive/non-exclusive status, affected area, holder classes, other overlapping determinations, prescribed procedural rights, neighbouring projects, historic extinguishment and Crown tenure. Use a qualified spatial specialist; store culturally sensitive overlays securely. **Evidence:** counsel-approved tenure/title map with uncertainty notes. **Actors:** native title legal team, authorised mapping knowledge holders. [LAW-NTA ss 55–59, 223, 225; LAW-PBC rr 4–5]

**Gate 03 — Identify the proposed future act and authority route.** Is the proponent seeking a grant of mining title, infrastructure construction, access permission, surrender, variation of land use, ongoing heritage service or retrospective regularisation? Which Native Title Act future-act provision applies? Is the agreement intended as body corporate, area or alternative-procedure ILUA, or s 31 right-to-negotiate agreement? Some contracts may combine routes and require separate instruments. **Evidence:** statutory pathway memorandum naming notice, consent and registration provisions. **Actors:** independent native title counsel. [LAW-NTA ss 24BA–24DM, 29–31; REG-NNTT-ILUA]

**Gate 04 — Identify affected holders, not simply members.** Map the people and classes whose determined native title rights the proposed acts affect. Different sites or kinds of rights may involve different holder classes. Identify holders living away, nonmembers, young people or groups potentially affected without assuming who may speak under customary law. Do not disclose restricted genealogy in the open corporate register. **Evidence:** appropriately authorised holder identification process, determination crosswalk, confidential record of scope and reasonable efforts. **Actors:** holders, RNTBC and independent counsel. [LAW-PBC r 8(3)–(4); REG-ORIC-HOLD]

**Gate 05 — Establish the legally applicable decision process.** Ask whether the relevant affected group has a specific decision process under its traditional laws and customs **which must be followed for that consent** (r 8(3)). If so, establish it respectfully through authorised evidence; **do not invent it** or rely on outsider assumptions. Otherwise establish the holders' agreed/adopted procedure under r 8(4). Document an actual process for this type of decision, including who decides, what outcome means consent, how representatives are authorised and how challenges are handled. An AGM majority is insufficient unless the actual regulation requirements independently make it appropriate. **Evidence:** privileged legal/process advice and community-approved process record. [LAW-PBC r 8(3)–(4); AIGI-CULTURE]

**Gate 06 — Test exceptional approval routes strictly.** If someone proposes standing instructions, inspect the decision type against **PBC r 3**: low-level, an ILUA for an act by or for the PBC's benefit, or an s 31 agreement where the PBC is the **only grantee party**. Find the prior holder approval **under r 8(8)**, relevant classes, restrictions, expiration and any revocation/change under r 8(9). For a low-level decision under r 8A, verify that the precise consultation process is in the rule book and relevant holders previously consented to its inclusion. A general board delegation, committee charter, ancient minute or membership admission is not proof of those statutory requirements. **Evidence:** process audit. [LAW-PBC rr 3, 8(2), 8(8)–(9), 8A]

**Gate 07 — Identify other legally separate authorities.** Map compulsory heritage, environmental, planning, Aboriginal land-rights, state mining, water and cultural-knowledge law. Check shareholder/member approvals, trust deeds, charity purpose restrictions, related-party benefits and government/funder consents. For community-held land, distinguish whether a title-holding trust, statutory land council or local body has its own approval role. **Evidence:** multi-law permits/approvals matrix showing jurisdiction, section, actor, stage and expiry. **Actors:** appropriate statutory and local independent advisers. [LAW-CATSI Ch 6; LAW-NTA Parts 2–3; relevant state/territory legislation]

**Gate 08 — Define the decision envelope before asking for consent.** Separate: project footprint and permitted activities; surrender/extinguishment; duration, renewals and successors; cultural-heritage access; environmental/rehabilitation conditions; information use; financial payments; taxation; releases and indemnities; assignment/change of control; dispute and suspension rights. Identify the *most consequential term*, not only headline cash. Use marked-up drafts, maps and alternative scenarios. **Evidence:** versioned term sheet approved for consultation, plain-language risk sheet and independent lawyer's issues log. [LAW-PBC r 8; AIATSIS-CODE]

**Gate 09 — Evaluate alternatives and no-deal position.** Compare the legal outcome if the RNTBC does not agree: government or tribunal procedures, project route changes, whether the future act can legally proceed, delay, negotiation power, heritage rights, opportunities and costs. Do not tell holders that an agreement is the only option unless counsel has verified that proposition. Consider counterproposals, limited-duration options, staged consent, tighter footprint or an independent benefits trust. **Evidence:** choices paper, legal advice, commercial scenarios. [LAW-NTA ss 24BA–24EB, 29–31; AICD RISK 38–70]

**Gate 10 — Independent financial and risk assessment.** Value separately fixed payments, contingent production-linked amounts, noncash commitments, jobs, contracting and cultural management, then consider payment timing, risk of default, inflation and transfer of the project. Stress-test commodity price, construction delay, cancellation, environmental failure and counterparty insolvency where relevant. Identify compliance costs borne by the RNTBC over the agreement's life, including holder meetings and independent audits. **Evidence:** assumptions book, scenarios, independent verification of material formulas and obligations. **Actors:** qualified financial adviser accountable to the decision-makers; legal counsel for interpretation. [ANALYSIS; AICD FIN 14–22; AICD RISK 71–86]

**Gate 11 — Benefits ownership and architecture.** Who receives money legally: RNTBC, common law holders, separate trustee, community charity, subsidiary or controlled account? Is the RNTBC trustee of native title, agent, or trustee **also** of benefits? Specify permitted use; direct/indirect distributions; relevant beneficiaries; eligibility; representation; investment powers; administration fees; intergenerational provision; audit; removal of trustees; and disputes. Distinguish compensation for extinguishment from community programme support. **Evidence:** deed/waterfall and legal title schedule; trust/beneficiary advice; treasury controls. [LAW-PBC rr 6–7; REG-ORIC-DM]

**Gate 12 — Conflict and related-party diligence.** Request transaction-specific interests from directors, advisers, negotiators and procurement/benefit committee members. Check project jobs, sponsorships, contracting opportunities, family-owned businesses and success fees. Determine any CATSI **ss 268 and 284–287** approval, unrelated funder restriction or charity duty. Design a process that is lawful without assuming all relationships are improper. **Evidence:** declarations, conflict management and independent market assessment, member approvals where required. [LAW-CATSI ss 265, 268, 284–287]

**Gate 13 — Consultation logistics and access.** Provide the actual affected holders accessible information about irreversible consequences, financial restrictions and alternatives, in arrangements they decide are meaningful. Document notice channels, translation, site maps, adequate timing, travel/digital access, independent advice and safe avenues for concern. Keep member-only meeting records separate from holder decision records. **Evidence:** consultation plan, versions, notice delivery, participation and responses, confidentiality design. [LAW-PBC r 8; AIGI-EFFECTIVE; AIATSIS-CODE]

**Gate 14 — Consent decision and conditions.** Conduct the **actual legally applicable process** and document its results. A valid process may use consensus, specified customary decision-makers or another holder-adopted method as facts require; do not impose generic corporate majority voting. Confirm that the **exact agreement version** is within the scope of what holders approved. Record conditions, abstentions and dissent in a manner consistent with the agreed process and confidentiality rules. Where a critical class was omitted, documents were misleading or the outcome disputed, **stop** and obtain independent advice, even if the board voted unanimously. **Evidence:** dated confidential decision record and counsel's process-compliance sign-off. [LAW-PBC r 8(3)–(4)]

**Gate 15 — PBC regulation 9 certificate.** As soon as practicable **after** the native title decision, prepare the written certificate. It must address the applicable r 9(2) pathway, date, details of decision-making, **names** of persons participating, the holder consultation/consent process or qualifying standing approval, and valid CATSI **s 99-5** corporate execution **or** chief executive signature under r 9(4). It is **prima facie evidence**, not conclusive proof. Confirm how requests for copies under r 10 will be handled. The CATSI Registrar may consider compliance under **CATSI Regulations r 55A**; the corporation should retain underlying evidence. **Evidence:** executed certificate and secure consent bundle. [LAW-PBC rr 9–10; LAW-CATSIREG r 55A]

**Gate 16 — Corporate authority and signature.** Now obtain a proper board resolution: recitals of authority and holder consent; final identified agreement and schedules; any separate member or trust consent; related-party process; financial affordability; delegation/signature mechanics under **CATSI s 99-5**; and clear implementation ownership. A directors' authorisation alone is not holder consent; holder consent alone is not necessarily a CATSI execution instrument. **Evidence:** board paper, conflict register, resolutions, signature check and legal opinion on no remaining conditions. [LAW-CATSI ss 99-5, 220-5, 268, 274-1; LAW-PBC r 8]

**Gate 17 — Registration and third-party procedural review.** If ILUA registration is required, lodge using the appropriate **Native Title (Indigenous Land Use Agreements) Regulations 2024** form and NNTT process, including RNTBC determination evidence and r 9 certificate **when required**. NNTT guidance identifies one-month notification for **body corporate** ILUAs and three-month periods for **area/alternative-procedure** ILUAs, with particular grounds for objection. Track objections and statutory certification/authorisation without conflating them with PBC r 9. If a section 31 agreement, verify section 29 notice, native title parties, negotiation conditions and any approvals/filing relevant to that route. **Evidence:** application/receipt, registrar queries, notice expiry, final register extract or other operative instrument. [LAW-NTA ss 24BA–24EB, 29–31, 251A; LAW-ILUAREG; REG-NNTT-REGISTER]

**Gate 18 — Completion, controlled funds and obligations.** Establish conditions precedent and execution closing checklist; ensure no funds transfer to the wrong beneficiary or account. Record any trust establishment, trustee identity, bank controls, investment direction process, insurance, tax advice and disbursement schedule. Populate a lifecycle register for payment verification, project impacts, heritage management, conditions, successor obligations, reporting, information access and renegotiation. **Evidence:** fully executed documents, legal completion opinion, bank/ledger proof, trust records, contractor security and monitoring dashboard. [LAW-PBC rr 6–7; LAW-CATSI s 265-1; REG-ORIC-DM]

**Gate 19 — Independent post-decision assurance and learning.** Compare actual outcomes with assumptions: payments received, jobs actually delivered, cultural protections operating, rehabilitation issues, disputes, changes in holder representation and administrative cost. Permit entitled holders to receive meaningful accounts of benefits, subject to confidentiality. Hold a debrief using AIGI's principles: is governance customised, culturally legitimate, accountable in both directions and capable of adapting? Escalate serious breaches promptly. **Evidence:** annual independent compliance review or proportionate assurance plan, holder-facing report and board corrective-action tracker. [AIGI-EFFECTIVE; AICD BE 90–104; TOOL pp 27–35]

### 07.18.4 Exact rehearsal: test the board resolution versus affected holder consent

**Test A — INVALID AS AN AUTHORITY DEMONSTRATION (hypothetical).** A validly appointed RNTBC board passes **resolution 2026-14** unanimously authorising a 25-year mining access ILUA. A member general meeting also passes a valid corporate special resolution. The decision is made without consultation of twenty nonmember common law holders recognised in the relevant determination, including one class with rights in the affected area. A lawyer is told to produce a “regulation 9 certificate” based on the board minutes. **Result:** The available evidence does **not** establish PBC r 8(3)–(4) holder consultation/consent; corporate membership is the wrong population and r 9 cannot manufacture compliance. Directors must withhold assertion of completed native title authority, obtain independent advice on proper holder process and do not make a false certificate. If any party argues the ILUA's registration saves its legal effect, test r 8(6)–(7) carefully; **a possible statutory effect exception is not proof the RNTBC complied**. [LAW-PBC rr 3, 8(1), 8(3)–(7), 9; REG-ORIC-HOLD]

**Test B — POTENTIALLY SUFFICIENT, SUBJECT TO OTHER GATES (hypothetical).** The determination names three classes; counsel maps the proposal and confirms that two classes' rights would be affected. It is established, using appropriately authorised evidence, that the affected holders must use a particular process under traditional laws and customs for this consent. The affected holders follow **that** process, with adequate project information, and record valid consent including limits on disturbance and an annual payment formula. The RNTBC prepares a compliant **r 9** certificate after the decision, and the unconflicted board properly approves execution of the **same version**, with valid s 99-5 signatures, trustee arrangements and necessary ILUA party/registration steps. **Result:** The native title-holder/board distinction is appropriately recognised. **But** this does not itself prove cultural-heritage permits, project environmental approvals, absence of conflicting beneficiary claims or registration completion. The final legal opinion must verify all other specific gates. [LAW-PBC rr 8–10; LAW-CATSI s 99-5; LAW-NTA ss 24BA–24EB]

**Test C — VALID PROCESS, WRONG TRANSACTION (hypothetical).** The holders validly approve a limited exploration access agreement with no surrender. During final negotiation the proponent inserts a clause permanently surrendering rights over a road corridor and expands the project footprint. The board votes to accept the revised commercial value. **Result:** The holder's original approval cannot simply be extended by a later corporate vote to a materially different **high-level native title decision**. The director must identify the change, obtain appropriate holder decision authority for the revised scope and redo relevant certification/documentation before signing. [LAW-PBC rr 3, 8–9]

**Test D — HOLDER DECISION APPROVED; FINANCIAL SPENDING STILL BLOCKED (hypothetical).** A valid agreement promises $5 million in a benefits trust for descendants and cultural programmes. The RNTBC's directors agree to divert $1 million to pay unrelated corporation debts. **Result:** Even assuming the ILUA consent was valid, the board has not shown a valid trust deed power or relevant directions for this diversion; if the RNTBC holds native title money in trust, PBC rr 6–7 also matter. The directors should require trustee and beneficiary authority analysis and an independent insolvency response instead of assuming all funds belong to the corporation. [LAW-PBC rr 6–7; LAW-CATSI ss 265, 531-1]

### 07.18.5 Board paper recommendation template (original)

A final paper for an ILUA should require management or an authorised RNTBC negotiator to complete the following *separate propositions*:

1. **What is sought?** Describe the act, agreement type, area, rights, surrender, economic commitments and duration.
2. **Who has legal authority?** State the RNTBC's legal identity, trustee/agent status, exact determination and affected holder classes, including non-members.
3. **What is the legal holder process?** Attach an opinion identifying PBC r 8(3) or (4), any properly conditioned r 8A or standing approval and whether r 8B is separately relevant.
4. **What is the evidence of consent?** Give meeting/notice and representation evidence, outcome, conditions, legal challenge assessment and r 9 certificate. Do not circulate restricted cultural proof indiscriminately.
5. **What is the economic and non-economic assessment?** Put cash versus in-kind benefits, no-deal option, restrictions, downside scenarios, Country impacts and intergenerational effects side by side.
6. **Whose funds and liabilities?** Map legal and beneficial ownership; trust deeds, grant restrictions, financial controls, management cost and solvency.
7. **What has been independently checked?** Legal, financial, tax, heritage/environment, confidentiality/data and counterparty advice with dates and unresolved matters.
8. **What can directors actually decide today?** State only the corporate authority sought, subject to specific holder, trust, statutory and registration conditions.
9. **What will be recorded?** Minute advice, conflicts, votes, holder consent link, resolution text, actual signed version, rejection/abstention and conditions.
10. **What will the board monitor?** Named owners for payment audit, rights and heritage commitments, fund disbursement, community accountability, complaint handling and renegotiation.

A cautious resolution might approve **execution subject to counsel certifying specific completed preconditions**, but should not misrepresent the future obtaining of holder consent as though it had already occurred. Where the act of “making the decision” itself requires prior consent, the board should not purport to make that native title decision before consent and attempt to cure it solely through a later signature hold. Distinguish **authorisation to negotiate**, **holder decision**, **board approval to execute** and **actual execution** in both paper and minute. [LAW-PBC r 8(1), r 9; LAW-CATSI ss 99-5, 220-5, 274-1]

## 07.19 Legal developments, unresolved questions and circumstances requiring specialised independent advice

### 07.19.1 Research cut-off and change controls

This handbook uses law and official guidance checked as at **27 October 2026**. A legislative *compilation date* is not the same as the date this handbook was checked; an AICD 2025/26 course page is not evidence that the legal text remained unchanged. The Federal Register of Legislation identifies the latest CATSI Act compilation as **5 December 2025 (C27)**, the Native Title Act compilation as **12 June 2024 (C50)**, CATSI Regulations as **14 October 2024 (C07)** and PBC Regulations as **14 October 2024 (C05)** on the accessed live registers. Review any subsequently made, uncommenced or applied amendments and transitional provisions **before** advising on a live transaction. [LAW-CATSI; LAW-NTA; LAW-CATSIREG; LAW-PBC]

In particular, ORIC's **RNTBC decision-making** guidance shows **23 October 2024** as its last-update date (checked 27 October 2026), whereas ORIC's **first financial year** guidance was updated **1 October 2026**. The 2024 NNTT ILUA Regulations require current registration forms. An older 2016 AICD financial checklist and the **March 2024** legal appendix comparison of statutory duties are historical educational sources and not reliable substitutes for up-to-date legislation. [REG-ORIC-DM; REG-ORIC-FIRST; LAW-ILUAREG; AICD LAPP 5; AICD FIFTY 2016]

### 07.19.2 Situations that require specialised advice

Obtain **independent native title counsel** where the determination classes, overlap, beneficiaries or geographic extent are unclear; where a proposed activity involves surrender or extensive future acts; where authority comes from a claimed traditional decision process that outsiders cannot properly determine; where a standing-approval exception is asserted; when opponents dispute consent or statutory registration; and before concluding that PBC r 8(7) changes legal remedies. Counsel should explicitly identify *which* Native Title Act and PBC provisions apply to the actual decision and affected persons. [LAW-NTA ss 24BA–24EB, 55–61; LAW-PBC rr 3, 8–10]

Obtain **trust and compensation counsel** when money may have multiple beneficiaries, historical extinguishment, multiple determinations, overlapping benefit trusts, estate/descendant claims or restrictive deed terms; when a trustee proposes borrowing, lending, investing or distributing significant sums; and when the financial statements' consolidated treatment is inconsistent with legal ownership. Obtain **independent accounting/tax advice** for ORIC size/CGOI, charity overlap, grant acquittals, GST, related-party disclosures, audit scope, legal solvency and large investments. [LAW-PBC rr 6–8B; LAW-CATSIREG rr 8–9, 19–28; REG-ORIC-REPORT]

Obtain **specialist heritage, environmental and Indigenous cultural knowledge advice** where a project affects sacred/restricted information, water, heritage landscapes, intangible cultural property, scientific use, recordings, data-sharing, artificial intelligence training or access to Country. The statutory decision-maker under the relevant state's heritage law is not presumed identical to the PBC or holder group. Obtain **independent dispute and litigation advice** where deadlines or irreversibility make ordinary mediation insufficient. [AIATSIS-CODE; GIDA-CARE; relevant jurisdiction-specific law]

### 07.19.3 Open research questions and responsible uncertainty

The correct answer for any specific RNTBC remains fact-dependent: its **sealed determination**, registered rule book, affected holders/classes, locally established decision process, actual trust/benefit deeds, current statutory route, project contracts and third-party permissions are not supplied in this generic handbook. Some public contracts and cultural protocols are intentionally confidential. They are therefore **unresolved by design**, not missing facts to be invented. The biggest error would be converting a published governance illustration into a presumed right to speak for an unconsulted community. [AIGI-TOOLKIT; LAW-PBC r 8]

**Monitoring triggers:** amendments to CATSI/PBC law or ORIC policy; NNTT ILUA registration practice or significant judicial decisions on authorisation; native title determination variation; corporate rule-book changes; new or revoked standing instructions; grant or trust deed changes; financial size or audit changes; retirement/succession of key authorised representatives; project ownership changes; and a material complaint about consultation, misuse of benefit funds or cultural knowledge. Each change triggers a new authority check. [ANALYSIS]

| Minutes and monitoring | Conflicts, exact documents and version, vote, conditions, delegated signer, accountabilities and next holder report. |

**Reject a paper** that leaves the decision type blank or describes the authority only as “the community supports the proposal”. This phrase is neither a statutory authorisation record nor sufficiently attributable evidence of affected rights-holder consent. [LAW-PBC rr 3, 8–10; LAW-CATSI Chs 4–6; ANALYSIS]

### C.2 Restricted-fund control and reconciled liquidity sheet (original)

At every financial review show at least: **bank cash**; **cash held subject to grant or trust restrictions**; **amounts due to beneficiaries**; **unrestricted operating cash**; **restricted funding due to be spent in the next 90 days**; **committed payroll and tax**; **13-week minimum unrestricted cash headroom**; and **named approval authority for each restricted class**. Reconcile the amounts to the general ledger and to every relevant deed/agreement, not merely to the external balance sheet.

A board's allocation of a cost to a project does not alone change the legal purpose of the funds. A high balance in a benefit trust can coexist with a cash-insolvent operating corporation. Where a grant allows overheads, evidence the permissible calculation, supporting invoices and acquittal; do not transfer an arbitrary management fee. For an RNTBC also ask whether the funds are held **in the trustee or agent capacity** and what directions or restrictions apply. [LAW-CATSI ss 265-1, 271-1, 531-1; LAW-PBC rr 6–7; REG-ORIC-REPORT; ANALYSIS]

### C.3 Consent-and-agreement evidence index (original)

For each legal holder decision create a numbered index but do **not** put culturally restricted source material into the general board-pack repository:

1. **Rights map:** determination and affected holder population, including nonmembers and rights by area.
2. **Process proof:** verified applicable traditional-law process **or** holder-adopted process, identified decision class, any lawful standing instructions/alternative procedure.
3. **Information proof:** versions of land maps, proposed impacts, benefits, liabilities, compensation, surrender clauses and written/translated summaries actually provided.
4. **Participation proof:** notification, ability to ask questions and obtain independent advice, who was present/represented and who remained uncontacted or disputed.
5. **Decision proof:** decision outcome, conditions, abstentions, unresolved opposition, reasons the applicable legal threshold is met, signed r 9 certificate where required.
6. **Separate corporate proof:** rule-book quorum, conflicts, director/minute approval, authorised signatures and signed deed version.
7. **Separate registration proof:** ILUA category, required parties/attachments, NNTT application and final registration status.
8. **Separate asset proof:** payee/trustee, benefit deed, investment and distribution controls and reporting duties.

**Do not make a ‘consent percentage’ a universal statutory proxy.** The legal test depends on what process applies to that decision under PBC r 8(3)–(4), any properly conditioned exception, and the Native Title Act's separate rules for the agreement route. A documented attendance tally can be probative without becoming a legal rule in itself. [LAW-PBC rr 3, 8–10; LAW-NTA ss 24BA–24EB, 251A–251B]

### C.4 Board minute and escalations (original model)

**Minimum minute entry:** “The board considered agenda item [identifier] in its corporate capacity [identify capacity]. Counsel's opinion dated [date] classified the proposal as [class]; the affected native title holder process was [complete/not complete/not applicable, reasons], with related evidence held in restricted register [ID]. Interested directors [identities] disclosed [nature], and participation was determined under [exact law/rule]. Trusteeship, funding restrictions and solvency were assessed using [report]. On the verified version [document/number], the disinterested board resolved [decision], expressly subject to [enumerated conditions]. [Name] will verify compliance before any signature/payment, report exceptions by [date] and provide a holder-accountability update by [date].”

This is **not** a valid resolution wording by itself. Its purpose is to expose an absent native title, trust, corporate or statutory approval *before* one is incorrectly represented as complete. If a lawyer cannot explain whether the board is acting on a native title decision or merely conducting routine corporate affairs, the matter is not ready for unconditional approval. [ANALYSIS; see 07.11, 07.18]

### D.3 Material questions unresolved for an actual transaction

1. **Legal capacity and holder population:** sealed determination, precise boundaries, affected rights/classes, RNTBC trustee/agent role, current registered rule book, any process-specific standing directions and evidence of nonmember inclusion.
2. **Specific decision route:** correct high-level/low-level classification, s 31 or ILUA pathway, parties and registration, r 8/8A/8B/9 interpretation, impact of any r 8(7) exception and applicable heritage/environment conditions.
3. **Cultural legitimacy and restricted evidence:** identified locally authorised decision arrangements, acceptable process, reliable access to independent advice, sensitive information controls and how meaningful objections are addressed without outsider adjudication of culture.
4. **Financial authority and constraints:** beneficial owner of agreement revenue and compensation, trust deed, grant conditions, permitted administrative charges, taxation, CGOI consolidation and current solvency.
5. **Law changes and specific exemptions:** new commencement dates or amendments not reflected in the most recent consolidation, ORIC size/rules/exemptions, ACNC obligations and a decision-specific legal opinion.

These are **genuine, case-specific unknowns**, not reasons to apply invented Indigenous customs or assume a board has power that the law reserves elsewhere. When they matter, the correct outcome is **recorded deferral or conditional limited authority pending proof**, never a fabricated consent certificate. [LAW-PBC; LAW-CATSI; ANALYSIS]
