**Hierarchy.** (1) Binding legislation, legislative instruments and applicable case law; (2) binding exchange listing, licence, prudential, contractual and grant conditions within their scope; (3) official regulator interpretation and guidance, useful but not itself a substitute for law; (4) constitutions, rule books and valid corporate decisions within the governing statute; (5) voluntary governance principles; (6) AICD historical teaching; (7) this author's independent analytical and practical recommendations. A future consultation proposal is not operative law. In October 2026, the ASX Corporate Governance Principles **fourth edition** remains the published operative edition; the July 2026 proposed fifth edition remains a consultation draft [L7].

**Core answer.** A director must act with independent care and loyalty in the interests of the correct legal entity, exercise lawful authority, understand the organisation's business and financial position, challenge information that should reasonably concern them, and oversee an effective system that makes non-compliance visible and actionable. Neither incorporation, a committee, an expert report, insurance nor a well-intentioned corporate purpose removes the director's personal obligations. The precise statutory hook, exemptions and stakeholder decision rights depend on the entity.

## 01.01 Incorporation, separate legal personality, liability, ownership and control

### 01.01.1 What incorporation changes

**AICD historical curriculum:** Incorporation creates an entity separate from owners and directors. Incorporated entities include companies, cooperatives and incorporated associations. Unincorporated enterprises may comprise sole traders, general partnerships, joint ventures or associations without legal personality. Directors operate where owner/member interests and the power to manage are separated. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 9-12, 35-40]; AICD[GSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 7-12].

**Operative law:** Under the *Corporations Act* ss 119, 124-125 a company comes into existence on registration and has a separate legal identity and the powers of a body corporate. It owns assets, assumes debts, employs people and can sue or be sued. A shareholder owns a share and associated rights, **not** a proportionate legal title to each company asset. Directors have authority to govern through statute, constitution and valid delegation; their office is not ownership. Members' limited liability depends on legal form and unpaid amounts on shares or the guarantee, subject to statutory qualifications. The corporate veil does not extinguish liability for a personal guarantee, tort or offence, insolvent trading, trust liabilities, unpaid tax-related director penalties or personal breaches of duties [L1].

**Practical distinction:** A subsidiary's directors govern that *subsidiary*, not an economic segment of the group. A group cash pool or an instruction from the parent is not self-authorising. The parent might have sufficient resources while the subsidiary cannot pay its own debts. Conversely a company may have large accumulated losses yet remain solvent through cash and committed financing. At a charity, restrictions on using donations and grants constrain the organisation independently of whether funds sit in its bank account. At an RNTBC, the separate corporate form coexists with native-title rights and legally prescribed common law holder decision rights, which should not be treated as owned by the directors.

### 01.01.2 Corporate purpose and the control chain

Owners or members exercise the powers reserved to them under legislation and governing documents, commonly to elect directors, amend a constitution by the required resolution, and vote on specified transactions. The board directs or oversees management and appoints/monitors executives. Management executes operations under delegation. This is a distribution of **decision rights and accountability**, not a rigid one-way reporting chart. An owner cannot ordinarily direct individual directors to breach their duty to the company, although a valid constitution or shareholder resolution may lawfully affect corporate power. The often-used illustration 'owners appoint board; board appoints CEO; CEO manages' is a governance model, not a statement that all elections and appointments follow one process. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 37-39, 52-55]; *Corporations Act* ss 198A, 198C-198D, 201G, 203C-203D [L1].

**Director decision test:** Who holds legal title to the asset, who is party to the contract, who may approve the transaction, who bears the risk, and whose interests must the director consider? Request an entity chart, ASIC/ORIC/state register extracts, ownership and intercompany agreements, guarantee and trust records, current constitution, delegations and material funding conditions. **Red flags:** 'the group owns it' without identifying the contracting entity; cash sweeps without subsidiary consideration; expenditure beyond charitable purposes; instructions from an appointer treated as binding. **Minutes:** identify the legal entity, power exercised, commercial rationale for that entity and matters referred to members or rights holders.

## 01.02 Legal forms: companies, CLGs, cooperatives, associations, statutory bodies and CATSI corporations

### 01.02.1 Classification is the first legal task

**AICD historical curriculum:** Structure alters who belongs to the organisation, who appoints its governing body, how profits may be distributed, the regulatory system and liability. For-profit/NFP, listed/unlisted and incorporated/unincorporated are *different classification axes*. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 10-18]; AICD[GAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 32-33].

| Structure | Governing legal source and core design | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|
| ASX-listed Australian company | *Corporations Act*; constitution/replaceable rules; ASX Listing Rules and corporate-governance reporting | Listing is not a different type of legal personality; extra market duties attach. |
| Unlisted public company limited by shares | *Corporations Act* and constitution | Public-company governance and financial reporting obligations differ from proprietary companies. |
| Proprietary company (`Pty Ltd`) | *Corporations Act* ss 112-113; constitution or replaceable rules | Proprietary status does **not** mean exempt from duties, taxes or WHS law. |
| Public CLG | *Corporations Act* ss 112, 124 and constitution; members provide guarantee rather than share capital | Charity registration is a separate status, not an automatic property of a CLG. |
| ACNC-registered charity | Underlying entity law **plus** ACNC Act/Regulations, registration conditions and applicable purposes | Some provisions of the *Corporations Act* switch off for a company registered with the ACNC; some remain. |
| Incorporated association (e.g. WA) | Particular state/territory associations law and rules | State laws differ: statutory duties, voting and insolvency must be mapped locally. |
| Cooperative | Applicable state/territory cooperatives national law application and regulations; rules | Active member participation, mutuality and distribution rules require specific checks. |
| Statutory body/government-owned corporation | Enabling legislation, public-sector accountability law and sometimes the *Corporations Act* | The appointing minister's powers and public duties arise from the actual instrument, not generic company rules. |
| CATSI corporation | CATSI Act and registered rule book, regulated by ORIC | Specialised statutory duties, membership, intervention and insolvency regime. |
| RNTBC/PBC | Usually a CATSI corporation **plus** *Native Title Act 1993* and PBC Regulations | Affected common law holders may have decision rights regardless of corporate membership. |
| Trust / unincorporated association | Trust deed, trustee legal form or applicable general law | Trustee can incur personal liabilities and owes separate equitable duties; assess the actual trustee. |

### 01.02.2 Companies and charities are overlapping regimes

For an ACNC-registered body corporate, *Corporations Act* Part 1.6 (ss 111K-111Q) and ASIC's official guidance describe switched-off requirements. Specifically, for ASIC-registered companies that are also ACNC charities, **ss 180-183 and relevant s 185, certain parts of s 188, and ss 191-194 are switched off**, but **s 184 criminal conduct, other continuing statutory obligations and general law** are not thereby erased. This is not a blanket exemption from company law, nor an exemption from complying with a charity's ACNC Governance Standards. See 01.06, 01.08 and 01.19; [L1, L3-L4, L8].

A CLG charity's constitution commonly restricts distributions, asset application, board remuneration and dissolution. The corporation's members are not shareholders with a claim to accumulated charity assets. ACNC-registered charities must satisfy standards for purposes/NFP status, accountability to members where applicable, serious law compliance, suitability of Responsible People, duties-related reasonable steps and relevant redress requirements. The charity's *purpose* and other restrictions are legally consequential, not merely aspirational marketing language. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 41-45]; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 50-51] [L3-L4, L8].

### 01.02.3 CATSI and community corporations

CATSI registration gives corporate status under a separate Commonwealth statute. The corporation's rule book, members, directors, Registrar's powers and cultural/community context shape its decisions. **Do not** apply *Corporations Act* ss 180-183 directly: s 190B excludes CATSI corporations from that Division; CATSI ss 265-1, 265-5, 265-10, 265-15 supply corresponding duties. Insolvent trading applies through CATSI s 531-1, which expressly incorporates relevant *Corporations Act* provisions with substitutions. An RNTBC also operates under native-title obligations and protections, notably CATSI ss 265-20 and 268-5, and the PBC Regulations [L1-L2, L6, L9].

**Director decision test:** Before accepting any 'standard director duty' statement, demand a one-page **entity/registration/office/transaction** matrix. Check ACNC charity status, ORIC registration, state association registration, listed status, APRA licence, trustee and native-title capacities. **Red flags:** describing an association committee as a public-company board for statutory voting purposes; assuming ACNC registration removes all corporate liability; no identification of RNTBC affected common law holders. **Minutes:** identify the entity, statute, regulatory overlay and required member/rights-holder process.

## 01.03 Sources of authority: law, constitutions, rules, resolutions, charters and delegation

### 01.03.1 The authority ladder and internal legal environment

**AICD historical curriculum:** The director must know the constitution, material contracts, delegation schedule and policies. Documents confer power and constrain it; a clear governance framework should state matters for board decision and what management may decide. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 11-17]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 62-78]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 36-38].

**Operative law:** The company constitution and replaceable rules are recognised by *Corporations Act* ss 134-141, with s 140 giving the constitution and replaceable rules their statutory-contract operation between company, members and officers in the specified capacities. Replaceable rules do **not** apply to a sole-director/sole-shareholder proprietary company in the ordinary way (s 135; see s 198E). Section 136 controls constitution adoption and alteration; a mere board charter cannot amend the constitution or override statute. The company's management-power rule is s 198A, **a replaceable rule** unless displaced by the constitution, and s 198D addresses delegation. The *Corporations Act* and constitution may also reserve matters to members, impose special voting thresholds or regulate conflicts. CATSI corporations instead rely on statutory rule-book architecture; WA incorporated associations use association rules as constrained by their Act [L1-L2, L5].

| Instrument | What it can legitimately do | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| Statute/regulation | Creates powers, duties, offences and exceptions | Be overridden by a board policy. |
| Constitution / CATSI rule book / association rules | Sets lawful purpose, membership, meetings, appointment/removal, restrictions | Authorise illegal conduct or remove a mandatory statutory duty. |
| Members' resolution | Exercises powers lawfully reserved to members | Automatically make a loyalty conflict proper or erase creditor/native-title rights. |
| Board charter and committee terms | Allocates work and information, clarifies reporting | Create authority beyond the governing law; transfer ultimate oversight wholesale. |
| Delegations/financial authorities | Permit specified employees/committees to bind the entity within limits | Cure an invalid transaction merely because an executive signed it. |
| Contracts/grants/loans | Bind entity to counterparties and conditions | Override external legislation or necessarily bind non-parties. |
| Policies / codes | Make controls operational and measurable | Substitute for applicable law or external reporting requirements. |

### 01.03.2 Authority is specific to a decision

When a transaction requires board approval, distinguish an informed board *approval* from a director *signature*, a committee *recommendation*, a CEO *delegation*, a member *special resolution* and a third-party *consent*. Material related-party, share issue, change of purpose, property disposal, constitutional change, native-title decision, sale to a director and distribution may each require a different approval route. The approval matrix must check quorum, conflicts, notice, power to delegate, constitutional restrictions and regulatory preconditions. A board can make a sound commercial decision that is legally ineffective or prohibited if the required authority is absent.

**Director decision test:** What exact source gives us power, what is reserved, who is excluded from voting, and what consent is missing? Obtain certified constitution/rule book, current delegations, meeting notice and quorum records, shareholder/member agreement, grant deeds, lender covenants, property title and a legal sign-off on exceptional approvals. **Red flags:** retrospective ratification used routinely; signing authority confused with decision authority; old policy contradicting amended law; unrecorded delegations. **Minutes:** specify resolution wording, abstentions, quorums, conditions precedent, delegated signatory and documentary evidence of any other required approval.


### 01.03.3 Governance frameworks are analytical tools, not sources of legal power

The AICD **Company Directors' Corporate Governance Framework** groups practice in four quadrants: **individual director** (personal competence, responsibilities and leadership); **board** (collective functioning and collegial obligations); **organisation** (strategy, performance, risk, finance and management relationships); and **stakeholders** (relationships and external legitimacy). This is a coverage lens, not a statutory checklist or warrant to override the constitution. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 39-40]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 129-130].

**Robert Tricker's model** distinguishes board **conformance** (accountability and supervision) from **performance** (strategy and policy), with present/past operational oversight and future-facing choices. The practical failure is allocating the whole meeting to compliance reports while ignoring whether strategy creates sustainable value, or conversely pursuing growth without solvency and legality checks. A competent board must do both. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 40-41]. **Director test:** Compare agenda hours, decision quality and assurance outcomes across these lenses. **Evidence:** board calendar, papers, strategic decisions and overdue remediation. **Red flag:** oversight is strong only on paper or strategy proceeds without control. **Minutes:** link decisions to both purpose/performance and mandatory safeguards. The deeper behavioural analysis belongs in planned File 02.

## 01.04 Who is a director or officer? Formal, alternate, de facto, shadow and executive positions

### 01.04.1 Function can matter more than title

**AICD historical curriculum:** A director may be validly appointed; act as one without appointment (de facto); or exercise influence such that appointed directors are accustomed to act on their instructions (shadow). Alternates may be directors while acting in the alternate role. The distinction between executive, non-executive, independent, nominee and chair relates to board roles, not an automatic hierarchy of statutory standards. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 18-24]; AICD[LSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 5-7].

**Operative law:** *Corporations Act* s 9 defines 'director', including a person appointed to position of director or alternate and acting in that capacity; also persons acting in position and persons on whose instructions/wishes directors are accustomed to act, with the usual professional-advice exception. The 'officer' definition includes directors and secretaries, people who make or participate in substantial decisions, can significantly affect financial standing, or whose instructions the directors are accustomed to follow, as well as specified insolvency officeholders. *ASIC v King* [2020] HCA 4 confirms the officer category need not depend on a named office or formal appointment. The outcome is function-and-fact dependent; an external adviser is not an officer solely because their advice is influential [L1, J4].

A non-executive director ordinarily oversees rather than runs operations but must still understand issues and exercise care. An executive director combines board and employment responsibilities; an independent director is assessed for independence of judgment and relevant relationships, not awarded immunity. A nominee director must not simply follow the appointing investor's instruction to another company's detriment. A chair facilitates board performance, information flow and constructive challenge, without exclusive ownership of legal responsibility. The CEO may be a director, other officer, both or neither depending on role and acts. A company secretary is an officer and has its own administrative and legal responsibilities; *Shafron* confirms the real scope of assigned tasks can give rise to personal care duties [L1, J3].

### 01.04.2 Formal and informal influence

A founder who repeatedly directs board decisions, a controlling shareholder that dictates actions, or a 'consultant' who in practice assumes strategic decisions may trigger de facto/shadow director or officer questions. But being a lender, expert, minister, shareholder or Indigenous Elder does not automatically create a shadow-director status. In RNTBC governance, common law holder decision rights are an independent statutory design, not evidence by themselves of shadow direction. Document which role a person is acting in before attributing director duties.

**Director decision test:** Identify individuals who actually control banking, contracts, board agendas, strategy, staffing and material asset decisions. Compare title, appointment instrument, delegations, actual conduct and minutes. **Red flags:** 'advisers' routinely instructing directors; unofficial directors signing board approvals; CEO using an investor's instruction to avoid board scrutiny; company secretary excluded from legally consequential papers. **Minutes:** list actual attendees and capacities, alternate appointments and voting rights; distinguish invited advisers and observers from directors, and record independent board deliberation.

## 01.05 Eligibility, selection, appointment, diligence, induction, exit and disqualification

### 01.05.1 Recruitment and eligibility

**AICD historical curriculum:** Board recruitment should begin with purpose, needed competencies and diversity, not names. The prospective director must examine the organisation, its financial condition, legal exposures, culture, role and capacity to devote time. Appointment is a personal acceptance of risk; resignation is not a cure for prior misconduct. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 21-34]; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 18-23]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 41-46, 81-88].

**Operative law:** Company directors generally must be at least 18 and satisfy applicable eligibility and residence rules (*Corporations Act* ss 201B, 201A); appointing company directors involves consent, registers and ASIC notification. *Corporations Act* Part 2D.6 covers disqualification, including automatic disqualifications under s 206B, court orders and certain ASIC powers; director identification requirements appear in Part 9.1A of the Act and the relevant administration regime. For public companies, directors' removal by members has mandatory processes under s 203D; proprietary companies have provisions in s 203C, subject to constitution and structure. Resignation under ss 203AA-203AB has legal effective-date and lodgement consequences. These company rules cannot be assumed to govern a WA association or CATSI corporation: check its committee eligibility and exit provisions (WA ss 38-41) and CATSI appointment/removal/disqualification rules. Registered charities must take reasonable steps concerning Responsible People's suitability under ACNC Governance Standard 4 [L1-L5].

### 01.05.2 A defensible pre-joining review

Diligence should cover: entity and group chart; purpose and constitution; financial statements and detailed management cash flows; debt, arrears and contingencies; auditor letters; present and threatened proceedings; whistleblower, misconduct and workplace matters; tax/superannuation standing; major licence and permit obligations; regulator correspondence; insurance and exclusions; indemnity deed and indemnification solvency; board dynamics and minutes; cyber and safety exposure; conflicts; related-party arrangements; and, where relevant, members' rights or native-title decisions. For a distressed company, request current liquidity and tax liabilities, not only the previous annual accounts. For a community corporation, ask whose mandate is legitimate and whether affected common law holders have been engaged.

An appointment letter should state office, remuneration, estimated time, committee assignments, information access, induction, conflict disclosure, D&O cover, deed of access/indemnity and cessation arrangements. Induction requires site exposure, important personnel, regulator environment, business model, cash flows, governance culture and a discussion of how bad news escalates. A 'skills matrix' cannot guarantee competence; a director also needs judgment, preparation and independence of mind.

### 01.05.3 Exiting responsibly

Check legal rules before removal, resignation and public announcement. Ensure accurate lodgement and termination of bank access/delegations, preservation of records, insurance tail and continuing confidentiality. A resignation may be relevant where lawful board action is impossible, but first consider seeking advice, requiring immediate corrective action and recording the basis for dissent. A current director cannot transfer their duty by handing the role to someone else without valid appointment. A former director may still be liable for actions during tenure and misuse of confidential information.

**Director decision test:** *Can I reasonably supervise this business, and would I join knowing what I know now?* Obtain a signed suitability declaration, independent company/regulator searches, board packs, financial forecasts, material compliance reports and conflict register. **Red flags:** refusal to share accounts, unresolved qualified audit, repeated resignations, unavailable insurance, opaque related parties, hostility to questions. **Decision:** decline or condition joining on resolved information gaps and minimum protective arrangements. **Minutes:** record appointment authority, consent, disclosures, induction, checks and resignation/removal in accordance with the governing instrument.

## 01.06 To whom duties are owed: company, purposes, creditors, stakeholders, charity and community rights

### 01.06.1 The company is normally the beneficiary of company-law duties

**AICD historical curriculum:** Directors must act in the organisation's best interests and reconcile stakeholder interests. Shareholders, employees, creditors, communities and beneficiaries can be central to decisions without automatically holding an individually enforceable claim for breach of general company-director duties. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 23-29]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 80-88].

**Operative law:** For an ordinary company, *Corporations Act* s 181 requires directors/officers to act in good faith in the **best interests of the corporation** and for a proper purpose. The common law and equity also impose duties owed to the company. This is not ordinarily a distinct direct duty owed to each shareholder, employee or creditor. Duties can be engaged through equitable claims, oppression, statutory protections, contracts, torts, employment and other law; do not infer such a cause of action from s 181 alone. *Spies v The Queen* [2000] HCA 43 cautions against describing a freestanding direct duty enforceable by creditors. As insolvency approaches, however, creditor interests gain real weight in determining the **company's** interests; directors must also meet the specific insolvent-trading regime. *Walker v Wimborne* [1976] HCA 7 illustrates the problem of favouring group entities at an insolvent member's expense [L1, J5-J6].

Long-term company interests can legitimately encompass employees, customers, communities, natural resources, safety, licence to operate, environmental transition, tax compliance and reputation. This is not the same as a legal rule that a board must maximise every stakeholder's satisfaction. Directors must explain how trade-offs serve the company's lawful purpose and sustainable interests, respecting specific statutory rights. Material harm from ignoring a stakeholder may be economic, legal, operational or ethical even absent an independent fiduciary claim.

### 01.06.2 Charitable purpose is legally constrained

ACNC-registered charities must be governed consistently with their charitable purposes, NFP character, governing document and the ACNC Act/Regulations. **Governance Standard 5 is an obligation imposed on the registered charity to take reasonable steps to ensure its Responsible People are subject to and comply with specified duties**. It should not be misdescribed as re-enacting all *Corporations Act* civil directors' duties personally. Under regulation 45.25 the specified conduct covers care/diligence, honest and fair best-interests decisions for charitable purpose, position/information, conflicts, responsible finances and avoiding operation while insolvent. It does not switch off other enforceable duties at common law, under criminal company law, trustee obligations or applicable state legislation. ACNC guidance is useful for the meaning of 'reasonable steps', but the binding duty to maintain systems is grounded in the regulations [L3-L4, L8].

A grant to assist one beneficiary category may not be redirected to an unrelated activity because the board perceives a better general outcome. Purpose, donor intent where legally enforceable, restricted funds and charitable asset rules constrain discretion. In a non-charitable membership association, members' purposes and rules similarly matter but under different statutes.

### 01.06.3 First Nations entities and special native-title decision rights

A CATSI board's corporate duty to the corporation coexists with cultural legitimacy and membership representation. For an RNTBC, a native-title decision or compensation application often requires consultation with and consent from **affected common law holders**, not simply a majority vote of RNTBC members. ORIC's current guidance explains that affected holders may be non-members and that directors are accountable to them in these decisions. CATSI s 265-20 provides a tailored protection for specified acts or omissions done in good faith with the belief necessary to satisfy native-title legislation obligations; s 268-5 specifically addresses certain common-law-holder interests. Neither provision is unlimited immunity nor a substitute for proper consultation and certified decision records [L2, L6, L9].

**Director decision test:** Who benefits from and is legally entitled to decide this matter? Request purpose clauses, stakeholder rights mapping, legal advice on creditor exposure, restrictions on grants, native-title decision classification, holder lists/authorised representation and consent evidence. **Red flags:** board asserts 'all stakeholders equal' to avoid a legal priority; a CLG moves restricted grants into unrelated trading; an RNTBC relies solely on a members' vote for a native-title agreement. **Minutes:** state whose interests and rights were considered, competing alternatives, the entity-level rationale, legal consents and what remains outstanding.

## 01.07 Care and diligence: standards, inquiry, information and financial literacy

### 01.07.1 The standard is objective but contextual

**AICD historical curriculum:** The director must prepare, attend, read, inquire, understand the business, recognise foreseeable financial and non-financial risks, challenge management and access independent advice when required. The board is not a passive recipient of board packs. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 38-45]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 21-26, 66-73]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 60-66].

**Operative company law:** Under *Corporations Act* s 180(1), a director or other officer must exercise powers and discharge duties with the care and diligence that a reasonable person would exercise in the corporation's circumstances and in the same office and responsibilities. This combines an objective minimum standard with the reality of the role, size, business, financial condition, division of responsibility and specific expertise undertaken. A non-executive is not an executive and is not obliged to inspect every invoice, but cannot remain ignorant of the key operations or obvious risk. A director's specialised knowledge, committee work and actual responsibilities can make more inquiry reasonably necessary. Poor outcomes alone are not proof of negligence; the question is the process, information, foreseeable risk and judgment when the decision was made. The comparable CATSI duty is **s 265-1(1)**; WA association officer duty is **s 44(1)**. For an ACNC-registered company, note the switched-off civil company-law provisions and separately applicable duties in 01.02 and 01.06 [L1-L5, J1].

### 01.07.2 What competence requires

Directors should be able to understand how the entity creates value or delivers purpose, how it generates and uses cash, the link between balance sheet and funding, whether controls function, and what could make the strategy fail. Financial literacy means understanding the balance sheet, earnings, cash flows, debt maturities, contingent liabilities, solvency assumptions and financial reporting judgments. It does not require being the CFO, auditor or industry engineer. *ASIC v Healey* [2011] FCA 717 (Centro) makes clear that the process of external audit and management review does not displace each director's responsibility to read and understand financial information and notice matters apparent to them [J1].

The board should ask for a **decision-useful rather than information-dense** pack: action required, context, legal authority, options, downside, cash consequences, risks and mitigations, key assumptions, audit or external assurance, conflict declarations, and a proposed resolution. Where reports are late or misleading, defer the decision or demand additional work. A director cannot safely claim adequate information if the circumstances objectively required further inquiry. However, perfect knowledge is neither attainable nor the legal standard. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 62-74]; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 38-44]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 118-123].

### 01.07.3 Testing assertions, risk and assurance

An effective director triangulates: management's position; comparative data and external developments; financial and operational evidence; and expert/independent assurance. A headline 'green' risk dashboard is weak evidence where worker reports, cash metrics, incidents or a regulator letter contradict the conclusion. Question whether management has incentives to downplay failures. Challenge any sensitivity analysis whose assumptions rule out the consequential downside. An observation of poor culture is not a conclusion of breach; it is a reason to investigate whether risk and compliance systems work.

**Director decision test:** (a) What is being recommended? (b) What facts must be true? (c) What evidence contradicts them? (d) What is irreversible? (e) Who reviewed legality? (f) What would change our view? Request auditable budgets, cash-flow and covenant forecasts, decision papers, legal advice and risk-control exceptions. **Red flags:** unexplained forecast changes, repeated overdue management actions, 'the auditors signed it' as sole assurance, headline ratios without notes, critical matters scheduled under 'for noting'. **Decision:** approve with bounded conditions, request another option/assurance or defer. **Minutes:** record key assumptions tested, competing advice, information requested, limitations and reasons for the conclusion, without producing a performative verbatim transcript.

## 01.08 Good faith, proper purpose, fiduciary obligations, conflicts and related parties

### 01.08.1 Loyalty is separate from competence

**AICD historical curriculum:** Duties of loyalty require directors to put the company's interests ahead of their own, deal honestly, use powers for their proper purpose, avoid unauthorised benefits and disclose/manage conflicts. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 25-38]; AICD[LSH, 2025/26 V1, pp. 8-13].

**Operative law:** *Corporations Act* s 181 imposes the civil requirement of good faith in the corporation's best interests and proper purpose. Sections 182-183 concern misuse of position/information. The general law includes fiduciary obligations not to place self-interest or duties to others in conflict without appropriate authorisation and not to exploit corporate opportunities secretly. A director may breach a loyalty duty even without proven financial loss, and may act improperly in a transaction that appears profitable. Proper purpose asks **why the specific power was conferred**. A capital raising to fund genuine business need is different from issuing shares for the dominant purpose of manipulating control. Duties are not suspended by majority approval of a transaction unless a valid legal basis for approval/ratification exists. For a wholly owned subsidiary, s 187 is a *limited* conditional rule, not permission for every subsidiary board to prefer the parent; among its conditions the constitution must expressly authorise holding-company interests and the subsidiary must not be insolvent as relevant. CATSI ss 265-5, 265-10 and 265-15, and WA ss 45-47, supply their respective statutory duties [L1-L2, L5].

### 01.08.2 Disclose, classify and manage conflicts

For ordinary Corporations Act companies, s 191 generally requires notice to other directors of a **material personal interest** in company affairs subject to specified exceptions; s 192 allows standing notice. Section 195 generally prevents a materially interested director of a **public company** from being present or voting on the matter, subject to exceptions and prescribed mechanisms. A proprietary company's replaceable rule s 194 permits a director who has disclosed the interest to vote and be present, **unless the constitution modifies it** and subject to fiduciary standards. Disclosure does not alone validate an otherwise improper transaction. The separate related-party financial-benefits regime for public companies is Part 2E.1, especially ss 208-210; member approval is generally required unless a statutory exception (such as arm's-length terms) is properly made out. It is not correct to say all related-party payments require a shareholder vote, or that 'arm's length' can be assumed from a market-style label. ASIC Regulatory Guide 76 discusses the need for judgment in relying on exceptions [L1, L12].

For an ACNC-registered company, ss 191-194 are generally switched off but Governance Standard 5, general law, constitution, criminal conduct provisions and any remaining applicable statutory rules must still be addressed. A **WA incorporated association** is instead governed by ss 42-43: disclosure plus a specific ban on presence and voting on certain interests with particular exceptions and a general-meeting solution if quorum is lost. Under the **CATSI Act** disclosure is s 268-1, special common-law-holder interests s 268-5, and attendance/voting restrictions s 268-20. Applying company s 195 directly would be legally inaccurate [L2, L4-L5, L8].

| Conflict type | Example | Primary control |
|---|---|---|
| Personal financial | Director owns bidding supplier | Full prompt declaration; independent procurement; exclusion/consent per relevant statute. |
| Conflict of duty | Director also represents another competing or contracting entity | Separate information channels, disinterested review and clear recusals. |
| Related-party benefit | Loan, lease or consulting fee to connected person | Check Part 2E.1/CATSI related-party/charity or association rules and real economic terms. |
| Information conflict | Nominee has confidential information of a different company | No unauthorised sharing; legal advice on overlapping duties. |
| Perceived or relational | Close family, employer, funding association or community relationship | Declare and assess materiality/context; a perceived relationship is not always a statutory material personal interest. |
| Community native-title interest | Director is common law holder affected by native-title decision | Apply CATSI s 268-5 and PBC regulations, not a simplistic exclusion of every rights holder. |

### 01.08.3 Managing the transaction in substance

A defensible process tests: identify all direct and indirect interests; state whether statutory notification is triggered; determine quorum and whether participation/voting is prohibited; obtain independent valuation or alternatives; check related-party/member approval; ask whether the transaction is best for the correct entity; and document monitoring of connected payments. An independent director should not assume an interested colleague can cure conflict by simply promising to be fair. In a small volunteer board, losing quorum may require members to act or constitutional restructuring. In an RNTBC, excluding all native-title holders simply because they have rights could undermine the native-title-specific legal scheme.

**Director decision test:** Who benefits, who could be disadvantaged, who may decide and on what evidence? Obtain declarations, register of interests, beneficial ownership, procurement history, comparison bids, valuation, related-party advice and draft contracts. **Red flags:** interested director drafts recommendation or controls adviser; no genuine alternative; split invoices beneath approval thresholds; meeting has defective quorum. **Decision:** exclude from discussion/vote where legally necessary; have disinterested board or members consider independent evidence; decline transaction if improper or unauthorised. **Minutes:** describe nature of interest without needless sensitive personal detail, exact statutory/constitutional treatment, entry/exit from meeting, quorum, abstention, approval basis and safeguards.

## 01.09 Misuse of position or information, corporate opportunities, insider trading and misconduct

### 01.09.1 Corporate opportunities and confidential knowledge

**AICD historical curriculum:** Directors must not use their position or information to gain an improper advantage or harm their organisation; duties concerning information continue beyond service. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 29-33]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 60-65].

**Operative law:** For ordinary non-exempt Corporations Act companies, s 182 prohibits improper use of position for advantage or corporate detriment, and s 183 prohibits improper use of information obtained through the role, including by former officers/employees. The statutory formulation does not require that the intended advantage or detriment actually materialise before the conduct can contravene the duty. Criminal provisions in s 184 involve additional mental elements and must be separately established. Equitable duties concerning confidential information, opportunities and conflicts can be independently actionable. CATSI ss 265-10 and 265-15, WA ss 46-47, apply within their regimes. ACNC charity status does not authorise misuse and s 184 remains material [L1-L5].

A director who learns that the organisation is negotiating for land, technology, contracts or an acquisition should not quietly purchase the opportunity or steer it to their own company. An advisory role on another board makes information barriers and active conflict-management essential. A 'commercial opportunity' does not automatically belong to an organisation forever; the relevant analysis concerns source of opportunity, corporate interest/expectancy, conflict, authorisation and duties under governing law.

### 01.09.2 Insider trading and market manipulation

*Corporations Act* Part 7.10 Division 3, principally ss 1042A and 1043A, prohibits trading, procuring trading and certain communications involving inside information about relevant financial products when statutory elements are met. Inside information is information not generally available which, if generally available, a reasonable person would expect materially to affect the price or value of relevant financial products. The prohibition is **not limited to directors or to their own listed company's shares**. A director may learn inside information about a supplier, takeover target, competitor or joint venture. Closed trading windows, pre-clearance and insider lists are governance controls, not substitutes for applying the law: a trade within a permitted window may still be unlawful. Market manipulation, false statements and misleading or deceptive conduct are governed by other provisions of Part 7.10 and competition/consumer or financial-services law [L1].

**Director decision test:** Was information received by virtue of office, confidential or market-sensitive? Has its disclosure been authorised? Has anyone traded, recommended trading or passed it to a likely trader? Request information classification, dealing policies, pre-clearance records, market announcements and access logs. **Red flags:** gifts or commissions for introductions, unusual personal trades before announcements, unprotected board portals, 'quietly tell our largest shareholder'. **Decision:** preserve evidence, restrict access, halt questionable trading, seek independent counsel and consider disclosure/regulator action. **Minutes:** record escalation and action taken without distributing price-sensitive information unnecessarily; preserve a separate privileged investigation record if appropriate.

## 01.10 Business judgment rule: elements, boundaries and non-applicability

### 01.10.1 Exact statutory test

**AICD historical curriculum:** A business decision may fail commercially without director negligence if sound process and statutory conditions are met. Directors must be properly informed, act honestly and independently, and reasonably assess the company's interest. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 45-47]; AICD[LSH, 2025/26 V1, p. 17].

**Operative law:** *Corporations Act* **s 180(2)-(3)** provides that a director or officer who makes a **business judgment** meets s 180(1) and equivalent common-law/equity duties **in respect of that judgment** if all four conditions hold:

1. The judgment is made **in good faith for a proper purpose**.
2. The decision maker **has no material personal interest** in the judgment's subject matter.
3. The person **informs themselves to the extent they reasonably believe appropriate** about the subject matter.
4. The person **rationally believes the judgment is in the corporation's best interests**. That belief is rational unless no reasonable person in their position would hold it.

A 'business judgment' is a decision to take or not take action in respect of a matter relevant to the corporation's business operations. The provision protects only s 180(1) and equivalent duties, **not** duties under other provisions/laws, and does not immunise a failure to make a judgment through ignorance. It is not a universal excuse for compliance failure, insolvency, improper purpose, criminal conduct or an unlawful dividend. The burden of establishing relevant facts and procedural operation is issue-specific in litigation. For CATSI corporations the corresponding test is **s 265-1(2)-(3)**, while a WA association has **s 44(2)-(3)**. Charity Responsible People should not simply invoke company s 180(2) where the underlying s 180 duties are switched off; evaluate applicable general law, ACNC duties and governing entity law [L1-L2, L4-L5].

### 01.10.2 Commercial discretion is not the same as statutory compliance

The legal protection is especially relevant where a board chooses between plausible business alternatives: build versus buy, acquisition value, market entry, portfolio divestment, project cancellation, capital allocation or risk acceptance. An informed board can be wrong about an oil price, interest rate, consumer demand or construction schedule while making a rational judgment. By contrast, ignoring an obvious licence condition, approving accounts without adequate reading, misleading investors or concealing a personal benefit is not cured by the commercial attraction of the decision.

A defensible paper should identify the decision, legal and delegated authority, alternative courses (including doing nothing), time-critical evidence, downside cases, reversibility, business model effects, reasonableness of assumptions, financial capacity and affected stakeholders. Not every decision requires a large consultant study, but high impact and irreversible choices justify deeper inquiry.

**Director decision test:** Is this genuinely a judgment on the entity's operations, or a legal compliance duty masquerading as a business choice? Can each statutory element be evidenced? **Red flags:** known conflict; limited alternatives; advice received only after decision; 'we can take the legal risk' about a mandatory rule; no understanding of insolvency. **Decision:** separate legality from commercial merits; resolve the former first. **Minutes:** record the alternatives, information considered, independence/interest position, reasoning for the best-interests conclusion and any conditional safeguards. Do not insert formulaic words about s 180(2) in place of real deliberation.

## 01.11 Individual and collective accountability, delegation, reliance and committees

### 01.11.1 A board acts together; duties are borne by individuals

**AICD historical curriculum:** Directors collectively govern, while each person must exercise personal judgment. Board committees perform deeper work and report to the full board; they must not become unaccountable 'boards within the board'. Delegations should be current and monitored. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 52-79]; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 16-17, 38-45, 51-54]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 17-23, 36-38, 113-117].

**Operative company law:** A valid board resolution can be a company act, but individual directors have separate statutory/general law responsibilities. *Corporations Act* s 198D allows delegation unless the constitution provides otherwise, to a committee, director, employee or other person, and requires a delegation to be recorded in the minute book. Section 190 states the default responsibility for delegate acts and specifies the **limited circumstances** in which a director is not responsible under that section, requiring reasonable grounds to believe the delegate would comply and was reliable/competent, with inquiry where circumstances demand. Section 189 addresses reliance on employee, expert, director/committee and other information; protection requires good faith and an **independent assessment** having regard to the director's knowledge and the corporation's complexity. These provisions are not general immunity and do not create permission to ignore contradictory facts. AICD's 'board retains accountability' is sound guidance, but exact delegation provisions must be matched to each entity: CATSI ss 265-45 and 265-50, WA s 49 regarding reliance and the rules applying to particular powers [L1-L2, L5].

### 01.11.2 What is properly delegated

Management may be empowered to enter contracts, hire employees, implement budgets, manage risk controls and communicate routine information within set limits. Boards typically reserve constitutional questions, CEO appointment and termination, strategy, material capital, borrowing and guarantees, major acquisitions, risk appetite, financial accounts and material conflict or regulatory responses. The list is **governance practice**, not a statement that every item is non-delegable in all entities. The law or constitution determines which power can be delegated. The board must specify delegates' authority, transaction thresholds, conditions and reporting, testing whether limits work in practice.

A committee can recommend approval or have expressly delegated authority. Its report should show scope, attendance, independent adviser input, material dissent, conclusions, assurance gaps and matters needing full-board determination. Members of other committees should not treat an audit committee's work as a replacement for their own consideration of financial statements. Committees should contain relevant capabilities without excluding constructive scrutiny by the rest of the board.

### 01.11.3 Reliance on experts: reasonable, not automatic

Directors can and should use legal, accounting, safety, cyber and technical experts. The board must select competent advisers, provide a full brief, assess independence and assumptions, test consistency with known facts and decide what remains unresolved. Advice that says 'commercially attractive' does not certify legal authority; an unqualified audit does not remove responsibility for visible inconsistencies. **Centro** is especially important: apparently reliable accountants and external auditors do not excuse directors from reading reports, noticing significant classification errors and asking questions. Competent reliance and independent judgment are complements, not alternatives [J1].

**Director decision test:** Is delegation lawful, appropriate, limited, monitored and reversible? Who owns each material risk, and what independently verifies performance? Obtain board/committee charters, delegation register, adviser appointment/assumption letters, committee minutes, exceptions and remediation log. **Red flags:** repeated 'delegated to management' as answer to a board risk; expired delegations; no committee-to-board reporting; experts instructed to assume away disputed facts. **Decision:** adjust delegated powers, improve information and assurance, reserve material matters. **Minutes:** record relevant delegation and reliance, why information is credible, queries/limitations, and the board's independent conclusion.

## 01.12 Insolvent trading, cash-flow distress, safe harbour, defences and entity differences

### 01.12.1 Cash-flow insolvency is not an accounting loss

**AICD historical curriculum:** Directors should look for signs of financial distress early, test liquidity and obtain expert advice before additional debts are incurred. Safe harbour may permit an active turnaround; directors must not use it as a reason for delay or inaction. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 47-50]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 42-46, 124]; AICD[FLP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 90-100].

**Operative company law:** *Corporations Act* s 95A defines solvency by the ability to **pay all debts as and when they become due and payable**. Balance-sheet net assets, current ratio, net profit, equity and a going-concern accounting basis are evidence but not the legal test by themselves. An apparently profitable entity may fail because cash is unavailable at due dates; a loss-making entity may remain solvent through adequate cash, enforceable financing and achievable receipts. Consider the whole commercial context, including realisations, supported refinancing and non-deferable liabilities. Historic late payments, unpaid payroll, overdue tax/super, defaults and uncommitted facilities may indicate trouble, but no one metric establishes a legal conclusion.

Under **s 588G** a director may incur liability connected with company debt incurred while insolvent (or rendered insolvent) where there were reasonable grounds to suspect insolvency and the other statutory knowledge/awareness elements are met. The timing when a debt is incurred matters. Directors must not permit preventable debt accumulation on unrealistic assumptions. Breaches can lead to compensation and, where the criminal mental elements are met, criminal consequences. **Section 588H** sets out defences including reasonable expectation of solvency (stronger than hope), reasonable reliance on a competent reliable information provider, non-participation due to illness or other good reason, and reasonable steps to prevent incurring the debt. Defences are fact-specific and evidence dependent [L1, L10].

### 01.12.2 Safe harbour is a structured route, not a moratorium

*Corporations Act* **s 588GA** can disapply s 588G(2) for debts within its conditions where, after starting to suspect possible insolvency, a director starts developing one or more courses of action **reasonably likely to lead to a better outcome for the company** than immediate administration or liquidation. The course of action and covered debts must have the required connection. Consider whether information is properly maintained, employees are paid and tax reporting obligations are met, competent advisers are engaged, and a genuine plan exists. The section restricts availability in relation to employee entitlements and taxation reporting failures, with prescribed exceptions or court relief. It ends on specified events, including failure to implement within reasonable time, ceasing the course, loss of reasonable prospect or external administration. It is **not** a blanket period of insolvent trading immunity, a defence to all director duties or a licence to prefer selected creditors. ASIC's Regulatory Guide 217 (December 2024) is useful explanatory guidance, but the statute is the controlling text [L1, L10].

The relevant benchmark is *better than administration or liquidation at that time*, not merely a better profit forecast, improved share price or shareholder recovery. A workable restructuring course usually needs a dated cash forecast, accurate liability ageing, credible funding sources, named actions, milestones, responsible owners and stop/go triggers. Restructuring and rescue can generate value but can also deepen creditor losses. The board should separately assess whether prompt external administration, formal small-business restructuring or liquidation is needed.

### 01.12.3 Entity-specific insolvency rules

A CATSI corporation is subject to applicable Corporations Act insolvent-trading and creditor-defeating-disposition provisions **through CATSI s 531-1** with substitutions; the source of application matters. For a **WA incorporated association**, s **127** creates an offence concerning debt incurred while insolvent or made insolvent, subject to its own wording and specified defences. It is **not** identical text to s 588G, and company safe harbour cannot be automatically imported into association s 127. For an **ACNC charity**, Governance Standard 5 imposes a charity-level reasonable-steps obligation concerning insolvent operation, while other personal and entity obligations may also operate according to structure. State or territory incorporated-association laws, trustees and charity regulation require separate advice [L1-L5, L8].

| Evidence in distress | What it tests | Weak assumption to challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly rolling 13-week receipts/payments | Immediate due-date funding | 'The annual budget is positive'. |
| Twelve-plus-month integrated cash/earnings/balance-sheet forecast | Medium-term debt service and going concern | 'We can always refinance'. |
| Bank statements and undrawn committed facilities | Actual liquidity available | 'Term sheet equals committed cash'. |
| Aged creditors, unpaid taxes and wages | Arrears and statutory exposure | 'We have negotiated verbally'. |
| Covenants, cross-defaults, guarantees | Contractual acceleration and contagion | 'The bank has never called a default'. |
| Asset-sale valuations and completion dates | Realisable cash and probability | 'Book value will be received'. |
| Project/restructuring options and milestones | Whether a turnaround is credible | 'Sales will recover automatically'. |

**Director decision test:** Can this **legal entity** pay each debt when due under realistic scenarios? What additional debts will the next decision create? Obtain daily bank position, aged liabilities, rolling cash forecast, covenant headroom, lender confirmation, PAYG/GST/super status, feasibility of restructuring and written external specialist advice. **Red flags:** missed payroll, payment plans repeatedly broken, tax returns not lodged, unexplained overdrafts, supplier cash-on-delivery, directors asked to approve new orders without solvency analysis. **Decision:** stop or limit further debts, convene immediate board meetings, secure independent insolvency advice, develop and monitor a genuine course where lawful, or appoint an appropriate practitioner. **Minutes:** record date of suspected distress, cash information, alternative paths, s 588GA criteria (if applicable), professional advice, debt restrictions, action owners and review date; preserve the contemporaneous evidence.

## 01.13 Listed-company disclosure, insider information and market conduct

### 01.13.1 Two disclosure channels: listing rules and legislation

**AICD historical curriculum:** The board must oversee timely and balanced communication with markets, the integrity of financial and non-financial reports, and the control of confidential price-sensitive information. Listing Rule 3.1 is central, but it operates alongside legislation and other rules. AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 41-51, 63-71]; AICD[GAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 5-10]; AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 90-103]; AICD[LAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 6-7].

**Operative framework, Australia, 27 October 2026:** An Australian Securities Exchange (ASX) listed entity must ordinarily tell ASX immediately when it becomes aware of information concerning it that a reasonable person would expect to materially affect the price or value of its securities, under **ASX Listing Rule 3.1**. An information item is market sensitive if it would, or would be likely to, influence persons who commonly invest in such securities in deciding whether to buy or sell. The entity must comply with the exact definition of awareness, market sensitivity, and exceptions in the current ASX rules and Guidance Note 8 [L7]. The Corporations Act imposes overlapping obligations on *disclosing entities*: **ss 674 and 674A** concern listed disclosing entities, while **ss 675 and 675A** can apply to other disclosing entities. These are not four interchangeable penalty rules: their formulations and associated offence/civil-penalty requirements differ. For example, **s 674A** specifies a knowledge, recklessness or negligence element relating to material price effect for the civil-penalty pathway, whereas the offence wording in s 674 has its own elements. Both statute and listing obligations need analysis [L1, L7].

The obligation is imposed on the listed **entity**, with director/officer exposure arising through the applicable provisions, involvement, individual duties, or failure to maintain reasonable compliance arrangements. It is inaccurate to assume every disclosure failure automatically makes every director personally liable. Nor is it accurate to assume that board approval is required before every price-sensitive announcement. If management must disclose immediately, the board's arrangements should anticipate that rather than become a bottleneck.

### 01.13.2 Rule 3.1A: three cumulative conditions, not a broad confidentiality exception

ASX Listing Rule **3.1A** permits withholding particular information while **all three** cumulative elements hold: (i) one of the nominated categories applies, including an incomplete proposal or negotiation, insufficiently definite matters, internal-management information, breach of law if disclosed, or a trade secret; (ii) the information remains confidential and ASX has not formed the view that it ceased to be confidential; and (iii) a reasonable person would **not** expect it to be disclosed. The loss of any one condition requires renewed immediate-disclosure assessment. Rule **3.1B** allows ASX to require disclosure to correct or prevent a false market, potentially overriding a prior assumption about confidentiality [L7].

Consider a potential asset sale. Early confidential negotiation may fall within 3.1A. As terms firm up, counterparties multiply and market rumours accurately identify details, the conditions may cease. The board should not equate a signed confidentiality agreement with actual confidentiality. A trading halt may be a practical interim step, subject to ASX rules; it is not a substitute for making the announcement.

### 01.13.3 Announcements, forecasts and sustainability claims

Forecasts, project schedules, reserve/resources estimates, earnings surprises, major contract awards, litigation, refinancing, environmental incidents, cyber events, CEO changes and material impairment indicators may require disclosure if the legal tests are met. There is no general mandate to publish numerical earnings guidance simply because analysts want it, but where an entity has market-sensitive information or previously published statements become misleading, disclosure and correction issues arise. A board should evaluate the entire communication, including qualifications and omissions, not merely literal accuracy of selected sentences. Continuous disclosure must be integrated with the duty not to mislead under the Corporations Act and Australian Consumer Law where applicable.

Oil, gas and minerals issuers have additional **ASX Listing Rule Chapter 5** technical reporting requirements, including the relevant reporting codes and competent-person/reserves practices. A technically accurate resources announcement can still mislead where project economics, financing, permits or timing are presented without material conditions. Listed entities should apply the actual Appendix 5A/Chapter 5 rule relevant to their industry and security. Claims such as 'net zero', 'fully funded', 'final investment decision' and 'construction ready' require evidence consistent with their ordinary investor meaning.

### 01.13.4 Disclosure governance in practice

A credible framework has a named disclosure officer/committee; upward reporting from business units; triggers and thresholds; a current insider list; rules for external presentations and earnings calls; after-hours protocols; confidentiality practices; and a recorded basis for each significant announce/withhold decision. Materiality judgments should be reviewed by people capable of challenging the commercial and technical assumptions, not only by communications staff. A legal adviser can interpret obligations but cannot replace directors' knowledge of the underlying commercial facts. The board receives prompt copies of material disclosures, periodic exceptions and independent assurance on the system.

**Director questions and evidence:** What events could change our investment case or market valuation? How quickly does the board or disclosure officer hear of slippage, impairment, litigation or regulator contact? Obtain disclosure policy, training results, escalation logs, source-to-announcement reconciliations, market enquiry responses, announced guidance and actual trends. **Red flags:** material project facts buried in operating reports; different messages to analysts and ASX; selective disclosure to a major investor; market-sensitive data circulated outside a controlled group; ever-changing definitions of 'material'. **Decision/escalation:** immediately consult the disclosure lead and legal counsel, protect confidentiality, consider trading halt and determine whether corrective announcement is required. **Minutes:** record the facts known at the time, the materiality analysis, basis for any 3.1A exception, disclosure timing and responsibility; never manufacture an after-the-event rationale.

### 01.13.5 Insider trading, dealing windows and selective briefings

Corporations Act **s 1043A** prohibits insider trading in relevant financial products where a person possesses information that is not generally available and, if generally available, would be expected materially to affect price/value, and the other knowledge elements apply. Prohibited conduct covers acquisition/disposal or agreements to do so, procurement of another person, and specified communication/tipping. The law can reach executives, directors, consultants, relatives and anyone else meeting the statutory elements; it does not depend on whether the person was formally on a board. An internal 'trading window' is a governance overlay, **not** a statutory safe harbour: possession of inside information overrides permission to trade. Separate prohibitions address misleading or deceptive market conduct, market manipulation and false trading [L1].

**Director test:** Would the security be priced differently if the confidential fact were public, and does the person know the relevant nature of the information? Obtain the securities dealing policy, insider register, training, approvals and transaction reviews. **Red flags:** trades shortly before announcements, informal tips to family, project advisers not subject to access controls. **Escalate** suspected conduct for prompt independent legal assessment and evidence preservation. **Minute** the governance response without unnecessarily repeating inside information in widely distributed records.

Identify the regulator whose jurisdiction is engaged rather than assuming one corporate regulator supervises everything. ### 01.14.2 APRA and AUSTRAC: regulated-sector overlays

For an authorised deposit-taking institution, insurer or relevant superannuation institution, prudential governance under APRA instruments and licensing law materially raises board-specific expectations. For example, **CPS 234 Information Security** concerns security capability, controls, testing and incident notification; **CPS 230 Operational Risk Management** concerns critical operations, tolerance levels and service-provider risk, with a 2026 version effective from 1 July 2026. Application depends on regulated entity type and prudential standard; no ordinary charity is bound merely because it collects sensitive information [L16].

Anti-money laundering/counter-terrorism financing obligations under the **Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006** depend on whether an entity provides designated services and on the commencement of applicable reforms. Because reforms have staggered application, a responsible board obtains a service-by-service applicability opinion instead of assuming universal requirements. Check reporting-entity registration, the compliance officer, independent review, suspicious matters and outsourced controls where relevant.

### 01.14.3 Regulatory response should be proportionate and truthful

When notified of a regulator enquiry, the board should distinguish a routine request, suspected breach, investigation, statutory notice, enforceable undertaking, licence condition and prosecution. A legal hold should preserve relevant information; directors should avoid coaching witnesses, destroying records or issuing public statements not reconciled to verified facts. Preserve applicable legal professional privilege through proper procedures, recognising privilege does not immunise underlying facts. Where a whistleblower, victim, landholder or community group is involved, address their rights rather than treating the event solely as a 'reputation issue'.

**Director questions and evidence:** Which regulators can issue orders affecting our licence to operate or corporate status? Obtain regulator-contact register, licences/conditions, history of notices, periodic returns and designated response owners. **Red flags:** compliance manager cannot identify regulator deadlines; informal regulator contacts concealed from board; different internal and external accounts of a breach. **Decision:** classify seriousness, assign counsel and accountable executive, commission independent investigation where justified, determine immediate mandatory notifications. **Minutes:** chronology, legal advice received or sought, decisions and remediation gates; handle privilege thoughtfully.

## 01.15 Board oversight of the external legal environment

### 01.15.1 Common method: law applies to the activity, not just the corporate form

**AICD historical curriculum:** The Legal Environment organises external legal oversight into workers, operations, consumers, data, cybersecurity and artificial intelligence (AI). Culture and control design determine whether misconduct is promptly identified and corrected. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 74-112]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 31-33, 93-94, 98-99, 106-107, 139].

The board's question is not whether directors personally understand every regulation. It is whether the entity has identified its material legal exposure, built competent management arrangements, tested actual performance and escalated exceptions. Legal responsibility may attach to the organisation, directors/officers, senior managers or persons 'involved' in a contravention, depending on the specific Act. Governance oversight should distinguish those mechanisms rather than treating a corporate breach as automatically personal. The sections below apply a five-part test: **legal trigger; board question; evidence; red flag; action and record**.

### 01.15.2 Employment, wages, contractors and industrial relations

Australia's **Fair Work Act 2009**, modern awards, enterprise agreements, the National Employment Standards, workplace instruments, tax/superannuation and state regimes interact. Worker classification depends on applicable legal tests and the employment relationship, not the label 'contractor'. The Fair Work Ombudsman enforces many federal employment obligations. **Intentional underpayment** became a criminal offence under federal amendments from **1 January 2025**, with specific elements; errors are not all automatically criminal. Right-to-disconnect provisions began on different dates for larger employers and small businesses. Accurate payroll and reliable classification are therefore substantive legal-risk controls [L13].

**Board question:** How do we know everybody entitled to an award, overtime, allowances, leave, super and relevant minimum conditions receives them, including casuals, trainees, labour-hire workers and contractors? **Evidence:** payroll reconciliations to awards and enterprise agreements, independent employee sample tests, leave accruals, contractor classification, systemic remediation reporting and whistleblower trends. **Red flags:** historic underpayments treated as isolated processing errors, unexplained manual payroll overrides, executive incentives tied to low labour cost without compliance controls. **Decision:** remediate workers, self-report where legally required or strategically appropriate after advice, correct systems and accountability. **Minutes:** scale, estimated liabilities, privilege status, payment timetable and assurance tests. Do not treat pay remediation as a discretionary public-relations expense.

### 01.15.3 Work health and safety: officers' personal due diligence

Under work health and safety (**WHS**) Acts in harmonised jurisdictions, including the **Work Health and Safety Act 2020 (WA)**, a person conducting a business or undertaking (PCBU) owes primary duties; an **officer** owes a distinct **due diligence** duty under **s 27** to ensure the PCBU complies. Due diligence includes acquiring/keeping current safety knowledge; understanding operations and hazards; ensuring resources and processes; receiving/considering information; and verifying implementation. Scope and penalties are jurisdiction-specific. **Psychosocial hazards** such as bullying, sexual harassment, excessive demands, violence, isolation and inappropriate work design can be WHS risks; they are not limited to personal resilience or employee assistance programs. WA's WHS regulations and regulator guidance should be checked for the precise risk-management requirements [L13].

**Board question:** What evidence proves controls are effective at critical exposure points, beyond declining injury rates? **Evidence:** material hazard registers, hierarchy-of-control decisions, site visits, contractor incident reports, psychosocial assessment, worker consultation, leading indicators, critical control verification and regulator notices. **Red flags:** repeated high-potential near misses, workers discouraged from speaking, uncorrected control failures, reports that exclude contractors. **Decision:** stop unsafe work, allocate funding, appoint accountable leaders, obtain independent safety assurance and notify authorities as required. **Minutes:** identified critical hazards, tests, resources, due-diligence inquiries, unresolved controls and scheduled verification. A director should distinguish actual legal due-diligence elements from a voluntary target of 'zero incidents'.

### 01.15.4 Harassment, discrimination and positive duty

The **Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth)** imposes a **positive duty (s 47C)** on covered organisations and businesses to take reasonable and proportionate measures to eliminate specified unlawful conduct, including sex discrimination, sexual harassment, sex-based harassment, hostile workplace environments on the ground of sex and related victimisation, as far as possible. The **Australian Human Rights Commission (AHRC)** can investigate systemic compliance; this is more than responding to an individual complaint. General prohibitions, the Fair Work Act and WHS duties can also apply. Test the organisation's size, industry, workforce and risk profile when assessing proportionate measures [L13].

**Board question:** Are executives measuring risk and actual staff experience, or merely counting policy acknowledgements? **Evidence:** confidential survey trends, staff turnover, complaints, time to resolve, outcome equity, investigation independence, bystander training and leadership consequences. **Red flags:** repeated allegations involving senior executives; systemic confidentiality pressure on complainants; no monitoring after closure. **Decision:** protect complainants and respondents, commission proportionate independent review, prevent retaliation, set remediation milestones. **Minutes:** intervention and system assurances without disclosing unnecessary personal information.

### 01.15.5 Modern slavery, supply chains and community impacts

The **Modern Slavery Act 2018 (Cth)** requires covered entities with consolidated revenue at least **A$100 million** for the relevant reporting period, and certain other reporting entities, to prepare and submit an annual modern slavery statement meeting the Act's criteria. It is a reporting and due diligence transparency regime, not a statutory guarantee that supply chains contain no modern slavery. Reporting entities must evaluate structure, operations, supply chains, risks, actions, due diligence and effectiveness; relevant approval/signature and timing requirements apply. As of 27 October 2026 any proposed changes to threshold or penalties must be distinguished from enacted amendments [L15].

**Board question:** Where could coercion, child labour or exploitative recruitment arise in contractors and lower supply-chain tiers? **Evidence:** country/commodity exposure maps, labour-provider checks, worker grievance channels and outcome tracking. **Red flags:** boilerplate statements with no evidence, suppliers operating only through paper audits, red flags ignored because replacement costs are high. **Decision:** set risk-based audits, remediation and procurement actions. **Minutes:** approval basis for the statement, unresolved data gaps and monitoring commitments.

### 01.15.6 Whistleblower protection and speak-up systems

The **Corporations Act Part 9.4AAA** contains whistleblower protections for eligible disclosures to qualifying recipients about regulated entities and misconduct, including confidentiality, protection from victimisation and certain immunities. Certain classes of companies must have a compliant policy under **s 1317AI**; the scope is not 'every incorporated organisation'. Separate public-interest, tax and sector-specific whistleblowing laws may be relevant. Charities, associations and CATSI corporations must check the **applicable legal hook** before adopting a Corporations Act label. A functional speak-up process must be accessible to employees, contractors, volunteers and affected people where appropriate; handle disclosures according to the legislation actually applying [L1, L14].

**Board question:** Could a person safely report misconduct involving the CEO, chair or major funder? **Evidence:** independent intake channel, policy, eligibility matrix, case-management access rights, anti-retaliation measures, ageing and trend reports. **Red flags:** all complaints sent to implicated manager; retaliation hidden as 'performance'; allegations silently dropped after departure of subject. **Decision:** independent investigation and legal protection pathway, action on substantiated controls, escalation to chair or independent committee. **Minutes:** de-identified systemic matters; preserve confidentiality and privilege without leaving evidence of oversight absent.

### 01.15.7 Privacy, personal data and breach notification

The **Privacy Act 1988 (Cth)** and **Australian Privacy Principles** govern covered APP entities; scope depends on entity type, turnover, activities and exceptions (including some small-business exceptions, with qualifications). The **Notifiable Data Breaches scheme** obliges covered entities to assess a suspected eligible data breach expeditiously, generally within **30 calendar days** of suspicion, and to notify affected individuals and the OAIC when the statutory eligible-breach test is satisfied, subject to exceptions. 'Cyber incident' and 'eligible data breach' are not synonyms: personal information, likelihood of serious harm and remedial action matter. Privacy reforms of 2024 introduced phased changes, including a **statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy commencing 10 June 2025**; applicability and procedural elements differ from APP complaints [L14].

**Board question:** Can we explain why data is collected, who has access, how long it is kept, and how promptly it can be deleted or secured? **Evidence:** data inventories, retention schedules, third-party flow maps, breach simulations, consent arrangements, privacy impact assessments, incident log, technical test results. **Red flags:** unnecessary copies of identification documents, indefinite retention, staff using unsanctioned consumer AI with confidential personal data, third parties without enforceable security rights. **Decision:** stop unsafe processing, contain harm, assess notification obligations, preserve evidence and repair root causes. **Minutes:** factual incident chronology, likelihood/serious-harm assessment, escalation and oversight of remedial action.

### 01.15.8 Cybersecurity and critical infrastructure

Cybersecurity is a **business continuity, resilience and legal** issue, not solely an information technology (IT) problem. Direct duties may arise under privacy, contracts, financial services licensing, prudential standards, the **Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018** for relevant assets and the **Cyber Security Act 2024** for covered situations. In particular, the 2024 Act's ransomware/cyber-extortion **payment reporting** regime has specified eligibility criteria (including relevant Australian business turnover thresholds) and a **72-hour** reporting window after payment; it is **not** a universal obligation to report every attempted attack or any ransomware demand. Verify its scope and regulator requirements for the affected entity. Some security incident reports have separate 12/72-hour or other deadlines under critical-infrastructure or prudential regimes, which must not be conflated [L14, L16].

**Board question:** Could we maintain critical operations and meet mandatory notification deadlines during a widespread attack? **Evidence:** asset criticality, penetration-testing and remediation, multi-factor authentication, backup recovery evidence, supplier access, tabletop exercises, identity access controls and incident protocol. **Red flags:** backup never restored in a test, unpatched exposed systems, no external response adviser, security metrics limited to phishing clicks, contractor connections uncontrolled. **Decision:** prioritise systemic fixes and restore capability, activate crisis structure, involve specialists, notify as applicable and review disclosure obligations. **Minutes:** exercise outcomes, real risk acceptance with expiry dates, incident decisions, accountabilities and residual exposure.

### 01.15.9 Artificial intelligence: existing law first, emerging guidance second

As of **27 October 2026**, an organisation should not assume that a single general 'AI Act' replaces Australia's existing contract, privacy, IP, discrimination, product safety, negligence, financial services, company and consumer laws. Specific government uses or sectors can have mandatory policies, procurement conditions or regulatory expectations; general voluntary frameworks must be labelled as such. The board needs to separate **model capability** from lawful use and reliable outcomes. Governance must cover model data provenance, confidential-information leakage, automated discrimination, hallucinated advice, vendor dependency, intellectual-property rights, cyber attack and human accountability [L14].

**Board question:** Which decisions use AI, which could affect legal rights, and who can stop or override a model? **Evidence:** AI use-case register and risk classification, impact assessments, approved data flows, model evaluations, human review, change logs, vendor contract rights and incident reporting. **Red flags:** material credit/employment/customer decisions from untested generative systems; false precision in board papers; unlicensed copyrighted data or confidential board information entered into public models; 'AI did it' offered as responsibility explanation. **Decision:** prohibit specific unassured high-consequence uses, set guardrails for approval and continuous monitoring, obtain independent evaluation. **Minutes:** scope of approved use, residual risk and mandated verification. A board policy without adoption and evidence is not a control.

### 01.15.10 Competition and consumer protection

The **Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth)** prohibits cartel conduct (with civil/criminal routes depending on elements), certain anti-competitive agreements and practices, misuse of market power, and misleading or deceptive conduct under the **Australian Consumer Law** (ACL, Schedule 2). The ACL also regulates consumer guarantees, unfair contract terms, product safety and specific sales practices. Merely having market power is not itself illegal; conduct and the applicable statutory tests matter. Directors in trade associations, joint ventures, consortia or procurement collaborations face elevated information-sharing risks. Green, ethical, 'Australian made', safety, price and performance claims are subject to the same demands for substantiation [L15].

**Board question:** What competitors, suppliers and customers could be affected by proposed pricing, exclusive agreements or collaborations? **Evidence:** competition-law approval of information sharing, bid protocols, contract terms, product claims substantiation, recall procedures, customer complaints and training. **Red flags:** discussions of future competitors' prices; unjustified market allocation; sustainability claims based only on offsets without clear boundaries; intentionally ambiguous pricing. **Decision:** halt suspect exchanges, seek privileged legal advice, correct claims/terms and assess regulator/self-report obligations. **Minutes:** competing explanations, advice and corrective steps; do not sanitise the fact a problem was raised.

### 01.15.11 Environment, approvals, heritage and climate-related statements

The **Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (Cth)** (EPBC Act), state/territory environmental laws, pollution licensing, planning, water, waste, native title and cultural-heritage regimes can constrain projects and operating sites. A major development in **2026** is the establishment of the **National Environmental Protection Agency** on **1 July 2026**, under reforms passed in 2025. **New National Environmental Standards and related assessment rules have staged operation; not every test was operative on 27 October 2026**, even where instruments were already made. DCCEEW's October 2026 implementation updates must be consulted for precise phase dates and transitional arrangements [L15].

Director oversight must distinguish (a) approval eligibility, (b) conditions, (c) operational compliance, (d) remediation/closure liabilities, (e) downstream environmental impacts, and (f) sustainability reporting and misleading-claim exposure. Climate reporting obligations in the Corporations Act/Australian Sustainability Reporting Standards apply only to entities meeting the relevant phased thresholds and periods; they are not a blanket reporting requirement for all organisations. Indigenous land and cultural interests may generate separate legal decision rights and consultation/consent requirements that are not exhausted by environmental impact statements.

**Board question:** Which projects depend on approvals not yet granted, and what happens if conditions become more costly or prohibit development? **Evidence:** permits, consent/land access matrix, compliance monitoring, incidents, independent modelling, restoration security, sensitivity of project net present value to permit conditions, and regulator correspondence. **Red flags:** final investment decision assumptions treat ungranted permit as certain; unprovided closure costs; differences between public claims and internal emissions/impact data; cultural rights handled only as a communications exercise. **Decision:** condition capital commitments on verified legal and financial gateways, rectify breaches and seek affected rights-holder input. **Minutes:** legal dependencies, scenario consequences, thresholds, grounds for approval and stop/go triggers.

### 01.15.12 Intellectual property, confidential information and contracting

The principal intellectual-property (**IP**) regimes include the **Copyright Act 1968**, **Patents Act 1990**, **Trade Marks Act 1995**, **Designs Act 2003** and confidential-information principles. Ownership depends on the form of right, employment and contract terms, creation process, assignment and relevant statutory exceptions. A company buying software may acquire only a licence, not its source code or copyright. Open-source licences and AI supplier terms can affect ownership, redistribution and security. Outsourced services can create risks around data location, step-in rights, portability and survival of confidential information duties [L15].

**Board question:** Which assets create value because we control rights, and which depend on fragile licences or individual employees? **Evidence:** IP register, inventor/author assignments, licences, counterparty concentration, trade mark renewals, cyber/data clauses and dispute history. **Red flags:** IP in former employee's name; unclear rights to datasets, third-party images and AI outputs; business-critical licences expiring without alternatives. **Decision:** register/protect/assign rights as appropriate, improve contract controls, quantify switching and infringement exposure. **Minutes:** material rights and assumptions associated with strategic investments.

## 01.16 Compliance systems, escalation, legal registers and governance assurance

### 01.16.1 Compliance is an operating capability, not a policy library

**AICD historical curriculum:** Boards should oversee compliance cultures supported by legal understanding, regulatory scans, risk registers, responsible controls, monitoring and timely reports. The course distinguishes 'tone at the top' from lived behaviours and emphasises that bad news must travel to the board. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 55-73]; AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 59-79, 89-99]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 3-11, 68-80].

An effective compliance management system begins with the organisation's **legal obligations**, not a generic enterprise risk heat map. A regulation can require a specific notification within hours or impose a strict prohibition regardless of its assessed low probability. A risk appetite statement cannot authorise unlawful conduct. Risk-weighted resource allocation is reasonable; consciously ignoring a mandatory requirement because its probability-weighted expected cost is low is not.

A board-level compliance architecture typically comprises:

1. **Obligations universe:** entity and activity, law and section, jurisdiction, enforcement body, commencement/transition, threshold, licence condition, reporting calendar and consequence.
2. **Interpretation and owner:** accountable legal subject-matter specialist, management owner, required control, frequency and evidence record; counsel resolves ambiguous law.
3. **Controls and implementation:** approvals, segregation of duties, training, IT validation, site and contractor controls, contract language, data handling and accessible escalation.
4. **Monitoring:** independent or management sampling, changes in complaints/incidents, statutory filing completion, exception ageing and repeated control failures.
5. **Escalation:** thresholds for CEO/chair/committee/whole board, regulator notices, emergencies, suspected criminal conduct, whistleblowing and mandatory reportable events.
6. **Remediation and assurance:** containment, root-cause review, owner/timeframes, independent re-test, audit reporting and board closure acceptance.

### 01.16.2 A usable legal register records the actual trigger

| Register field | Example of useful content | Typical failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Entity / operating capacity | 'WA CLG registered as charity'; 'CATSI RNTBC for defined native title determination' | One undifferentiated group-wide entry. |
| Applicable instrument | Act + section, regulation + clause, licence condition, ASX rule, contract | 'All relevant laws'. |
| Scope / exemption / commencement | Revenue threshold, designated service, protected activity; effective date | Assume a bill or guidance is already binding. |
| Duty-bearer | Organisation, director/officer, committee member, responsible person, licensee or individual worker | Automatic personal liability attached to every rule. |
| Key control and evidence | Board approval gate, assurance sample, safety system or signed return | Document exists but no verification of actual operation. |
| Trigger and deadline | 'Within 72 hours after a covered ransom payment'; specific licence reporting date | A generic annual board review for time-critical obligations. |
| Failure route and escalation | To independent director/chair, board, regulator or external adviser | Only reporting to potentially implicated management. |
| Assurance and remediation | Last test, exception owner, due date, independent closure evidence | 'Closed' based solely on the original owner's email. |

### 01.16.3 Three lines, internal audit and external assurance

Operational management owns compliance in its activities; a legal/compliance/risk function assists and challenges; independent assurance evaluates design and operation. The familiar 'three lines' approach is a **governance model**, not a rule requiring three departments in a small association. A volunteer association may combine roles but must manage conflicts and independent challenge proportionately. Internal and external audit, legal experts, external specialists, regulator reviews and frontline worker evidence supply **different assurance scopes**. An auditor's clean financial opinion cannot reasonably be used as a certificate that all modern slavery, harassment, privacy, safety and native title duties have been met.

Assurance should follow the **risk and decision consequence**, rather than equal attention to every policy. A high-consequence WHS control may warrant periodic independent verification. A low-materiality filing can be confirmed through a log and exception checks. Conversely, a low-probability event with mandatory notification may justify rehearsed rapid escalation even if no major capital allocation is needed.

### 01.16.4 Non-compliance response: identify, contain, investigate, remediate, verify

If significant non-compliance is alleged, the first decision is whether activities should cease to protect people, assets, markets or evidence. The board should establish who has authority to investigate, how independent the process must be, what legal privilege is appropriate, whether regulators or affected persons must be notified, and how operations continue lawfully. Distinguish **allegation, evidence, finding, legal breach and remediation**; conflating them can harm both procedural fairness and accountability. Directors must not confuse a written legal opinion with implementation of the control it recommends.

**Director questions and evidence:** Which laws could materially suspend operations, expose people to harm or jeopardise registration? Are legal change alerts fed into operating processes? Obtain legal register, material controls register, filing/incident log, whistleblower trend analysis, audit programme and open remediation items. **Red flags:** breaches repeatedly listed as 'pending management action'; compliance functions lack access to chair; assurance plans omit suppliers or remote sites; actions closed without testing; near misses disappear from reports. **Decision:** enforce stop/go conditions, reassign owners where necessary, commission independent assurance, notify and remediate as required. **Minutes:** severity, evidence, protected information, independent advice, decisions, accountable owners, funding and dated verification gates. See related corpus files `02_Board_Effectiveness_Leadership_and_Culture.md`, `03_Strategy_Risk_and_Organisational_Resilience.md`, and `08_Directors_Toolkit_Cases_and_Practical_Applications.md` (planned, not yet written).

## 01.17 Liability, remedies, penalties, indemnities, insurance, advice and records

### 01.17.1 Liability is provision-specific: do not collapse civil, criminal and equitable consequences

**AICD historical curriculum:** The consequences of a director breach can include regulatory proceedings, civil pecuniary penalties, compensation, disqualification, criminal charges and personal financial loss. Directors should understand insurance, indemnities, access rights and the importance of contemporaneous evidence. AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 70-73]; AICD[LAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 1-4]; AICD[TOOL, 2025/26 V1, pp. 41, 60-65, 95-96].

For a **Corporations Act company**, care and diligence (**s 180**), good faith/proper purpose (**s 181**), misuse of position/information (**ss 182–183**) and relevant other statutory contraventions may be civil-penalty provisions. **Section 184** criminalises particular conduct under the separate statutory elements, including required dishonesty or recklessness; it does **not** criminalise every commercially bad judgment or every breach of s 180. Other Acts, including WHS, tax, competition and environmental legislation, have distinct offences, attribution and accessorial liability pathways. Under Corporations Act **s 79**, involvement may expose a person where the relevant statutory conditions are satisfied. Criminal wrongdoing requires the elements and criminal standard of proof; a civil claim is not proof of a crime [L1].

Civil remedies under the Corporations Act include a **declaration of contravention**, **pecuniary penalty** under the applicable current provision (including **s 1317G** where engaged), **compensation** (**s 1317H**), **disqualification** (**s 206C** and related provisions), and **injunctions** (**s 1324**). Equitable remedies can include an account of profits, equitable compensation or setting aside transactions where the relevant fiduciary law applies. The exact proceedings, standing, mental element, causation, maximum penalty, entity and date matter. Penalty-unit values and maximum civil penalty formulas change; do **not** treat the historical AICD printed dollar cap as a 2026 figure without recomputing under the current legislation. The purpose of disqualification may include protecting the public as well as deterrence [L1].

### 01.17.2 Indemnities: company funds cannot buy immunity from every breach

The Corporations Act **s 199A** restricts exemption and indemnification for specified liabilities. A company cannot simply promise to reimburse everything, including liabilities owed to the company or related body corporate and specified pecuniary penalties and compensation orders. The exact exceptions for third-party liabilities and legal costs, outcomes and circumstances must be checked. **Section 199B** restricts the company's payment of insurance premiums for some liabilities involving wilful breach of duty and improper use of position or information; s 199C addresses arrangements ineffective to the extent contrary to these restrictions [L1]. These rules matter even when a deed uses the phrase 'to the fullest extent permitted by law'.

A **deed of access, indemnity and insurance** is contractual protection, not a grant of additional authority to break law. It should address permissible indemnity, advancement of defence costs subject to conditions, repayment where indemnity is prohibited, access to files, confidentiality, duration, run-off coverage, third-party claims, regulator investigations and dispute resolution. Former directors remain exposed for acts while in office and often require ongoing access and run-off insurance. A deed must be adapted for a WA association or CATSI corporation rather than copied without checking the relevant law and rules.

### 01.17.3 Directors' and officers' insurance: what it may cover, and gaps

**Directors' and officers' (D&O) insurance** commonly distinguishes personal loss when the entity cannot indemnify; reimbursement when the entity lawfully indemnifies; and sometimes entity securities claims. Actual policies vary. Investigate limits shared across directors, deductibles, covered persons, civil penalties where legally insurable, defence-cost advancement, regulatory and inquiry costs, exclusions, retroactive dates, claims-made notification, insolvency, known circumstances, prior/pending litigation and post-resignation run-off. Insurance cannot make unlawful indemnification lawful. Civil penalties, dishonest conduct, personal profit, sanctions and some employment/cyber/environmental liabilities may be limited or excluded, subject to the precise policy and final-adjudication wording. More insurance is not a substitute for adequate internal controls.

A **small NFP** may be especially exposed if one programme accident, claim or fraud consumes its unrestricted cash; a **CATSI corporation** may need to consider whether insurance premiums and exclusions align with its lawful purposes and cultural/community obligations. A listed company may need a programme with multiple layers and geographic exposures. Insurance should be considered alongside cash reserves, enterprise liability cover, professional indemnity, cyber cover and WHS-related exclusions, not as a stand-alone governance test.

### 01.17.4 Access to documents, independent advice and privilege

Corporations Act **s 198F** gives a director a right of access to company books for board purposes; **s 290** covers access to financial records, and **s 198F** contains post-cessation provisions relating to access for relevant legal proceedings. AICD teaching and common practice encourage deeds that spell out continued access and copies of board papers. Access rights depend on entity type, circumstances and applicable law; a CATSI rule book or association statute may differ. **Legal professional privilege** belongs to the client and depends on the nature and purpose of the communication; it does not arise merely because a lawyer is copied. In the same way, marking a board paper 'privileged' is not conclusive [L1].

Directors may seek independent legal advice through processes in the charter or deed. A director facing potential conflict with the company, other directors or its counsel should consider whether a separate independent solicitor is necessary. Do not assume a solicitor retained by the company also personally represents every director. The board should preserve accurate copies of documents and avoid retrospective editing of minutes or deletion once a claim or investigation is reasonably anticipated.

### 01.17.5 Judicial relief: discretionary and exceptional

Corporations Act **ss 1318 and 1317S** permit courts, in their separate contexts, to relieve a person from specified liability if statutory requirements are met, including relevant honesty and fairness considerations. They are **discretionary**, not a right to forgiveness. In *ASIC v Healey (No 2)* [2011] FCA 1003 the Centro directors were not generally excused from liability despite being experienced and honest. In the James Hardie litigation, the court also refused to treat the approval of a crucial misleading announcement as a minor lapse. The weight given to honest reliance, seriousness and public accountability is fact-specific [J1, J2; AICD[LAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 3-4]].

**Director test:** What liability could attach personally under this *specific* Act and role? Obtain current D&O policy schedule/wording, disclosure proposal, deed, constitution, proof of policy payment, legal fee arrangements, statutory inspection rights and regulatory notices. **Red flags:** broad assurance 'you are fully insured'; policy lapse; insufficient aggregate limit; the company and director have divergent interests; pressure to destroy documents; indemnity claiming protection from deliberate wrongdoing. **Decision:** obtain independent advice where conflicts exist, resolve coverage before appointment or renewal, preserve documents, notify insurers within policy terms. **Minutes:** record the factual basis and the collective decision on indemnity/insurance, excluding a conflicted director where required, while preserving legal advice appropriately.

## 01.18 Judicial case analysis: authoritative holdings, limits and director lessons

### 01.18.1 Reading cases without overgeneralising

A reported judgment supplies a **holding on particular facts and legal provisions**, not a universal rule that every company, charity and association has identical personal duties. The following cases are principally **Corporations Act company** judgments; they inform judgment and governance across other organisations by analogy but do not independently create statutory duties under CATSI or associations law. Facts, issues, result, limits and board consequences are presented separately. High Court judgments below were checked against the Court's own judgment records. The Federal Court judgments are cross-checked against the Federal Court's official annual reports and published judgments where available; the difference in source type is expressly identified in the case register [J1-J7].

### 01.18.2 Centro: *ASIC v Healey* [2011] FCA 717 and *ASIC v Healey (No 2)* [2011] FCA 1003

**Material facts.** The directors of major listed Centro entities approved 2007 financial reports containing significant misclassifications of short-term liabilities as non-current and omissions of guarantees after balance date. Much relevant information was within board knowledge or should have been noticed on reading the financial statements. Board audit committees, management and external audit had participated in the reporting process [J1].

**Legal issue.** Did each director, including non-executive directors, exercise reasonable care and diligence when approving reports under **s 180(1)** and the connected statutory financial-reporting duties? Were reliance on management/auditors and the reporting process sufficient to discharge those duties?

**Holding.** The Federal Court found contraventions. The core reasoning was that approving financial reports demands a critical assessment by directors, who must read and understand the material, apply their knowledge of the business and inquire when inconsistencies are apparent. The audit committee's work and expert assistance are valuable, but the obligation to satisfy oneself about important obvious matters remains with each director. In the later relief proceedings the court declined to excuse the contraventions simply because the directors had acted honestly [J1].

**Limits.** The case does not require every director to be a technical accounting specialist or to independently re-audit figures; it concerns serious items which reasonably competent directors could identify and test. It does not establish that all balance-sheet classification errors constitute insolvent trading or necessarily mean the company cannot pay debts as they fall due. Personal obligations under financial-reporting law depend on whether the entity is subject to those provisions.

**Practical governance lesson.** Insist that board papers reconcile financing facilities, maturity tables, post-balance-date guarantees, covenants, liquidity and the directors' declaration. Examine materiality qualitatively as well as numerically. **Questions:** Which figures would change our solvency opinion and what do we already know that contradicts them? **Evidence:** complete accounts and notes, maturity ladder, audit exceptions, board-observed refinancing status. **Red flags:** unexplained reclassification, non-current debt that matures within months, management saying auditors alone are responsible. **Decision:** defer sign-off until reconciled. **Minutes:** document questions and substantive basis for resolution, not formulaic reliance.

### 01.18.3 James Hardie: *ASIC v Hellicar* [2012] HCA 17

**Material facts.** During a 2001 group restructure, James Hardie Industries Ltd established a foundation intended to address asbestos-related liabilities of separated subsidiaries. The company released an ASX announcement that conveyed misleading comfort about the foundation's capacity to fund existing and future asbestos claims. The directors contested whether they had approved the particular draft announcement. Board minutes recorded that a materially equivalent draft had been tabled and approved, and were subsequently adopted [J2].

**Legal issue.** Whether ASIC established directors' **s 180(1)** breaches in approving the misleading announcement, including disputed evidence about what happened at the board meeting; whether its failure to call a solicitor witness undermined its case.

**Holding.** The High Court restored the relevant findings that non-executive directors approved the misleading statement and breached their duties. The adopted minutes, considered with the rest of the evidence, could support that finding. The litigation also considered what fairness required of ASIC in presenting its civil penalty case [J2].

**Limits.** The finding is not that all inaccurate projections automatically create director liability, nor that minutes must always defeat direct evidence. The High Court evaluated the particular record, contents of the announcement, evidence and statutory duties. A press release's precision, underlying assumptions and investor impact are material to evaluating director conduct.

**Practical governance lesson.** Communications are governance decisions when they assert material facts. For terms such as 'fully funded', 'guaranteed', 'no further liability' and 'final', require the documentary basis and an honest description of qualifications. **Questions:** What would a reasonable investor understand, and does the evidence actually justify that understanding? **Evidence:** exact final announcement, draft versions, liabilities/funding model, legal and actuarial review. **Red flags:** announcement wording stronger than source advice; board approvals without seeing final version. **Decision:** revise or withhold misleading wording; delegate release only with clear controls. **Minutes:** accurately describe drafts considered and authority to amend; approve minutes promptly and correct genuine errors contemporaneously.

### 01.18.4 Senior officers and divided roles: *Shafron v ASIC* [2012] HCA 18

**Material facts.** Mr Shafron held the roles of company secretary and general counsel at James Hardie. His duties involved the restructure and communications associated with the asbestos compensation proposal. He argued that conduct in his capacity as legal counsel should be distinguished from the responsibilities of his office of company secretary [J3].

**Legal issue.** What duties did **s 180(1)** impose on a statutory **officer** with overlapping responsibilities, and could responsibilities be separated mechanically by job title?

**Holding.** The High Court dismissed his appeal. His actual responsibilities and contribution, including relevant legal and corporate governance tasks, informed the duty of care and diligence attached to his office. One cannot isolate duties by drawing artificial distinctions between functions the corporation entrusted to the person [J3].

**Limits.** Not every lawyer, external adviser or employee is a statutory officer. The analysis hinges on statutory officer status and responsibilities *within that corporation*. A solicitor advising from an external firm does not become an officer merely by giving influential advice; the actual facts and definition matter.

**Practical governance lesson.** Define officer roles realistically and ensure company secretaries and general counsel have explicit authority and obligations to escalate material legal or disclosure concerns. **Questions:** Who is accountable for warning that the board's announcement is inaccurate? **Evidence:** position descriptions, legal briefings, disclosure approval records. **Red flags:** one senior individual owns an issue but says neither role obliged them to act. **Decision:** clarify accountability without diffusing it. **Minutes:** record who gave legal/governance advice and who must follow up.

### 01.18.5 Functional status: *ASIC v King* [2020] HCA 4

**Material facts.** In a financial-services group, the group chief executive exercised overall control across entities. A subsidiary responsible entity drew on a facility intended to benefit an investment fund and deployed funds to pay debts of other group companies without equivalent benefit or repayment protection for the fund. The executive was not a formally appointed director of the responsible entity at the time [J4].

**Legal issue.** Did the person qualify as an 'officer' under the **s 9** definition because he had **capacity to affect significantly the corporation's financial standing**, despite lacking a formal role in that specific subsidiary?

**Holding.** The High Court held that a recognised office within the corporation was not necessary for the statutory limb in issue; factual capacity to affect significantly its financial position can be enough. The group boss could be an officer of the subsidiary on the relevant facts [J4].

**Limits.** Not every adviser, shareholder, influential financier or parent director is automatically an officer of each subsidiary. 'Capacity to affect significantly' and statutory context require evidence of real power, not simply general influence. This case did not abolish separate personality: rather, it addressed an individual's functional statutory status.

**Practical governance lesson.** Identify effective power as well as title, particularly in group treasury, funding, procurement and shared services. **Questions:** Who can direct entity cash and approve related-party transfers? **Evidence:** actual decision logs, authority matrices, treasury bank mandates, group correspondence. **Red flags:** subsidiary board approval is ceremonial; parent executive reroutes cash irrespective of entity-specific interests. **Decision:** ensure independent entity governance and proper funding consideration. **Minutes:** document subsidiary-specific benefit, risks, conflicts, approvals and financial capacity.

### 01.18.6 Corporation versus creditor claims: *Spies v The Queen* [2000] HCA 43

**Material facts.** A corporate officer faced criminal-law allegations connected to conduct affecting the position of company creditors. The proceedings raised whether directors' duties were directly owed to creditors when a company was in financial difficulties [J5].

**Legal issue.** Whether general law imposed a distinct fiduciary duty owed by directors **directly to creditors**, and whether the alleged criminal offence was supported by the supposed duty.

**Holding.** The High Court did not endorse an independent general fiduciary duty by directors to creditors simply because a company was insolvent or near insolvency. Creditors' interests can become relevant to duties owed to the company; separate statutory liability may also exist [J5].

**Limits.** This does not excuse creditor-prejudicing transfers, insolvent trading, fraud or breaches of statutory obligations. Other circumstances, such as a personally assumed responsibility or separate wrong, may create duties or remedies; each is legally distinct.

**Practical governance lesson.** During distress, avoid treating shareholder return or group support as the only interests at stake. **Questions:** What is the impact of this decision on creditors of this entity, and is the company capable of paying debts when due? **Evidence:** legal opinion on timing and liabilities, weekly cash forecast, related-party proposals and liquidator alternatives. **Red flags:** extracting value for parent/owners while debts to staff/tax/suppliers go unpaid. **Decision:** obtain insolvency advice and reconsider transfers or new debts. **Minutes:** corporate and creditor-interest analysis, not a blanket assertion that directors owe identical duties to everyone.

### 01.18.7 Group dealings and separate interests: *Walker v Wimborne* (1976) 137 CLR 1; [1976] HCA 7

**Material facts.** A group of companies was managed with overlapping directorships, financial transfers and advances among entities. The circumstances called into question transactions favouring related entities rather than the company making the transfer [J6].

**Legal issue and holding.** The High Court emphasised that each incorporated company is a distinct legal entity, so directors must consider the interests and legal position of the company whose powers they exercise rather than treating the economic group as one undivided owner of cash [J6].

**Limits.** The case is not an absolute prohibition on intercompany lending or group services. An appropriately authorised, adequately documented and commercially supportable transaction can be legitimate. It requires an entity-specific assessment of benefit, solvency, interests and consideration, with legal restrictions and guarantees examined.

**Practical governance lesson.** Group treasury structures need debtor-creditor agreements, repayment evidence, approved terms, liquidity rules and entity-specific authorisation. **Questions:** If this subsidiary stood alone, why would its board approve the transaction? **Evidence:** intercompany contracts, transfer pricing, forecast and covenant impacts, ownership controls. **Red flags:** transfers demanded solely by parent without subsidiary rationale; undocumented loans; hidden cross-guarantees. **Decision:** seek arm's-length protections or reject transactions. **Minutes:** the subsidiary's interests, legal authority and liquidity impact.

### 01.18.8 Corporate exposure from deficient conduct: *Cassimatis v ASIC* [2020] FCAFC 52

**Material facts.** The directors of Storm Financial exercised extensive influence over a financial-services business that gave high-leverage investment advice to financially vulnerable retail clients. The directors' practices exposed the company to a serious risk of statutory contraventions and harm [J7].

**Legal issue.** Whether directors who caused or permitted this business model and advice could contravene **s 180(1)** by creating a foreseeable risk of serious harm to their company through unlawful or deficient conduct.

**Holding.** The Full Court upheld relevant findings that directors breached the care and diligence duty. A corporation's likely exposure to statutory penalties, loss or reputational damage may be a material risk in assessing reasonable director conduct. The legal analysis considered the extent of the directors' control, foreseeable harm and the concrete duties involved [J7].

**Limits.** The result is not a universal rule that any company contravention proves a director's care breach. The underlying substantive wrong, prospective harm, actual responsibility and reasonableness must be analysed. A clear commercial strategy is not a substitute for lawful execution.

**Practical governance lesson.** Ask not just whether customers are buying or revenue is growing but whether the business model depends on conduct that exposes the company to unacceptable legal harm. **Questions:** Would independent legal and conduct testing support the product or advice given to vulnerable customers? **Evidence:** suitability assessments, complaints and breach reports, incentives and independent compliance samples. **Red flags:** management treats rapid sales growth as evidence of lawful conduct. **Decision:** suspend/remediate the product or conduct and commission independent review. **Minutes:** legal risk, alternate actions and controls. This logic can apply by analogy to consumer, safety, environmental and data businesses without simply copying financial-services obligations.

## 01.19 Six-context applicability matrix: listed, private, charity, association, CATSI and RNTBC

### 01.19.1 Classification precedes advice

The legal form, registered status, transaction, operating jurisdiction, participants and asset or right determine the applicable rule. A listed company may be the parent of proprietary and CATSI joint-venture entities; a company limited by guarantee (CLG) may be a registered charity; a CATSI corporation may also be a registered charity and a registered native title body corporate (RNTBC). **Membership is not the same as shareholding; charity registration is not itself a corporate form; RNTBC status is not a substitute for CATSI registration.** The same person may wear different hats with different duties depending on the entity and transaction. For multi-entity groups, do not apply one entity's board resolution to every legal counterparty without confirming authority.

### 01.19.2 Control mechanisms must fit each context

**Example A, ASX issuer:** The board approves a major acquisition subject to independent valuation and financing. Management identifies an unforeseen material tax liability. The board must revisit value, financing and any disclosure duty; it cannot treat the approval as irrevocable or insist market disclosure waits until next monthly meeting. A committee can test the risk but cannot substitute for a full-board decision reserved by charter and law.

**Example B, family proprietary company:** A founder-director proposes lending company funds to a related property venture. The question is not only whether the founder owns the shares. Directors must consider corporate benefit, statutory duties, conflicts, creditor capacity, approval/constitution and whether the loan is commercially supportable. A majority shareholder cannot always authorise prejudice to creditors or unlawful conduct.

**Example C, charity CLG:** A theatre charity proposes a profitable commercial venture outside its core objects. The board must first verify that the project is compatible with its charitable purpose, constitution, tax/concession conditions and proper management of charity assets, as well as commercial returns. ACNC Governance Standard 5 concerns the **charity's reasonable steps regarding Responsible People**, not a one-for-one application of company civil duties. A purpose-compatible commercial activity may be lawful, but trading profit does not automatically justify mission drift.

**Example D, WA incorporated association:** A volunteer committee member's family company wants to supply services. The committee applies **WA ss 42–43** and its own constitution and conflict procedures; simply citing Corporations Act s 191/195 does not establish the applicable legal duty. Approved terms must also be fair, authorised and consistent with the association's objects. For liquidity distress, consider WA **s 127**, not a claimed automatic entitlement to a Corporations Act safe harbour.

**Example E, CATSI corporation:** A corporation with community services and mining contracts must follow its registered rule book, CATSI statutory duties, contractual obligations and any cultural-authority arrangements actually authorised by its community. A non-member adviser should not be called a corporate director without a basis in law, although functional control may still create other risks. An ACNC registration adds charity duties but does not displace CATSI duties.

**Example F, RNTBC:** The board considers an agreement affecting native title rights. Even where every registered corporate member approves, the relevant native title **common law holders** may have consent rights under the Native Title Act and PBC Regulations. The board must identify the decision class, the holders affected and the legally required consultation and consent or certification process. It is wrong to infer that a corporate member vote replaces holder consent or that a director can invent culturally legitimate process on behalf of the community [L6, L9].

### 01.19.3 A cross-entity legal research decision tree

For every contentious matter, complete the following sentence in writing: **'The legal decision maker is [person/body] for [entity and capacity] under [specific instrument and clause], involving [affected persons/rights], subject to [conflicts, consent, threshold, disclosure and solvency], with [evidence and independent review]'.** If any bracket cannot be filled, the decision is not necessarily unlawful but is **not yet legally characterised**. Ask counsel to resolve the narrow uncertainty. Do not fill it by analogy to the closest ASX example.

**Director questions and evidence:** Which exact entity contracts or holds the asset? Is it both a charity and CATSI corporation, or a company and trustee? Obtain ASIC/ORIC/ACNC/state register extracts, constitution/rule book, licence/holder status, controlling agreements and dated legal applicability advice. **Red flags:** a group-wide board paper does not name contracting entity; charity says 'we follow company law' without checking modified application; RNTBC consultation only reached members. **Decision:** delay or condition the resolution where a required approval/consent is not established. **Minutes:** legal form, source of authority, affected rights, evidence of required approvals and the resolution's limitations.

## 01.20 Original practical director tests and decision records

The following six tools are original research instruments. They distil the legal and governance principles from this file but **do not reproduce AICD director tools**. They are designed for chair-led discussion, individual director preparation and use in a decision file. A 'pass' means the essential questions have a credible evidence trail, not that legal compliance is certified. Escalate unresolved legal questions to appropriately qualified advisers; the board then forms its own conclusion.

### 01.20.1 Test A: before joining or accepting reappointment

**Scenario:** An invitation to a paid or unpaid directorship comes with a polished strategy pack, assurances of insurance and a short deadline.

| Gate | Question requiring a substantive answer | Evidence to obtain | Failure signal/action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identity and purpose | Exactly which legal entity, purposes and registered status? | Registers, constitution, objects, company/ORIC/ACNC/state records | Unclear entity: **do not consent** until confirmed. |
| People and authority | Who controls decisions, and can I challenge management? | Board charter, directors list, delegation register, minutes, chair interview | Founder/veto-holder bypasses board: clarify powers or decline. |
| Solvency and financial health | Can it pay debts when due; are forecasts credible? | Three years' accounts, management results, 13-week cash and financing headroom | Repeated missed payments: specialist advice before acceptance. |
| Conduct and compliance | What legal or regulatory issues are open or historic? | Litigation, regulator notices, material incidents, independent audit, complaints | 'Nothing to see' without source material: obtain independent review. |
| Conflicts and time | Can I act for the entity with enough time and without disabling conflicts? | Conflicts register, other roles, meeting calendar | Frequent recusal on core decisions: declining may be prudent. |
| Protection and exit | Are insurance, indemnity, document access and offboarding appropriate? | Full D&O wording, deed, policy payment, constitution, removal rules | 'You're covered' without policy: do not rely on assertion. |

**Director decision:** Accept only if conflicts are manageable, role and standards are understood, time/skills are adequate, credible financial and compliance evidence is available, and material risks are accepted knowingly. It is reasonable to decline even a legally permissible appointment if independence, purpose or assurance are inadequate. **Document:** original review note, material omissions, expert advice, declaration/consent and an induction plan. **Post-appointment:** verify that board discussions match the assurances provided.

### 01.20.2 Test B: identify, disclose and manage a conflict

**Scenario:** A board member owns shares in a supplier; the company proposes a material multi-year contract. Ownership is known to some directors but has not been formally addressed.

1. **Identify** whose interests/duties are in tension: personal financial gain, association with relatives, nominee loyalty, another directorship, confidential information or fiduciary duty elsewhere.
2. **Classify law and entity:** company (ss 191–195/Chapter 2E), charity GS5, WA association ss 42–43, CATSI ss 268-1 onwards and relevant rule book. Ask whether a statutory exception applies rather than assuming all conflicts are automatically prohibited.
3. **Disclose promptly** and accurately enough for the board to identify the nature and extent. Consider related parties and indirect financial interests; do not use a blanket annual declaration in place of disclosure as a matter arises.
4. **Manage participation** according to statute and rules: attendance, information access, discussion, quorum, voting and approval. In some cases independent directors must deal with the item; consider member or regulator approval where required.
5. **Test substance:** Is the transaction within purpose, commercially defensible, priced independently, adequately documented and financially prudent? Recusal does not cure a poor or unlawful transaction.
6. **Verify record:** Minutes describe the disclosure, governing provision and exception if relevant, who was absent, quorum, independent review, resolution and monitoring.

**Red flags:** conflict 'approved' by the conflicted director; undocumented permission; board asked to choose a related party without competing bids or a defensible reason. **Decision:** take independent advice and defer if statutory approval, disinterested decision-making or transaction support is absent. **Escalation:** chair, independent directors, legal adviser, members or regulator as legally required.

### 01.20.3 Test C: challenge legality before approving a commercial opportunity

**Scenario:** Management seeks urgent final investment approval for a large project, arguing first-mover advantage, but essential permitting and land-user conditions remain unresolved.

| Question | Required evidence | Possible board response |
|---|---|---|
| Can we legally undertake the activity? | Permit/approval schedule, applicable state/federal law, legal opinion | Condition approval; no physical commitment until legal gateway. |
| Who can lawfully decide and give consent? | Entity rules, rights-holder/land access consents, authorised decision body | Refer to holders/members or regulator as required. |
| What has *not* been approved? | Approval caveats, environmental conditions, renewal/appeal risk | Make uncertainty explicit in base/downside economics. |
| Do the economics survive legal conditions? | Alternative designs, capital costs, delay sensitivities, financing covenants | Require revised valuation, option staging, stop/go thresholds. |
| Are public claims supportable? | Technical evidence, disclosure review, project definition | Rewrite announcement or constrain disclosure. |
| What is the reversible commitment today? | Contracts and break-fees, letters of intent, staged capital plan | Approve reversible limited spend or postpone full investment. |

**Red flags:** 'we will sort permits later' with material non-refundable spending; approval apparently held by different entity; risks expressed only in probability-impact colours. **Decision:** distinguish conditional exploration authority from irrevocable commitment. **Escalation:** legal specialists, native-title/cultural governance counsel, environmental regulator and independent technical reviewer as relevant. **Minutes:** legal gates, quantified financial/operational consequences, approvals outstanding, delegated signing authorities and explicit stop condition. This is compatible with value creation; it prevents irreversible capital commitment on an unverified legal assumption.

### 01.20.4 Test D: suspected non-compliance or whistleblower report

**Scenario:** An employee reports persistent wage underpayments and says senior management has suppressed evidence. The report reaches a committee member rather than the CEO.

**First 24 hours and immediate actions** depend on seriousness and applicable statutory deadlines, not a universal 24-hour legal requirement. Protect the reporter and potential victims, limit access to sensitive data, preserve payroll and communication records, and decide whether to stop an ongoing harmful practice. Confirm the person is a qualifying whistleblower if statutory protections are relevant, but do not deny confidentiality or protection simply because their classification is disputed. Establish who can investigate independently and whether legal advice is privileged.

**Within a documented investigation plan**, identify all potentially affected worker groups and periods, quantify liabilities, interview relevant people fairly, seek specialist advice, consider mandatory and discretionary notifications, and suspend implicated delegations where justified. Avoid prejudgment. If misconduct is substantiated, remediate individuals and system controls, determine accountability and review whether the board was previously misinformed. A small association may need an external investigator rather than a formal internal committee; a listed entity must also assess continuous disclosure if the problem becomes market sensitive.

**Director questions:** Who knew what and when? Could the same weakness occur elsewhere? Did incentives reward concealment? How do we test the fix? **Evidence:** original report/metadata, protected witness records, payroll data, audit findings and independent test. **Red flags:** retaliation, destruction of records, investigation by the person named, settlement conditioned on illegal silence. **Decision:** independent investigation with a reporting line to unconflicted board members; remediation and assurance timetable. **Minutes:** non-identifying allegations, protection steps, actions and findings as they mature, preserving privacy and privilege.

### 01.20.5 Test E: financial distress and new debt

**Scenario:** Accounts show an annual operating loss, the CFO forecasts break-even next year, cash is low, several tax payments are overdue, and the CEO asks for approval to order inventory on 60-day credit.

**Never decide insolvency from the loss alone.** The legal question for a company is ability to pay debts when due, using a realistic cash assessment under **s 95A**. Examine weekly 13-week liquidity and wider cash forecasts; bank statements; legal commitments and acceleration; committed financing; ageing of payables; payroll, super and tax; inventory sales conversion; and lender/vendor terms. Separate reasonably expected incoming finance from speculative support.

**Decision ladder:** (1) Can the entity reasonably meet liabilities when due? If yes, assess new borrowing/credit under normal care and risk rules. (2) If there are reasonable grounds to suspect current or prospective insolvency, seek immediate insolvency-specialist advice and reassess the incurring of any new debts. (3) If exploring **s 588GA** safe harbour for a company, record why the restructuring course is reasonably likely to achieve a better outcome than immediate administration/liquidation, the qualifying conditions, linked debt and real-time milestones. (4) If evidence no longer supports the plan, or creditor prejudice increases, reassess external administration promptly. (5) For WA associations use **s 127**, for CATSI the **s 531-1** application of insolvency provisions, and for ACNC charities include GS5, rather than pretending these are one statute.

**Red flags:** overdue super/wages, tax filings not lodged, facility not binding, unexplained daily balances, debt incurred to keep appearances up, speculative sale proceeds treated as cash, safe harbour invoked without a course of action. **Minutes:** contemporaneous cash figures, dissent, advice, alternative resolutions, debt restrictions, follow-up and stop/go criteria. Decisions should be revisited at the frequency required by the cash risk, potentially daily, not only at the next ordinary meeting.

### 01.20.6 Test F: record a board decision that can be audited

A useful minute records **decision authority, material facts, alternatives, key questions, conflict treatment, independent advice, reasons at an appropriate level, vote or resolution, conditions and accountability**. It does not need verbatim transcripts or excessive sensitive data. Good minutes show that directors thought about the decisive questions and took actions, rather than asserting formulaically that 'the board considered its duties'. An inaccurate minute can mislead the board and, as James Hardie illustrates, become significant litigation evidence. The minute should not be constructed to create a defence after the event.

**Original decision-record template (illustrative; adapt to law and context):**

> **Matter / legal entity / date:** [decision title, entity identity, meeting date].
>
>
> **Authority and governing instruments:** [relevant statute/rule/charter, approval threshold and quorum].
>
>
> **Purpose and commercial rationale:** [what the organisation seeks to achieve, consistent with objects].
>
>
> **Evidence reviewed and material uncertainties:** [papers, cash/risks, technical and external advice; missing evidence].
>
>
> **Conflicts and attendance:** [declarations, recusal, statutory rules and eligible decision makers].
>
>
> **Options and challenge:** [credible alternatives, cost/opportunity and downside scenario, minority questions].
>
>
> **Decision and conditions:** [full resolution, threshold or contingencies, approval limits, not an implied broader authority].
>
>
> **Escalation and assurance:** [name/role, milestones, independent verification, next reporting date].
>
>
> **Disclosure/notification:** [market/regulator/member/common law holder steps where legally required].

The records must use true facts, not fill-in boilerplate. Directors should review whether their reasons were recorded fairly, without treating meeting minutes as a substitute for the substantive decision process. Where individual dissent is material, the director should seek an accurate record, continue reasonable engagement, obtain advice if appropriate, and consider their ongoing role. A recorded dissent is helpful evidence but **does not automatically discharge individual duties**.

### 01.20.7 Combined director gate: legality, evidence, value, risks, accountability and records

Use the six questions below as a final pre-vote 'decision integrity' check:

1. **Legality:** Which entity may lawfully act, under what authority, for what purpose, and with whose approval/consent?
2. **Evidence:** Which crucial facts are reported, derived, estimated, disputed or unknown, and who has independently challenged them?
3. **Value:** What is the defensible benefit to *this* organisation, including downside, opportunity cost, recoverability and funding?
4. **Risk:** Could the action expose people, creditors, rights holders, assets or the organisation to material harm or statutory breach? Do the proposed controls work in practice?
5. **Accountability:** Who decides, implements, monitors, independently assures and escalates, and how quickly must the board revisit the decision?
6. **Record:** Would an informed successor understand why the decision was reasonable given the facts actually known at the time?

A board should not seek a perfect evidence set at any cost. The amount of inquiry should be proportionate to materiality, reversibility, time pressure and legal consequence. For an irreversible project, complex related-party contract, distressed company or native-title right, an apparently small unanswered question may be a critical approval barrier. For an ordinary low-risk operational choice, an appropriate delegation and exception report can be sufficient. Governance quality is the ability to distinguish the two.
