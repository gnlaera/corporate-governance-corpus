**Status:** Independent research and board-study handbook. Not legal, accounting, fundraising or tax advice. Legislation and regulator rules change; confirm the operative rule for the entity, jurisdiction, reporting period and transaction before relying on it.

> **Central proposition.** A not-for-profit (NFP) board governs an organisation whose resources are held for a purpose rather than for distribution to private owners. Its test of performance is therefore two-dimensional: *is the organisation making a demonstrable difference, and can it do so lawfully, safely and sustainably?* Neither fundraising growth nor an accounting surplus alone answers those questions. A charity adds an Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission (ACNC) regulatory layer; incorporation supplies the underlying legal entity; neither label replaces the other.

## [06.01] Not-for-profit identity, charity, public benefit, surplus and mission protection

### [06.01.01] Five labels that must not be conflated

| Term | What it actually describes | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Not-for-profit (NFP)** | A purpose and distribution constraint: income and assets are applied to organisational purposes, with no ordinary entitlement to distribute private profits to members or controllers. | That the organisation is incorporated, an ACNC charity, tax-exempt or a deductible gift recipient. |
| **Charity** | An entity with charitable purposes and public benefit (or a legally applicable exception), satisfying the *Charities Act 2013* (Cth) and registered by the ACNC if described as an ACNC-registered charity. | A particular legal vehicle or automatic entitlement to every tax concession. |
| **Incorporated association** | A separate legal entity registered under a particular state or territory association law, ordinarily with a committee, members and registered rules. | That its purposes are charitable or that the ACNC regulates it. |
| **Company limited by guarantee (CLG)** | A public company formed under the *Corporations Act 2001* (Cth), with members' limited contribution on winding up and no ordinary share capital. | That it is an NFP or a registered charity without reviewing its constitution and status. |
| **ACNC registration** | Commonwealth recognition and continuing regulatory duties of a charity, layered over its underlying legal vehicle. | Incorporation by the ACNC, Australian Taxation Office (ATO) deductible gift recipient (DGR) status, or exemption from all other laws. |

**Illustration:** A Perth amateur sports club may be a WA incorporated association and an NFP without being a charity. A welfare service may be both a WA incorporated association and an ACNC charity. A nationwide health charity may use a CLG. An Aboriginal corporation may be registered under the *Corporations (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander) Act 2006* (Cth) (CATSI Act) and with the ACNC. These combinations yield different governance and reporting answers.

**Authority:** *Charities Act 2013* (Cth), ss 5, 6, 12–15 (charity definition, NFP and charitable purposes/public benefit); *Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission Act 2012* (Cth) (ACNC Act), Division 25; [ACNC, charity registration](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/start-charity); AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 9–20, 41–50].

### [06.01.02] Charitable purposes and public benefit

The *Charities Act 2013* (Cth) provides the statutory definition. Charitable purposes include, subject to the statutory provisions, advancing health, education, social or public welfare, religion, culture, reconciliation and mutual respect between groups, human rights, security or safety, the natural environment, and purposes beneficial to the general public analogous to recognised charitable purposes. Some entities may qualify through prevention or relief of poverty, distress or disadvantage. **An attractive cause is not necessarily a statutory charitable purpose.** The organisation must be established and operated for qualifying purposes rather than for private gain. Incidental or ancillary non-charitable purposes may be permissible under the statutory tests; a substantive independent non-charitable object can defeat registration. Public benefit is not synonymous with universal access: the beneficiaries may be a sufficiently public class, subject to specific statutory and legal exceptions.

Practical purpose test: define *who* is served, *what change* is intended, *how* activity advances an eligible charitable purpose, *how private benefit is controlled*, and *what evidence shows beneficiaries rather than insiders receive the value*. A tightly framed purpose protects governance: it narrows strategy, investment, fundraising claims and permissible changes to the constitution. [LAW] *Charities Act 2013* (Cth), Part 2; [ACNC, charitable purposes](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/start-charity/charity-subtypes).

### [06.01.03] Surpluses, commercial activity and legitimate payments

An NFP **may earn fees, hold investments, trade, budget for surpluses, accumulate reserves, employ staff, pay market remuneration and purchase services**. The constraint is primarily *who may receive the residual benefit and for what purpose*, not whether revenue exceeds expenses. Reasonable pay for real work, substantiated expense reimbursement and arm's-length procurement can be legitimate. Dividends or transfers of residual assets to members by virtue of membership ordinarily are not. A board should scrutinise de facto distributions through inflated contractor invoices, favourable leases, family employment, over-generous entertainment and transactions without real services.

Three financial classifications must remain separate: **donated** describes *source* of money; **legally or contractually restricted** describes *conditions on use*; **unrestricted** describes money available within organisational objects, budgets and lawful commitments. A donation can be unrestricted. A grant can be restricted even if called income. A board-designated reserve is a voluntary internal allocation and may be redesignated by the board unless another legal restriction attaches; it is not the same as donor-imposed conditions. Cash received is not automatically cash available for general expenditure.

### [06.01.04] The mission lock and the board's real constituency

Members of an NFP do not ordinarily own its net assets. The board is accountable to the legal entity and its purposes while respecting enforceable rights of members, donors, beneficiaries, regulators, creditors and, where applicable, native title holders. A popular donor or founding member does not acquire a general right to redirect the mission. The constitution's objects, non-distribution clauses and winding-up asset transfer provisions are therefore more consequential than their boilerplate appearance suggests.

A useful board question is: **If this initiative makes the organisation larger but produces less benefit per dollar, would we still approve it?** Also ask whether a prospective sponsor would narrow access to services, whether grant conditions distort service priorities, and whether a well-funded initiative consumes the capacity needed for a smaller but more effective program. [AICD] GOV pp. 35–51, 80–88; RAPP pp. 23–26.

### [06.01.05] Purpose and performance are inseparable from legitimacy

Charities rely on a multi-sided grant of legitimacy. The law permits the vehicle; members and donors fund it; employees and volunteers deliver it; government may commission it; and beneficiaries or communities judge whether it meets their needs. These parties have different influence and legal rights. Listening is essential, but stakeholder pressure does not change statutory objects or substitute for a valid board decision. The analytical centre is *benefit achieved within legal, financial and ethical constraints*.

**Evidence to retain:** dated objects review, ACNC Charity Register extract, current governing rules, donation conditions, annual impact assessment, stakeholder map, policy on reimbursements and remuneration, and minutes explaining how proposals advance charitable purposes.

## [06.02] Choosing the legal vehicle

### [06.02.01] A structured choice, not a status hierarchy

The most appropriate vehicle depends on geographic reach, capital needs, membership rights, contractual exposure, tax prospects, administrative capacity, assets, founder succession and cultural/community authority. **A CLG is not inherently better-governed than an association; its national form is more suited to some operational footprints.** Conversely, a small association should not retain a structure that makes national contracting, group reporting or director recruitment unnecessarily difficult.

| Vehicle | Legal features | Comparative advantages | Principal limitations and board cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **State/territory incorporated association** | Separate legal person under local Act, elected or appointed committee, members and registered rules. | Economical community membership vehicle; rules aligned with local clubs and services. | Different laws in eight jurisdictions; activity across borders, eligibility, reporting and winding-up rules; larger projects may outgrow it. |
| **Company limited by guarantee** | Corporations Act public company; members guarantee a nominated sum on liquidation; directors and constitution. | National structure, widely understood by lenders, major funders and counterparties; flexible governance. | Company secretarial rules, ASIC interaction and member rights; charity exemptions must be mapped, not presumed. |
| **Charitable trust** | Property held by trustee under trust deed; trustee may be individual or incorporated. | Suitable for endowments, purpose-specific assets and long-term donor conditions. | Trust deed, fiduciary standards, trustee liability, variation powers, beneficiary/charitable enforcement; trust has no conventional democratic membership. |
| **Co-operative** | Co-operative entity under relevant state/territory co-operatives national law application; member participation and active membership rules. | Member-controlled mutual service models. | Special distribution/membership rules and regulator arrangements; NFP status must be separately established. |
| **Unincorporated group** | No ordinary separate corporate personality. | Informal, short-lived activity with limited property/contract needs. | Personal contractual or tort exposure, difficulty holding assets and employing staff, governance continuity risks. |
| **CATSI corporation** | Separate corporation under CATSI Act, ORIC regulator, rule book, membership and directors. | Specialised Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander corporate framework; can support culturally designed representation and native title roles. | Distinct statutory duties, reporting and ORIC powers; ACNC is additional layer, and native title decision rights may lie outside corporate membership. |

**Legal references:** *Corporations Act 2001* (Cth), Part 2A.2 and Part 2H.1; *Corporations (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander) Act 2006* (Cth); relevant associations Acts at §06.07. [AICD] GOV pp. 9–20; LEG pp. 11–17; LAPP p. 5 (historical comparison only).

### [06.02.02] Questions before incorporating or converting

1. Is the purpose charitable, and is public benefit substantiated, or is this a mutual member activity that should **not** seek ACNC registration?
2. Who should elect governors, approve fundamental changes, access records, and receive dispute resolution rights? Can the membership actually exercise those rights?
3. Will employees, leases, high-risk activities, overseas operations, children or long-term contracts materially increase liabilities?
4. Will the entity operate interstate or internationally, hold significant assets, or own subsidiaries? Will counterparties or lenders require a particular vehicle?
5. Does a donor's gift or trust place restrictions on conversion, title transfer or disposal? Can it be changed at all?
6. Are First Nations community consent, CATSI membership, or native title rights relevant? If yes, do not default to an ordinary CLG without community mandate and specialist assessment.
7. What are the aggregate costs over five years, including audit, membership administration, tax, annual returns, director identification and regulator updates?

### [06.02.03] Conversion or restructuring requires more than a resolution

When a WA association becomes a CLG, separately verify: power and required member resolution under the association Act and rules; incorporation/registration procedure; asset and contract succession or transfers; property titles, leases and encumbrances; employment and intellectual property; deductible gift recipient endorsement and ACNC changes; controlled-entity consolidated accounts; liability continuity; donor and grantor consent; and whether registrations and fundraising permissions continue. A conversion can simplify nationwide operations yet introduce additional reporting, governance and transactional work. **Never infer an automatic transfer of restricted gifts or native title assets.** [AICD] FAPP pp. 7–14 illustrates Surf Life Saving Queensland's historical change of legal form, not a standard process applicable to every association.

### [06.02.04] Scale and complexity are independent dimensions

An association holding $50,000 but providing unsupervised children’s services may need more safeguarding governance than a foundation with several million dollars of low-risk invested assets. A $15 million annual government service contractor may need financial controls comparable with a large company even though there are no shareholders. Governance intensity should track **risk to people, financial exposure, contractual commitments, complexity and reputation**, not only revenue. Written delegation and assurance become more important as volunteers transition into employees or shared leadership.

## [06.03] Constitutions, registration, objects, membership and winding up

### [06.03.01] The constitutional chain of authority

[LAW] A board must be able to trace a decision from law to registered rules or constitution, then to the decision-making body, notice, quorum, conflict handling and resolution. A registered association cannot simply use generic company replaceable rules; a CLG cannot operate only by an informal committee handbook. An ACNC-registered entity must keep its governing document and registration circumstances consistent with charity law.

A sound constitution ordinarily addresses name and objects; no-private-distribution restriction; membership classes, joining, resigning and discipline; member register; member voting and general meetings; committee/board composition, eligibility, election and terms; conflicts; removal and vacancies; governance powers and delegations; banking and finance; disputes and procedural fairness; amendment; and allocation of surplus assets on winding up. Jurisdictional laws may prescribe mandatory rules or deemed model rules, including protections for member access to records or grievance procedures. [AICD] LEG pp. 11–17; GOV pp. 52–79.

### [06.03.02] Registration sequence

The legal entity is created or recognised by the company, CATSI, trust or association framework. **ACNC registration is a distinct decision about charitable status.** The application should be supported by an accurate governing document, explanation of activities, public benefit, accounts and governance arrangements. Check ABN and ATO endorsements separately; see §06.09. For a WA association, verify eligibility and minimum membership under the WA Act; for CLGs, complete ASIC formation and ongoing obligations; for a CATSI corporation, follow ORIC registration/rule book procedures.

A useful pre-submission test is to ask whether the actual activity evidence supports every charitable purpose claimed and whether the organisation's *non-charitable* functions are genuinely ancillary. A charitable label in the constitution is not enough where operations contradict it. [REGULATOR] [ACNC registration checklist](https://www.acnc.gov.au/tools/guides/registration-application-checklist-and-guide).

### [06.03.03] The difference between members and beneficiaries

A member's rights derive from statute, constitution/rules and valid agreements. Typical rights include notices, votes on eligible matters, election of committee members and participation in special resolutions; exact rights vary. A beneficiary may be affected profoundly by the organisation's decisions but may have no vote. The response should be designed **stakeholder voice with procedural fairness**, not the fictional statement that beneficiaries automatically have shareholder-style rights. In many organisations the board should hear beneficiaries through advisory groups, surveys, service-user representation or independent complaints processes. RNTBC common law holders may have separate, enforceable decision rights not dependent on membership (§06.16).

### [06.03.04] General meetings and constitutional amendments

Do not use one default AGM rule across Australia. State association Acts prescribe different annual meeting times and filing requirements (§06.07), and a charity CLG must consider the specific Corporations Act modifications for ACNC-registered charities (§06.06). Formal amendment procedure includes valid notice and special resolution where required, correct voter eligibility/quorum, any donor or charitable purpose constraints, regulator approval or filing, and communication to members. Changing rules about who may be a member or who can remove directors can alter control and accountability more than a change in headline purpose.

**Model amendment approval record:** identify clause and present legal text; record the problem and alternatives; attach legal advice where purpose or assets change; confirm members' notices and thresholds; list grants/trusts possibly affected; record each conflict; state commencement and filing actions; appoint an owner to update ACNC and legal register. An amendment approved by members but never filed may not become effective under the applicable law.

### [06.03.05] Winding-up clauses and purpose-locked assets

NFP constitutions typically prevent distribution of surplus property to members and require lawful transfer to an eligible NFP, charity or other defined recipient. A DGR may have a separate gift-fund transfer requirement. Some assets are not the entity's freely distributable property at all: held on trust, secured to a lender, subject to a reversion clause, subject to government grant repayment rights, or held under native title arrangements. Discharge liabilities and determine restrictions *before* identifying eligible recipients. Dissolution of a legal entity, cancellation of ACNC charity registration and revocation of DGR endorsement are different legal events; each requires separate action (§06.15).

### [06.03.06] Constitution review red flags

Warning signs include contradictory objects; no meaningful membership admission or discipline procedure; unresolved quorum for conflicts; a chair apparently empowered to override all decisions; vague executive delegations; unbounded term limits; only an aspirational conflict clause; amendment provisions inconsistent with the statute; no valid winding-up recipient; outdated charity tax/DGR clauses; and operating practices inconsistent with governing rules. A lawyer should review fundamental defects; the board should fix day-to-day procedural defects through clear minutes, formal policy and the legally necessary amendments rather than ignoring its constitution.

## [06.04] ACNC Act, six Governance Standards and External Conduct Standards

### [06.04.01] What is binding, and on whom?

[LAW] The *ACNC Act 2012* establishes registration, regulatory powers, reports and standards. The operative delegated instrument is the **Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission Regulations 2022** (not the superseded 2013 regulation, although some legacy material refers to it). Registered charities generally must meet the six Governance Standards under ACNC Act s 45-10 and Regulations Part 45. **Basic Religious Charities** have a specific Governance Standards exception, but they are not exempt from every charity obligation or from relevant External Conduct Standards; National Redress Scheme consequences also interact with Basic Religious Charity status. [Official Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2012A00168/latest/text); [2022 Regulations](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L01301/latest/text); [ACNC standards](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/governance-hub/governance-standards).

### [06.04.02] The six Standards: operative idea and evidence

| ACNC Governance Standard | What the **charity** must achieve | Practical evidence/questions |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Purposes and not-for-profit nature** | Maintain and pursue a qualifying charitable purpose and operate without private distributions; be able to show it. | Objects, activity/impact reports, non-distribution clause, funding map. Has mission shifted? |
| **2. Accountability to members** | Take reasonable steps for accountability to members and opportunities for member questions/concerns, subject to relevant legal form. | AGM or equivalent member communication, accessible accounts, feedback responses, member-rights log. Does membership have genuine information? |
| **3. Compliance with Australian laws** | Not engage in specified serious unlawful conduct that may jeopardise registration; understand relevant Australian legal obligations. | Regulatory register, breach escalation, legal advice, material incident reporting. Do serious compliance issues reach the board? |
| **4. Suitability of Responsible People** | Take reasonable steps to ensure those governing are not disqualified under specified tests and act if disqualified. | Initial and periodic checks, signed eligibility statements, ACNC/ASIC registers, appointments log. |
| **5. Duties of Responsible People** | Take reasonable steps to ensure Responsible People are subject to, understand and carry out the six specified duties. | Duties induction, conflict register, financial dashboard, solvency checks, decisions and corrective action. |
| **6. Maintaining and enhancing public trust and confidence in the Australian not-for-profit sector** | **Specific National Redress Scheme duty:** where the charity is, or is likely to be, identified in relevant institutional child sexual abuse redress processes, take reasonable steps to participate in the Scheme. | Historic institutional exposure review, receipt of scheme notifications, legal advice, participation or documented steps. This is **not** a catch-all 'good reputation' rule. |

**Scope precision:** Standard 2 has application nuances for charitable trusts and entities without conventional members; read the legal text and ACNC guidance rather than pretending all charities hold the same AGM. Standard 3 is not a general ACNC enforcement power over every minor breach; serious relevant conduct and its statutory formulation matter. Standard 6 has a narrow Redress Scheme trigger, although broader safety and reputation controls remain important under other law. [REGULATOR] [ACNC governance hub](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/governance-hub/governance-standards); [Standard 6](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/governance-hub/6-maintaining-and-enhancing). [AICD] GOV pp. 41–49, teaching baseline only.

### [06.04.03] Governance Standard 5 is a charity-level reasonable-steps obligation

The precise legal statement is: **the registered charity must take reasonable steps to ensure its Responsible People are subject to, understand and comply with the designated duties.** Those duties concern reasonable care and diligence; good faith in the charity's interests and for its purposes; proper use of position and information; disclosure of real or possible conflicts; responsible financial management; and not allowing insolvent operation. The governance standard is **not automatically identical to every personal statutory director duty in Corporations Act ss 180–184**. The underlying legal form supplies parallel, modified or additional duties; criminal misconduct provisions, common law and specific association/CATSI legislation can continue. The actual penalty and enforcement pathway must be tested in the proper regime. [ACNC, Governance Standard 5](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/governance-hub/5-duties-responsible-people); [ASIC charity obligations](https://www.asic.gov.au/for-business-and-companies/nfps-and-charities/registered-charities-obligations-to-the-acnc-and-asic/); AICD[LEG, 2025/26 V1, pp. 18–54].

A reasonable-steps program includes written appointment terms, an actual induction, a relevant conflicts policy, accessible financial data, review of board attendance and contributions, escalating concerns and acting on repeated breaches. A signed policy unread by members is weak evidence; training combined with documented enquiry and corrective action is stronger. Not every error is a breach, nor does volunteer status eliminate governing responsibility.

### [06.04.04] Four External Conduct Standards where relevant

An ACNC-registered charity **operating outside Australia, or sending money/resources overseas for another organisation to use**, may engage the External Conduct Standards, including activities delivered through partners; the amount need not be large. The standards are broadly: **1 Activities and control of resources** (control overseas resources and activities); **2 Annual review of overseas activities and record-keeping** (adequate country/program evidence); **3 Anti-fraud and anti-corruption** (prevent and respond to corrupt conduct); **4 Protection of vulnerable individuals** (safeguarding). [LAW] ACNC Act s 50-10; Regulations Part 50; [ACNC overseas standards](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/governance-hub/acnc-external-conduct-standards).

This demands more than a signed grant agreement. Know who receives resources, sanctions/bribery exposure, how partners are vetted, what evidence of actual service delivery exists, whether benefits are diverted and how beneficiaries report harm. A board should approve a **country-risk and partner due diligence matrix** before an overseas initiative, and should not assume its Australian protections automatically govern local staff or partners. See Early Gifts case §06.17.02.

### [06.04.05] Reporting, information and regulatory response

Keep ACNC registration details current, advise changes in Responsible People, address or purposes within applicable time limits (generally **28 days for medium/large and 60 days for small** for notifiable changes), submit reporting as applicable and answer regulator requests accurately. A charity may have to notify the ACNC of significant governance breaches. ACNC registration can be revoked for lawful reasons, but that is a separate process from dissolution. Financial reports and reviews under Division 60 depend on **ACNC revenue size**, not an association's local tier. [ACNC change notification](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/notify-us-changes); §06.08.

### [06.04.06] A proportionate compliance map

| Control | Small volunteer WA association, not charity | Medium ACNC charity with employees | Large CLG charity | ACNC CATSI charity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Six ACNC Standards | Not legally applicable unless charity registered; useful voluntary benchmark. | Applicable, subject to specific exceptions. | Applicable; greater scale implies stronger reasonable-steps evidence. | Applicable with ACNC/CATSI interaction and relevant exceptions. |
| Board suitability | State eligibility and good practice. | Document checks for every Responsible Person. | Central onboarding, annual checks and independent governance assurance. | ORIC eligibility plus ACNC checks, culturally appropriate process. |
| Overseas conduct | Contract/other law if applicable; ACNC standards only if registered charity. | Apply standards if relevant, including agents. | Formalised overseas assurance and safeguarding. | Apply if overseas activity, with any community and ORIC protections. |
| Finance oversight | Reconciled bank and budget each meeting. | Restricted funds, contract obligations and cash forecast monthly. | Audited annual statements, audit/risk committee and internal controls assurance. | Separate ORIC reporting, community-relevant funds analysis, ACNC oversight. |

## [06.05] Responsible People, duties, conflicts and stewardship

### [06.05.01] Identify the actual duty holders

ACNC **Responsible People** generally include the directors of a CLG, committee members of an incorporated association, trustees or directors of a corporate trustee, and CATSI directors; the title 'volunteer' is not an exclusion. The Corporations Act definition of a director can include properly established de facto or shadow situations, but those categories should not simply be transferred to a different statute without checking its definitions. Some staff may be officers under other laws. [ACNC, Responsible People](https://www.acnc.gov.au/tools/factsheets/responsible-people-board-or-committee-members); AICD[GOV, pp. 21–34]; AICD[LEG, pp. 18–25].

### [06.05.02] Duty, beneficiary and purpose

The governing body must exercise powers in the entity's interests and for its legally authorised purposes. Donors, employees, clients, communities and creditors are important, but have different rights and remedies. The absence of shareholders does **not** justify lower financial competence; it increases the importance of transparent accountability to people who often cannot exit or vote. The board may balance present and future beneficiaries, but must not convert managerial convenience or a director's preferences into 'the mission'. In distress, creditor interests and statutory insolvency safeguards become more prominent (§06.15).

### [06.05.03] Conflicts: interests, duties and appearances

An actual conflict arises when the director's personal interest or separate duty can materially diverge from the charity's interest. A potential conflict might mature into an actual one; a perceived conflict can damage confidence even if no misconduct occurs. Common charity situations: a founder's company bids to supply services; a councillor sits on both grantor and grantee boards; a relative applies for an executive job; a director receives travel or gifts from a sponsor; a member of a grant committee is also a beneficiary. A **dual duty conflict** can exist without a financial payment.

**Original seven-step control:** (1) ask early and disclose in writing; (2) describe both factual and perceived dimensions; (3) enter in standing register and specific meeting minutes; (4) identify the applicable statute and constitution voting/attendance rule; (5) decide recusal or appropriate management without conflicted participation; (6) independently compare value, options and mission impact; (7) record decision, abstention, any permitted participation, notifications and post-contract monitoring. Some laws permit formal member approval or exceptions, but **disclosure alone is not a universal cure**. See WA Act s 42, and local statutes matrix; Corporations Act ss 191–195 only where applicable.

### [06.05.04] Related-party transactions and private benefit

Not every related-party transaction is prohibited. The board must establish **authority, fair value, actual necessity, independent judgement, transparent reporting and controls against inurement**. For each transaction collect relationship declarations, a comparison/competitive tender or justified exception, scope and acceptance criteria, independent approval and relevant financial-disclosure classification. Charity accounting disclosure and the ACNC Annual Information Statement's related-party questions may apply even when a payment is below a director's preferred internal materiality. A transaction can be legal yet reputationally indefensible; a free 'service' from a connected party can also distort independence.

| Situation | What to do | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Director owns venue proposed for charity event | Declare interest, exclude according to law, seek independent quotes, minute price and suitability. | Price checked only with director; conflict omitted from minutes. |
| Honorarium or director's travel | Verify constitution, statute, charity purposes, policy, reasonable quantum and beneficiary/business purpose. | Retroactive self-approval or spouse costs. |
| Executive appoints relative | Independent recruitment, qualifications and oversight; consider approval escalation. | Role created for person; salary above comparators. |
| Sponsor requests preferential service | Review contract against eligibility, mission, charitable purpose and client fairness. | Sponsor benefit outweighs service purpose. |
| Member volunteers sell materials to charity | Verify invoicing, source, receipt and purchase delegation; log interests. | Cash without evidence; repeated exclusive awards. |

**Key record:** a standing interest register is not a substitute for **meeting-specific disclosures** and documented director exclusion where required. [AICD] GOV pp. 103–104; LEG pp. 25–38; TOOL pp. 60–65.

### [06.05.05] Reliance, delegation and individual enquiry

A board may delegate operations, but needs to set boundaries, receive reliable information and respond to exceptions. A treasurer is not the only person responsible for finances; an accountant's signature does not resolve board judgement. Directors should seek advice proportionate to risk and should record unresolved issues before approving action. A useful five-question routine: *What do we know? How do we know? What are plausible alternatives? What could go wrong? What change would make us revisit the decision?* [AICD] LEG pp. 38–54; FIN pp. 11–22; TOOL pp. 42–53.

### [06.05.06] Accountability as stewardship

Stewardship goes beyond preventing fraud. It covers the ethical use of scarce philanthropic resources, donor intentions, intergenerational service capability, accessible complaints, fair employment and safeguarding. Directors should track both **money at risk** and **people at risk**. A small association can implement strong controls through two-person bank authorisation, clear budget limits, independent monthly reconciliation and a concise escalation register. A large charity needs independent lines of assurance, quality audit and documented follow-up; having more policies is not itself evidence of control effectiveness.

## [06.06] Company limited by guarantee: members, meetings, ASIC and ACNC

### [06.06.01] Legal architecture of a CLG

A **company limited by guarantee** is a public company under the *Corporations Act 2001* (Cth) rather than a company owned through tradeable shares. Members agree, through the constitution, to contribute a specified limited amount if the company is wound up in defined circumstances; membership itself is not ownership of accumulated charity reserves. It generally needs at least **three directors** (at least **two ordinarily resident in Australia**) and at least **one company secretary** (at least **one ordinarily resident in Australia**), and it must identify people holding office, maintain registers and meet its constitutional obligations. A CLG need **not** be a charity, and its charity and ATO status must be separately checked. [LAW] *Corporations Act 2001* (Cth), ss 112, 117, 140, 201A, 204A; [ASIC, NFPs and charities](https://www.asic.gov.au/for-business-and-companies/nfps-and-charities/).

The guarantee amount should be specified, understood by members and reflected in the constitution. It is not normally a mechanism for financing ordinary operating deficits. Members have the rights their legal constitution and remaining applicable legislation give them: electing or removing directors as applicable, voting on specified fundamental matters, receiving eligible notices and asking questions. They cannot simply demand back 'their share' of retained charity funds.

### [06.06.02] ACNC-registered CLG: which regulator handles which obligation?

[LAW] The Corporations Act contains **special modifications for ACNC-registered charities**, including the charity-specific modifications in **Part 1.6, ss 111K–111Q**. ASIC's current explanatory table differentiates obligations that still apply to registered charity companies from those effectively administered through ACNC law. **Do not assume a charity CLG has exactly the same statutory AGM, director duties, ASIC annual financial-report lodgement or annual review fee requirements as a non-charity CLG.** Conversely, ACNC registration does **not** remove the underlying company, ASIC's corporate register, lawful name use or all criminal/company obligations. [ASIC, charity interaction](https://www.asic.gov.au/for-business-and-companies/nfps-and-charities/registered-charities-obligations-to-the-acnc-and-asic/).

| Matter | Ordinary CLG not ACNC-registered | CLG registered with ACNC |
|---|---|---|
| Incorporation and company number | ASIC; public company under Corporations Act. | ASIC incorporation and company status continue. |
| Charitable objects | May be NFP but not necessarily a legal charity. | Must retain qualifying charitable purposes and satisfy ACNC standards, subject to exceptions. |
| Directors' duties | Corporations Act civil and criminal duties, common law and other applicable law. | Charity governance standards replace **most** Corporations Act civil duty provisions; criminal provisions/common-law and other duties still require review. |
| Annual reporting | Corporations Act financial-reporting and CLG-specific tests apply, varying with size and DGR criteria. | ACNC AIS and financial report according to ACNC revenue band; special arrangements may affect ASIC reports. |
| Meetings/member rights | Corporations Act public-company rules and constitution, including AGM framework. | ACNC-specific modification means **the constitution, ACNC Standard 2 and retained Corporations Act rules** must be considered separately; do not mechanically impose ordinary company AGM requirements. |
| Company changes | ASIC filings for register changes as required. | Some changes reported to ACNC, some to ASIC, some to both; confirm exact change and company status. |
| Tax and DGR | ATO concession tests are separate. | Charity registration opens eligibility pathways, **not** blanket ATO/DGR endorsement. |

**Director check:** ask the secretary to produce a *two-column ASIC/ACNC compliance map* listing the specific surviving obligations and their statutory basis. A generic claim 'ASIC does not regulate charities' is materially wrong. [REGULATOR] ASIC page above; [ACNC, charities and companies](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/other-regulators).

### [06.06.03] Meetings and member legitimacy

A CLG should maintain a valid member register, clear admission/removal process, nomination rules, proxy and electronic meeting arrangements, information rights and dispute procedures as allowed by law. Where a constitution provides for an annual meeting, follow it. The board should still report to members even if a particular statutory AGM obligation is modified for ACNC registration. Governance Standard 2 asks whether there is meaningful member accountability, not merely technically sufficient annual communications. Audit and financial reports may need presentation to members under rules or grant agreements even when a particular regulator accepts an alternative filing.

**Meeting evidence:** register of voting members and classes; complete notices and papers; quorum calculation; proxy authorisations; signed minutes and poll records; conflicts; committee elections; financial report adoption or receipt; resolutions and subsequent ACNC/ASIC updates. Record **which person could vote on which matter** rather than treating attendance as consent.

### [06.06.04] Board approval, special resolutions and transactions

The constitution may require member approval for objects, winding up, director removals, class rights or fundamental restructuring. Other transactions may trigger related-party rules or statutory limits, with charity modifications altering the Corporations Act pathway. The board should ask: *Is the grant or donation agreement actually a trust? Does a fee-producing subsidiary dilute the charitable-purpose test? Do the members represent beneficiaries adequately? Is the transaction arm's length? Which authority or approval is decisive?* Obtain legal advice for major transactions, related-party materiality or proposed conversion between forms.

### [06.06.05] When CLG scale justifies a different operating model

A large CLG commonly needs an experienced company secretary, statutory reporting calendar, explicit reserved matters and CEO delegation, a board-approved three-year strategy, a finance/audit committee with independent assurance, programme quality data, safeguarding oversight and a clear succession process. This is not an instruction to replicate a listed company's governance bureaucracy: committees should exist because their work improves decision quality, not as prestige titles. For a national service charity, oversight must encompass *service fidelity and safety across local offices* and not just consolidated finances. [AICD] GOV pp. 52–79; FIN pp. 23–44; BE pp. 89–104.

### [06.07.02] What the eight laws have in common—and do not

All eight regimes provide incorporation and a mechanism for governing people and members, define how rules operate, constrain private distributions and establish some financial accountability. They vary materially on minimum membership, thresholds, auditor qualifications, record retention, required AGM, public officer requirements, extent of codified individual duties, annual filing and charity harmonisation. In particular:

- An association with **$400,000 revenue** can be small under WA/VIC thresholds, Tier 2 under NSW if other asset criterion satisfied, **large or medium in QLD depending on assets**, and medium under ACT review rules if *not* an ACNC charity. NT category depends on gross assets and trading status. SA category depends on *defined gross receipts*, not the ACNC measure.
- A non-charity ACT association may need review even when small; a NT Tier 1 must still have an eligible independent audit; neither should be compared to a WA Tier 1 with no ordinary independent assurance.
- State charity harmonisation can waive a particular **filing**, a particular **state financial reporting provision**, or a fee, in different combinations. It rarely erases membership disputes, employment law, fundraising or safeguarding requirements.
- Where a local law was changed during a financial year, the **effective date and reporting period** matter. A statute's latest online consolidation should be checked alongside transitional instruments.

### [06.07.03] WA detailed case: formation, rules and member control

The *Associations Incorporation Act 2015* (WA) is the principal regime for a typical Perth theatre, sporting, arts or community service association. Its legal identity is separate from committee members, but those members still owe statutory obligations. The association must be suitable for incorporation, have at least **six voting members** and maintain relevant rules; Schedule 1 specifies matters rules must address. The latest amendments changed aspects of management, electronic participation and allowable property distribution recipients, but some amendments are not commenced automatically merely because the amendment Act passed. [WA current Act](https://www.legislation.wa.gov.au/legislation/statutes.nsf/main_mrtitle_13715_homepage.html); [WA 2025 changes](https://www.consumerprotection.wa.gov.au/publications/changes-associations-incorporation-act).

A properly governed small association should check that the rules address: objects and eligibility; classes of members and voting; grounds and fair procedure for discipline; committee appointment and removal; chair/treasurer/secretary powers; quorums and member meeting notices; application of income; material conflict procedure; financial records; grievance/mediation; alterations; and residual assets. **Member participation is a governance asset:** founders should not quietly bypass an election because 'we have always done it informally'.

### [06.07.04] WA duties, conflicts and financial obligations

The WA Act codifies an interest disclosure regime at **s 42**, care and diligence at **s 44**, good faith/proper purpose at **s 45**, and prohibitions on improper use of position and information at **ss 46–47**. These apply according to WA definitions and exceptions; they are not simply adopted by quoting Corporations Act s 180. When the association is an ACNC charity, the **charity's** reasonable-steps obligations sit alongside them. The board should maintain annual declarations and explicit meeting-time disclosure/recusal, including when choosing committee-connected suppliers.

Financial records must be sufficiently accurate to explain transactions and support true and fair statements; retention is **seven years** under the WA Act. Report content and assurance depend on tiers in Matrix B: a Tier 2 needs reviewed accounts, a Tier 3 audited accounts, subject to any lawful direction or permitted alternative. A committee declaration and going-concern enquiry may be required. **A 'treasurer's verbal report' is not equivalent to statutory accounts.** The AGM generally must occur within **six months after financial year-end**. [WA finance guide](https://www.consumerprotection.wa.gov.au/inc-guide-incorporated-associations-western-australia/tiered-financial-reporting); AICD[TOOL, pp. 53–59].

### [06.07.05] WA: a sample lawful decision chain

**Illustrative scenario:** a WA arts association wants to accept a $180,000 local-government grant, use $60,000 of reserves as matching funds and expand into after-school youth programs. The board should establish in sequence: (i) objects and member approval implications; (ii) grant restrictions and unfunded costs; (iii) staff/volunteer child-safety capacity; (iv) ability to meet payments before reimbursements; (v) delegation and bank approvals; (vi) director conflicts, including related service providers; (vii) revised revenue tier for the year; (viii) whether the use of charity registration, if any, is accurate; and (ix) reporting obligations under the grant and association Act. The existence of cash on the bank statement does not settle affordability.

**Model minute extract (original):** 'The committee reviewed the three-year operating forecast and grant schedule, noted the declared interest of Director A in Supplier B and their exclusion from consideration and vote, requested legal clarification of restrictions on the $60,000 reserve, and deferred final acceptance until the treasurer supplied a 13-week cash forecast, independent quotes and the safeguarding implementation plan. The secretary will report on approval conditions and any changed tier obligations before execution.' The minute demonstrates informed judgement; it does not claim the grant was approved.

### [06.07.06] WA ACNC and fundraising overlays

**Incorporation is not a fundraising licence.** WA charitable collections are regulated under the *Charitable Collections Act 1946* and associated conditions, subject to important **2026 deemed-licence reform**: an ACNC-registered organisation may obtain deemed licensed status **by notifying WA Consumer Protection of its intention to collect in WA**, and must follow the code and relevant conditions. Non-ACNC organisations may still need a conventional licence. Do not advertise the charity as automatically licensed solely because the ACNC register lists it; document the notification and applicable fundraising scope. [WA Consumer Protection, charitable collections](https://www.consumerprotection.wa.gov.au/charitable-collections-western-australia); [licence conditions](https://www.consumerprotection.wa.gov.au/charity-licensing-conditions).

For an ACNC-registered WA association, annual state information-statement reporting can be harmonised subject to specific conditions. **Request evidence of the actual ACNC filing and state exemption eligibility**, not a generic assertion from the treasurer. WA must also be listed as the association's home jurisdiction in all regulatory and legal records. The charitable fundraising role may involve public appeals, raffles, face-to-face collections and online campaigns; each activity's licensing, permit and donor-consent rules may differ.

### [06.07.07] Local law verification protocol for board secretaries

Before annual accounts or a constitutional amendment, keep a **one-page per-jurisdiction evidence card**: association registration and legal number; Act and latest consolidation date; relevant regulation; public officer/secretary and contact; rules version; financial-year dates; category revenue and assets formula; auditor qualification; AGM statutory deadline; financial statement content/presentation; actual electronic regulator filing deadline; charity exception conditions; fundraising licence/deeming/notification; relevant changes awaiting commencement; name and date of legal/regulatory verification. This is much safer than copying an older statewide checklist.

**Jurisdiction verdict at 27 October 2026:** all eight official regulator regimes and core Acts identified and checked at principle level. **Full transactional certification is not claimed** for commencement of Tasmania's 2026 reforms, individual charity harmonisation conditions or any bespoke ministerial exemption. The WA financial-tier boundary is resolved by its Act s 64 and current Regulations reg 11A notwithstanding imprecise regulator summaries. [AICD] LAPP p. 5 is historical March 2024 comparison, not evidence that these 2026 details were taught.

## [06.08] ACNC reporting, annual statements, audits, groups and ORIC overlap

### [06.08.01] The ACNC size test is annual revenue

[LAW] Under the ACNC Act/Regulations and ACNC guidance, the primary charity size classification for reporting is based on **annual revenue**, rather than assets, cash balance, receipts, members or revenue of a particular grant alone. As at October 2026:

| ACNC category | Annual revenue | General annual ACNC reporting and assurance baseline |
|---|---:|---|
| **Small** | **Less than $500,000** | Annual Information Statement (AIS) generally; no mandatory ACNC annual financial report, but keep adequate records and check other laws/constitution/funders. |
| **Medium** | **$500,000 to less than $3 million** | AIS **and financial report**; report must generally be **reviewed or audited** by an eligible practitioner. |
| **Large** | **$3 million or more** | AIS **and financial report**; financial report generally **audited** by an eligible auditor. |

Do **not** use a WA Tier 2 or SA prescribed-association test to determine ACNC size. Be attentive to aggregation and reporting groups, unusual revenue recognition, subsidiaries and restrictions; a board should obtain the accountant's reasoning where the entity is near a threshold. [ACNC reporting](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/annual-information-statement); [ACNC size and financial reports](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/obligations-acnc/reporting-annually-acnc).

### [06.08.02] AIS period and actual 2026 due dates

The general rule is **six months after the charity's reporting period**. However, **for charities with the standard financial year ending 30 June 2026, the 2026 AIS deadline was extended to 31 January 2027** (rather than the ordinary 31 December 2026). For calendar-year charities ending **31 December 2026**, the ACNC published **30 June 2027** as due date. Document the charity's actual financial period and ACNC portal deadline. The 2026 AIS was made available on 9 September 2026; check its current questions, including related-party and overseas activities. [ACNC current due dates](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/annual-information-statement/annual-information-statement-due-dates); [ACNC 2026 announcement](https://www.acnc.gov.au/media/news/2026-annual-information-statement-now-available).

### [06.08.03] What the ACNC financial report is—and is not

A statutory annual financial report is a prescribed set of financial statements, notes and relevant declaration prepared under applicable Australian Accounting Standards. Depending on the entity it includes financial performance, position, cash flows and changes in equity; appropriate comparative information, related-party disclosure and accounting policies matter. It is not the same as an internal budget, annual fundraising slide, bank statement, impact brochure or grant acquittal. Standards governing revenue from grants and fundraising, and whether special-purpose reporting is permissible, require current Australian Accounting Standards Board analysis. **An externally audited report does not automatically validate program outcomes, safeguarding, grant compliance or the appropriateness of management's strategy.** [AICD] FIN pp. 23–44, 50–79; FAPP pp. 7–14; TOOL pp. 47–59.

### [06.08.04] Review versus audit

A review is a **limited assurance** engagement normally based on enquiry and analytical procedures; an audit is **reasonable assurance** and ordinarily involves more evidence and testing, but is not a guarantee or a fraud-proof certificate. The auditor/reviewer must meet statutory eligibility and independence requirements. A board should independently decide whether to audit even when a review is legally sufficient if there are safeguarding exposures, complex grants, unreliable accounting, large restricted balances, a turnover in finance staff, material related-party transactions or an emerging going-concern risk. Conversely, a small low-risk association should not spend scarce service funds on an elaborate assurance process where simpler controls and an independent check are proportionate.

**Audit commissioning questions:** Do the terms cover consolidated entities? Are there disputed donor restrictions? Is grant revenue recognition assessed? Does management understand findings? Can the auditor meet the board without management? Is the management letter actioned and tracked? Are audit fees commercially and ethically independent? Has the finance team documented the going-concern forecast? See `05_Financial_Reporting_Audit_and_Assurance.md` and AICD[FIN, pp. 38–44; TOOL, pp. 17–23, 47–53].

### [06.08.05] Exemptions and reporting group traps

Some charities qualify for exceptional treatment: **Basic Religious Charities**, **ACNC-approved group reporting**, particular operating structures and **CATSI corporations reporting to ORIC**. Do not combine all exemptions into the statement 'all charities submit the AIS'. Similarly, a state's harmonisation can be unavailable when a charity elects to withhold its financial report or joins an ACNC reporting group. NSW's current 1 April 2026 exemption is particularly explicit (§06.07). Charity law may allow concessions, but a government funding contract can still require audited accounts. The operative exemption should be evidenced by a regulator page or written determination **for the period concerned**.

### [06.08.06] ORIC and ACNC do not use the same size tests

A CATSI corporation is administered by the **Office of the Registrar of Indigenous Corporations (ORIC)** under the CATSI Act. ORIC's small/medium/large categorisation considers **consolidated gross operating income, assets and employee count** (a two-of-three framework), while certain reporting triggers refer specifically to **gross operating income**. These are **not** the same as ACNC's pure revenue size bands. Registered CATSI charities generally report under ORIC rather than submit an ordinary ACNC AIS, under applicable statutory arrangements; that does not necessarily remove ACNC registration and governance obligations. [ORIC, reporting](https://www.oric.gov.au/for-corporations/meeting-reporting-and-changing/reporting); [ACNC, CATSI reporting arrangements](https://www.acnc.gov.au/for-charities/manage-your-charity/obligations-acnc/reporting-annually-acnc).

For reference, the commonly published ORIC size scale has **small** below $100,000 income/assets and fewer than five employees on its relevant tests; **medium** around $100,000–<$5m operating income, $100,000–<$2.5m assets or five to 24 employees; **large** at or above $5m, $2.5m or 25 employees on the applicable two-of-three classification. The detailed ORIC report trigger and eligibility must be verified against the current ORIC decision guide and Act rather than relying on these short labels. See §06.16 and `07_Aboriginal_Corporations_and_Indigenous_Governance.md`.

### [06.08.07] Reporting calendar and board sign-off packet

A practical ACNC board cycle is: **2–3 months before period-end**, review grant cut-offs, accounting choices and auditor appointment; **at year-end**, confirm bank, balances, restricted funds and related parties; **after year-end**, reconcile draft financials to internal reports and impact claims; **before approval**, review risks, estimates, subsequent events and cash forecasts; **after approval**, lodge AIS, financial statements and required state/ORIC/ATO material, record acceptance receipts and update the website and member communications.

**Minimum sign-off packet:** signed financial report and assurance opinion where required; reconciliation from management to statutory numbers; related-party listing; restricted-fund schedule; 12-month rolling cash and grant repayment stress; management letter and responses; list of major after-balance events; legal and safeguarding incident register; AIS responses, approval resolution and evidence of submission. Additional corporate director declarations apply only when the relevant statute imposes them; avoid mechanically transplanting a listed company's declaration to every association.

## [06.09] Tax status, concessions, GST, FBT and DGR: separate approvals

### [06.09.01] Four different questions

**First:** Is the entity an NFP in purpose and practice? **Second:** is it registered with the ACNC as a charity, and in what subtype? **Third:** has the ATO endorsed the relevant income tax and other charity concessions? **Fourth:** is it a **deductible gift recipient (DGR)** for all of its operations, a particular fund, or a particular purpose? These four decisions are distinct. **Not all charities are DGRs.** Charity registration with ACNC is generally a prerequisite for charity tax concessions, but the ATO determines applicable endorsements. Tax-exempt and DGR status must not be inferred from a constitution or an ACNC listing alone. [ATO, charity concession endorsement](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/not-for-profit-organisations/getting-started/endorsement-to-access-tax-concessions); [ACNC, charity tax concessions](https://www.acnc.gov.au/tools/factsheets/charity-tax-concessions).

### [06.09.02] Income tax exemption and the NFP self-review regime

Charities seeking income tax exemption ordinarily require **ACNC registration and ATO endorsement**, consistent with Division 50 of the *Income Tax Assessment Act 1997* (Cth). A non-charitable NFP may qualify under a specified exempt-entity category and self-assess, subject to the relevant statutory tests. Many non-charitable NFPs with an active Australian Business Number (ABN) must lodge an **annual ATO NFP self-review return**, introduced from the 2023–24 income year, to confirm their income-tax exemption status. The standard annual window is generally **1 July to 31 October**, with applicable ATO extensions or categories checked for the relevant year. A genuine charity should not assume it can choose the self-assessing route in preference to ACNC registration. [ATO, NFP self-review return](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/not-for-profit-organisations/statements-and-returns/reporting-requirements-to-self-assess-income-tax-exemption); [ATO, NFP tax status](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/not-for-profit-organisations).

A board should keep ATO endorsement letters, eligibility summaries, ABN status, tax self-review receipts and dates of changes to the objects. Where activities evolve significantly or commercial income becomes substantial, obtain professional review of continuing eligibility and any 'in Australia' conditions. **Tax exemption concerns income tax**, not every other tax, withholding, payroll, land tax, fringe benefits tax or GST.

### [06.09.03] GST status and the difference between grants, gifts and sales

The Goods and Services Tax (GST) registration threshold for a qualifying NFP organisation is generally **$150,000 GST turnover**. This is a GST-defined calculation, not necessarily the charity's total accounting revenue or sum of gross cash receipts. A genuine gift without a material benefit supplied in return is generally not consideration for a taxable supply; a sponsorship contract providing advertising, naming or other benefits can be different. A government grant may fund a service or give rise to a taxable supply depending on the agreement. A charity's specific GST concessions may affect supplies below market value, donated goods, fundraising and non-commercial activities. [ATO, NFP GST registration](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/not-for-profit-organisations/your-organisation/tax-and-super-obligations/goods-and-services-tax).

**Example:** a community charity receives $110,000 unconditional donations and $100,000 taxable service fees. It cannot conclude from $210,000 total income alone whether the **$150,000 GST-turnover test** is met. Classification of gifts versus supplies, other taxable supplies, projected turnover and registration conditions determine the result. Even below the threshold, voluntary GST registration can impose ongoing business activity statement obligations. The decision deserves a short accountant note, not assumption.

### [06.09.04] Fringe benefits tax and employees/volunteers

Fringe benefits tax (FBT) applies to defined benefits provided in respect of employment unless an exemption or concession applies. Some categories of ACNC charity, especially eligible **public benevolent institutions (PBIs)** and **health promotion charities**, can qualify for FBT concessions with specific per-employee caps; other eligible NFPs may receive rebates rather than outright exemptions. An ordinary culture or arts charity should not assert it has the same concessions as an endorsed PBI. The cap is generally expressed on a **grossed-up taxable value** basis, so cash-equivalent spending limits cannot be assumed without computation. Relevant private-use vehicles, salary packaging, entertainment and employee arrangements need payroll review. Volunteers are not automatically employees; employment/worker and reimbursement characterization determines tax and legal treatment. [ATO, FBT for NFPs](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/not-for-profit-organisations/your-organisation/tax-and-super-obligations/fringe-benefits-tax).

### [06.09.05] DGR status: scope and donor promises

DGR eligibility derives from the *Income Tax Assessment Act 1997* (Cth), especially Division 30, and ATO endorsement or explicit listing by law. Endorsement may apply to a whole entity **or only to a specific fund, authority or institution it operates**. Governing documents, gift-fund accounts and transfer rules can therefore be decisive. An ACNC-registered charity that is not a DGR must not promise tax deductibility. A board should approve donor-facing language only after checking ABN Lookup/ATO determination and the **date and scope of endorsement**. [ATO, DGR categories](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/not-for-profit-organisations/getting-started/deductible-gift-recipients); [ACNC, tax concessions](https://www.acnc.gov.au/tools/factsheets/charity-tax-concessions).

A DGR receipt and a donor acknowledgement are different concepts. Gifts, fundraising event purchases, sponsorship, auction bids and membership payments can be treated differently even if donors perceive them as 'support'. Record restricted conditions accurately, particularly when an endowment is involved. Ensure dissolution clauses protect the transfer of a DGR gift fund to an eligible recipient as required by the relevant category and governing instrument.

### [06.09.06] A board tax-status certificate

Each year, management should present a one-page certificate: legal entity and ABN; ACNC registration/subtype; charity income-tax exemption endorsement effective date; DGR status/scope; GST registration and turnover estimate; FBT endorsement/category and annual exposure; payroll and superannuation compliance; state payroll/land tax concessions if any; dates due for returns; material changed objects and activities; outstanding ATO/ACNC correspondence. The board's job is not to compute every tax figure but to ensure someone competent owns the position and flags uncertainty. **If a high-value fundraising campaign hinges on DGR deductibility, independent verification is proportionate before launch.**

## [06.10] Mission strategy, theory of change and social outcomes

### [06.10.01] Strategy is the choice of outcomes and delivery model

A charity's strategy is not a long list of programs or an annual fundraising target. It is an integrated set of decisions about **which social need to address, for whom, through which intervention, with what resources, under which constraints and with what evidence of success**. The board must accept that a charity can be busy, popular and well-funded while producing little durable benefit. Equally, a service with modest financial surplus may deliver extraordinary public value. A robust board links charitable objects to service design, funding model, risk tolerance, measurement and the ability to change course. [AICD] RAPP pp. 23–26; GOV pp. 80–88; RISK course pp. 19–86; AICD 2024 NFP Principle 1.

### [06.10.02] A theory of change: a falsifiable cause-and-effect chain

**Original model:** social problem and baseline → affected population and barriers → resources and intervention → immediate outputs → intermediate outcomes → long-term impact → evidence and assumptions. Example for a mentoring charity: paid mentor training, school referrals and contact hours are **inputs/outputs**, while sustained school attendance, improved completion and safer wellbeing are **outcomes**. Change over time may reflect other interventions, selection effects or shifting economic conditions; it cannot automatically be attributed to the charity's work. A theory of change should identify what must be true for activities to create value and what evidence would disprove the assumption.

Boards should demand a short **causal logic and counterfactual challenge**: what happens to the same cohort without intervention, with a cheaper intervention, or when the charity withdraws? In small charities, this need not be a costly randomised trial. Consistent before/after evidence, external administrative statistics, client interviews and peer comparison can be enough to reveal whether the model is plausible.

### [06.10.03] Stakeholder and beneficiary voice as evidence

Stakeholder consultation is not limited to funders or member votes. Map beneficiaries, families, staff, volunteers, partner agencies, public authorities, affected communities, First Nations rights-holders, competitors and donors. Consider **power asymmetry**: a beneficiary dependent on the charity may fear retaliation for criticism; a powerful donor can access the chair directly. Useful mechanisms include anonymised feedback, independent complaints channels, co-design, representative advisory groups and transparent reporting of changes made because of feedback. Avoid token representation: one person cannot be assumed to speak for an entire community.

**Board question:** 'What are beneficiaries telling us that does not appear in the fundraising narrative?' Request direct, appropriately consented accounts and anonymised complaints analysis. For an RNTBC, go further: distinguish consultation and native title holder decision rights under law (§06.16).

### [06.10.04] Economic analysis of social value

Cost per contact or participant is incomplete because service intensity and results differ. Consider **cost per sustained outcome**, cost of failed interventions, time to beneficiary benefit, programme durability and opportunity cost. Where responsible, monetise avoided public costs or improved outcomes, but identify attribution assumptions, double counting, discount rates and distributional effects. Do not present an internally constructed 'social return on investment' multiple as audited profit or a market value. A $1 grant dollar might lever co-funding, but matching revenue is not social value unless it leads to additional outcomes.

**Illustrative decision:** Program A costs $400,000 and serves 800 people ($500 per client); Program B costs $350,000 and serves 350 ($1,000 per client). If B produces twice the durable improvement rate of A for a harder-to-serve population, the lower unit *contact* cost of A may be irrelevant. The board needs outcome-adjusted comparability rather than a reflex preference for low cost.

### [06.10.05] Mission drift through funding dependency

The first warning of mission drift is often not an explicit constitutional change; it is a rising share of restricted income from projects the organisation would never have chosen without a grant. Other warning signs: fee-paying programs crowding out disadvantaged beneficiaries, donations tied to visibility rather than need, a sponsor vetoing independent statements, government output measures dominating program design, and property acquisitions diverting leaders from outcomes. An independent strategic review should ask which programs are **mission-core**, **financially sustainable**, **underfunded but high impact**, **profitable but marginal to mission**, or **irrelevant**. Some may need redesign, cross-subsidy or exit, but no action should be framed as purely financial.

### [06.10.06] Strategy approval gate and learning cycle

Before approving a major program, require: stated charitable purpose; diagnosed unmet need; evidence for intervention; beneficiary and community consultation; alternatives and full cost including overheads; funding and contract restrictions; forecast unrestricted cash effect; staff/volunteer and safeguarding capability; key service outcomes; red lines for reputational/safety exposure; exit or transfer plan; review date. Monitor leading indicators (recruitment, timeliness, service quality) alongside lagging outcomes (sustained change). Record *what the board expects to learn*. This distinguishes rational experimentation from unmeasured expansion. [AICD] RAPP pp. 23–26; TOOL pp. 102–104; GOV pp. 35–51; BE pp. 72–88.

## [06.11] Financial stewardship, reserves, grants, fundraising and controls

### [06.11.01] A charity's financial objective is sustainable mission delivery

Financial stewardship requires **sufficient unrestricted cash, reliable funding, controllable commitments and assets available for their legally permitted uses**. Revenue growth or accounting surplus is not synonymous with resilience. A charity can show a surplus while carrying a growing grant liability, delayed government reimbursement or rapidly expanding restricted reserves. Conversely, an anticipated operating deficit can be prudent if funded from explicitly available unrestricted reserves with a credible recovery and impact case.

At each board meeting, ask three questions: **What cash can we really use? How much of it will we need before the next reliable receipt? What consequence follows if a major contract, fundraiser or grant fails?** [AICD] FIN pp. 11–22, 50–100; FAPP pp. 7–14; TOOL pp. 53–59, 124.

### [06.11.02] A practical fund-availability reconciliation

The board dashboard should distinguish *bank and investment balance*, short-term liquidity, **legally restricted cash** (donor terms, trust assets, unspent grants or funds not freely expendable), **board-designated reserves**, operating commitments and available unrestricted liquidity. These categories are not always cleanly captured by accounting equity classifications; management must reconcile legal restrictions to contractual source documents. Funds legally restricted for a future program cannot be used for payroll deficits merely because they share a bank account. A transfer between two bank accounts does not release a donor restriction.

**Worked illustration (all figures hypothetical, A$000):** closing cash and accessible deposits $620; contractually restricted project balances $210; donor-restricted capital fund $90; near-term tax/payroll commitments $75; unrestricted operating cash **after these earmarked amounts** $245, *assuming no double counting and no other obligations*. The board had not 'saved $620 for a rainy day'; its relevant liquidity reserve is closer to $245 before operating forecast, and the figure must be updated for overlapping restricted/project liabilities. Board designation of an additional $100 reserve narrows discretionary spending but is **not** the same legal restriction as the $300 restricted funds.

### [06.11.03] Restricted, designated and unrestricted reserves

A **restricted fund** is constrained by a legally enforceable donation, grant, trust or regulatory term; a **designated reserve** is internally allocated by the board for a purpose such as redundancy, IT replacement or building maintenance; **unrestricted reserves** provide general risk-bearing capacity, subject to ordinary obligations and charitable objects. Donors can make unconditional contributions even when a fundraiser mentions a broad activity; equally, wording of appeals or trust terms can impose enforceable restrictions. Record both the legal character and the management designation, rather than classifying all donations as restricted. Accounting revenue recognition may not follow the donor's cash payment date; consult relevant AASB standards and contracts.

A reserve policy should state the purpose, approved target range, calculation method, investment liquidity, authority to access reserves and rebuilding trigger. 'Three months of expenditure' is a possible starting heuristic, **not an Australian legal minimum or universal optimum**. Consider cash flow volatility, government contract payment lags, fixed staff costs, property repair exposure, grant clawback and speed of winding down. A small club with no employees may sensibly hold much less operating cash than a community housing charity with payroll and contractual obligations.

### [06.11.04] Cash-flow forecasting, solvency and working capital

At minimum, boards should receive an annual budget and 12-month rolling cash view; where grants or payroll are tight, an integrated **13-week cash forecast** is an effective early-warning tool. Include GST/PAYG, superannuation, wage awards, annual leave and redundancy, insurance renewals, supplier arrears, debt maturities, capital commitments, donor fund restrictions and the possibility of grants being received late or partly rejected. Distinguish unrestricted cash from cash that is conditional or held for others. **Cash-flow insolvency** concerns the ability to pay debts when due, not whether the year's accounting result is a loss.

| Core calculation | Definition | Why a charity board needs it | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unrestricted liquidity runway | Available unrestricted cash ÷ forecast average monthly net cash outflow. | Time to intervention before cash exhaustion. | Monthly averages conceal grant and payroll peaks; use 13-week cash too. |
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities. | Snapshot of short-term balance-sheet coverage. | Current assets may include restricted cash or illiquid receivables; not itself a solvency test. |
| Operating cash conversion | Cash from operations ÷ operating surplus (where meaningful). | Tracks whether reported activity creates cash. | Negative/near-zero surplus makes ratio meaningless; grants shift timing. |
| Grant concentration | Largest grantor receipts ÷ total recurring cash receipts. | Indicates counterparty/policy dependency. | Awarded multi-year contracts may be more or less secure than annual gifts. |
| Unrestricted operating margin | Unrestricted recurring income less recurring expenditure, divided by unrestricted recurring income. | Tests core service funding structure. | Must correctly allocate shared overheads and designated drawdowns. |
| Fundraising net contribution | Incremental fundraising cash inflows minus **full incremental costs**. | Tests whether appeals create cash for mission. | Do not confuse this with measured social value or short-term donor acquisition costs. |
| Grant delivery variance | Actual eligible expenditure minus approved milestone budget, with forecast to completion. | Identifies overrun or acquittal/clawback risk. | Underspend can be failure to deliver rather than efficiency. |

[AICD] TOOL pp. 58–59 sets out NFP ratios and illustrative benchmarks, some very conservative; **these benchmarks are pedagogical suggestions, not statutory minimums or valid comparisons for every sector.** See `04_Financial_Literacy_Performance_and_Solvency.md`.

### [06.11.05] Fundraising economics and restricted grant discipline

Fundraising performance should consider **net contribution, donor retention, dependency, programme fit and ethical acquisition**, not only gross appeals. Costs include platform fees, direct marketing, postage, staff, events, consultant fees, prize costs and realistic allocation of shared overhead. One campaign's first-year loss may be rational if donor retention is evidenced, but an optimistic 'lifetime value' model without retention data is not. High gross receipts can be matched by high delivery costs. Conversely, a small number of major donors can produce low fundraising costs but significant **concentration and influence risk**.

Grants are not always donations. Many are contracts to provide specified services, with milestone evidence, eligible cost rules, procurement conditions, audit rights, publicity restrictions, intellectual property clauses, data-sharing, termination and repayment. A charity should keep a **grant restriction register** tying signed agreement to cost codes, receipts, milestone reports, unspent funds and closing obligations. Recognise income/liabilities under the appropriate accounting standards, including **AASB 15, AASB 1058 and other relevant AASB requirements** as applicable, after accountant assessment; the shorthand 'all grants equal income when cash arrives' is unsafe. [AICD] FIN pp. 50–79; FAPP pp. 7–14; TOOL pp. 47–59.

### [06.11.06] Controls that actually prevent loss

**Minimum preventive controls:** bank payments require at least two authorised people, with no person creating and approving the same payment; purchase approval according to written limits; independent month-end bank reconciliations; changes to supplier bank details verified through independently sourced contacts; payroll and volunteer reimbursement checks; restricted-project coding; version-controlled budgets; physical asset registers; and secure financial system access. **Detective controls:** exception report of new suppliers, round-number invoices, split purchase orders, late acquittals, unusual reimbursements, access-rights reviews and budget variance explanations. **Corrective controls:** incident logging, repayment and fraud response, insurer notification, regulator notification if required, delegation suspension and independent forensic review.

For a two-person volunteer committee, use a responsible external bookkeeper and a second committee member to independently inspect monthly bank statements; automation cannot replace independent control. For a large charity with multiple locations, use reconciliations plus audit sampling, procurement data analytics and site visits. Spending on controls should be proportionate to potential loss and harm, but minimum segregation is not optional simply because the office is small.

### [06.11.07] Case-based challenge: Surf Life Saving Queensland

The 2024 historical extracts in §06.17.03 illustrate why **working capital and grant receipts** matter. The balance sheet can expand sharply through funded capital investment while short-term liquidity deteriorates and loans rise. The right response is not to conclude from a single ratio that the entity was insolvent, nor to claim that an audit established future resilience. The board should compare debt-service terms, accessible reserves, capital expenditure funding, deferred grant revenue, operating cash and specific service obligations. AICD[FAPP, 2025/26 V1, pp. 7–14] teaches this financial interpretation; the calculations in §06.17 are independently derived from its 2024 numbers.

## [06.12] Contracts, fundraising, volunteers, safeguarding and beneficiary protection

### [06.12.01] Government service contracts shift risk, not just income

Government funding may be a grant, procurement contract, licence, subsidy or outcome-based payment. The board should read clauses on **eligible expenditure, performance standards, outcomes, data reporting, subcontracts, audit rights, termination for convenience, indemnities, insurance, privacy, change of control, service continuity and clawback**. A contract that looks 'fully funded' can embed unpriced workforce awards, inflation, client acuity, cybersecurity, premises or safeguarding costs. Three-year funding with one-year political or budget termination rights is not equivalent to a three-year guaranteed cash stream.

**Contract approval check:** compare legal object and service eligibility; model realistic unit costs and volume; identify restrictive funds; price non-billable time and back-office overheads; confirm working capital and payment lag; review safety case; verify whether outcomes are controllable by the charity; assess termination costs; document management owner, delegations, financial limits and exit obligations. Where multiple contracts use the same facility or workers, allocate shared costs consistently and prevent double charging.

### [06.12.02] Grant-risk dashboard and warning indicators

Flag grants where reimbursements are more than 60 days late (threshold **illustrative**, tune to agreement), milestones slip, eligible costs are disputed, worker rosters exceed funded hours, delivery relies on one individual, project cash is overdrawn, unspent amounts appear in a general-purpose account, acquittals are overdue or the grantor has announced a policy review. A monthly grant dashboard should show **approved funding, cash received, legally restricted balance, earned revenue, cumulative eligible cost, forecast final cost, key milestones, date of next claim, recoverable debt and potential clawback**. The grant manager and CFO should jointly sign major forecasts; beneficiary risk owners should separately confirm service quality.

### [06.12.03] Fundraising, sponsorship and claims

Fundraising laws and permissions remain state/territory matters; some now implement a **deemed-licence or notification** model for ACNC charities. Conditions and national fundraising principles should be verified where and when collections occur, including internet and interstate campaigns. Charity collection rules may differ from raffles, gaming, street permits or Australian Consumer Law provisions. Donor communications should state accurately how funds are used, what is tax-deductible, fees or conditions, and whether donations are restricted. An assertion that '100% goes to beneficiaries' can be misleading if salaries, platform charges or overheads exist unless it is fully and defensibly explained.

A sponsorship is often a commercial exchange, potentially with GST and procurement or reputational implications, not necessarily a gift. A contract that grants a sponsor control over eligibility, policy advocacy or research publication may be inconsistent with charitable independence. A fundraising agency paid as a percentage of gross collections warrants stronger review of complaints, donor vulnerability, cancellation/refund rights and effective net contribution.

### [06.12.04] Volunteer governance is not free labour without responsibilities

Volunteers need safe recruitment, role descriptions, supervision, training, incident pathways, reimbursement rules, reasonable working conditions and clear boundaries with employees. Some legal duties to volunteers arise under work health and safety or other legislation depending on jurisdiction and organisational coverage. Volunteers may handle cash, clients, sensitive data and children, creating real fraud and safeguarding exposure. **A volunteer's goodwill is not evidence of competence**, and a retired professional does not automatically remain current in their regulated field.

The board should understand volunteer fatigue, whether staff feel able to challenge prominent volunteer committee members, and whether key operations depend on one person knowing all passwords or bank processes. An **exit/departure checklist** should recover assets, remove system rights, document handover, collect client information and protect confidentiality.

### [06.12.05] Child safety and safeguarding escalation

Safeguarding begins with the principle that organisational reputation and fundraising must **never override protection of a child or vulnerable adult**. Apply relevant state/territory child safe standards and working with children/vulnerable people checks, mandatory reporting, reportable conduct schemes, professional registration, disability/aged-care obligations and criminal law as they apply. Legal duties vary by state and service; an ACNC Governance Standard alone does not encode every safeguarding requirement. Where programmes involve overseas vulnerable people, External Conduct Standard 4 adds an ACNC obligation.

**Immediate escalation pathway:** ensure the person's safety, arrange emergency/police response where warranted, follow mandatory reporting and regulatory time limits, preserve evidence and confidentiality, prevent conflicted/internal interference, secure independent expertise, notify insurers/funders where required and commission an appropriately independent review. The board should receive prompt **de-identified** information on seriousness, safety action, notifications, exposure, service continuity and remedial controls; it should not run operational investigations or disclose identifying details in unnecessary board minutes. A senior fundraiser or board founder is **not exempt** from independent escalation.

### [06.12.06] Employment, privacy, cybersecurity and subcontractors

Charities with paid staff remain employers under the Fair Work and relevant state systems. Critical matters include award classification, payroll compliance, superannuation, work health and safety, psychosocial hazards, sexual harassment prevention, leave, long-service entitlements, worker/contractor classification and protection of whistleblowers. Data about health, children, donors and family circumstances warrants rigorous collection limitation, access control, retention and breach response; obligations depend on Privacy Act coverage and service-specific law. A subcontractor performing outreach under the charity's brand must be assessed for safety, financial, ethical and data risks, with contractual inspection and incident escalation rights.

### [06.12.07] Board risk appetite for people and service delivery

Boards should state clearly what risks are unacceptable: known abuse, retaliation for complaints, deliberate misuse of restricted funds and dishonest reporting. This is more useful than a generic 'zero risk' statement. Some risk to staff, innovative program delivery or operating in remote communities cannot be reduced to zero; decisions require credible benefit, feasible safeguards and an escalation plan. Risk tolerance should include maximum response times for critical incidents, qualifications checks, audit findings and grant non-compliance. [AICD] LEG pp. 55–112; RAPP pp. 23–26; TOOL pp. 30, 33, 89–93, 105–106, 139.

## [06.13] Organisational culture, volunteers, executive leadership and community legitimacy

### [06.13.01] Culture is what a charity protects when it is inconvenient

A charity may publicly champion care while privately tolerating bullying, unsafe service delivery, undisclosed related-party arrangements or intimidation of finance staff. The board's behaviour is therefore especially visible: what happens when a high-status founder breaches a travel policy or a major donor demands inappropriate access? **Rules enforced against frontline workers but waived for directors destroy legitimacy.** A code of conduct matters only if misconduct is reported, assessed and remedied fairly. [AICD] GOV pp. 89–99 and 103–104; BE pp. 33–71, 89–104; 2024 NFP Governance Principle 8.

### [06.13.02] Leading a mixed workforce

Employees and volunteers are motivated differently and face different scheduling, remuneration, supervision and burnout realities. A charity can have excellent directors yet low programme continuity because frontline volunteers depart each year. The board should oversee volunteer recruitment, role compatibility, development, welfare, complaint trends and volunteer-to-staff boundaries. Recognition should not become a substitute for safe conditions or training. A **volunteer leadership succession plan** may be as important as the chief executive officer (CEO) succession plan for a community-based emergency, youth or sport organisation.

A small association may lack a CEO. The chair must then recognise when the committee is simultaneously *governing* and *operating*, and deliberately separate decision roles in each agenda: 'board decision', 'member resolution' or 'volunteer operations'. It is not realistic to prescribe formal independent directors and three committees for a six-member volunteer group, but it is reasonable to expect clear bank authorisation, conflict records, financial visibility and safeguarding.

### [06.13.03] CEO oversight and culture intelligence

The board hires, evaluates, supports and may remove the CEO under the governing document and employment law. A competent CEO should be judged on **mission outcomes, sustainable finance, people safety, capability and truthful escalation**, not gross fundraising alone. Reward and performance metrics should avoid incentives to under-report incidents or select easy-to-serve beneficiaries. CEO succession must address temporary absence and the possibility that a long-serving founder dominates organisational knowledge or relationships.

Useful culture indicators include employee and volunteer attrition, unplanned absences, safety incidents, client complaints and time to resolution, whistleblower usage, overdue internal audit actions, staff confidence in raising bad news, supervisory span and site-to-site differences. A rising number of reports can initially indicate improved trust rather than worsening conduct. Use interpretation, not crude rankings. An independent board session without management and direct site visits can test whether the executive narrative reflects reality.

### [06.13.04] Diversity, skills and community voice

Board effectiveness requires a mix of financial, legal, service, lived-experience, fundraising, digital, safeguarding, strategic and community understanding. Diversity includes more than demographics; it covers independent judgement, relationships to beneficiaries, geography and culture. At the same time, lived experience is not a substitute for technical expertise, nor should a person be treated as the sole representative of an entire group. A skills matrix must identify **missing capabilities**, recruitment opportunities and education plans. [AICD] BE pp. 42–64; TOOL pp. 14–16 and 81–88.

In a First Nations organisation, legitimacy depends on specific community authority, rule book and legally required decision processes. A generic diversity target cannot replace native title holders' rights. In an ordinary community charity, carefully designed advisory mechanisms and participatory service reviews can strengthen responsiveness without misrepresenting the authority of service users. See §06.16.

### [06.13.05] Community legitimacy and operational independence

A charity depends on an implicit social licence to operate: public confidence that donations are used as promised and beneficiaries are treated fairly. This legitimacy can erode even without a reportable technical breach. Directors should monitor the organisation's public representations, complaint handling, government contract relationships, partnerships with politically contentious donors, and communications following harmful incidents. The board should engage stakeholders but resist granting special vetoes over programmes or public statements unless authorised and consistent with mission. A transparent explanation of a difficult trade-off may create more trust than denying it exists.

## [06.14] AICD 2024 Not-for-Profit Governance Principles: eight precise principles

### [06.14.01] Authority, version and deliberate separation from ACNC Standards

[AICD — VOLUNTARY GUIDANCE] The Australian Institute of Company Directors published the **third edition of its Not-for-Profit Governance Principles in April 2024**. These eight **voluntary, non-binding principles** are designed to help NFP boards govern well; they are **not** the six legal ACNC Governance Standards. There is no automatic breach of law simply because an NFP does not adopt every suggested practice. The legal standards still apply independently where an entity is a registered charity. The exact 2024 principle titles below are from the [AICD official third edition](https://www.aicd.com.au/content/dam/aicd/pdf/tools-resources/nfp-governance-principles/aicd-nfp-governance-principles-2024.pdf) and [AICD official overview](https://www.aicd.com.au/corporate-governance-sectors/not-for-profit/studies/2024-not-for-profit-governance-principles.html). The 2025/26 GOV slides abbreviate the eighth title as 'Culture'; the **published 2024 full name is 'Organisational culture'.**

### [06.14.02] Principles, meaning, board tests and proportionate practice

| Exact 2024 AICD principle | What it asks the board to govern | Original director question / evidence | Proportionate implementation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Purpose, vision, and strategy** | Clarity of mission and integrated choices that create meaningful outcomes. | Which programme outcomes actually advance our objects? What would cause us to exit an ineffective activity? | Annual purpose test and two-page strategy for small NFP; formal impact/portfolio review for large charity. |
| **2. Roles and responsibilities** | Clear authority between members, board, chair, committees, executives and volunteers. | Who can approve this grant or related-party contract? Does the treasurer have authority or only custody of records? | Simple decision and spending map; larger entities need board charter and delegation register. |
| **3. Board composition and effectiveness** | Competence, independence of judgement, diverse perspectives, director development and constructive decisions. | Which skills do we lack and whose perspective never reaches the room? Are director terms limiting renewal? | Skills and conflict review annually; larger board conducts formal evaluations and succession. |
| **4. Risk management** | Integrate risk with mission, strategic choices, people safety and resilience. | What is the most serious harm our service could cause, and how would we know early? | Top-ten risks and escalation matrix; enterprise risk/assurance architecture for complex delivery. |
| **5. Performance and accountability** | Measure and report financial health, service quality, outcomes, regulatory compliance and governance. | Are we reporting real outcomes or mainly activity counts? Are negative findings visible? | Quarterly impact/finance dashboard, accessible member update; auditable outcome evaluation for large charity. |
| **6. Stakeholders** | Deliberate relationships and listening to affected people, with lawful decision rights respected. | Whose voice is missing? Have we altered a policy because of beneficiary evidence? | Feedback and complaints loop; sophisticated consultation/co-design where needed. |
| **7. Sustainability** | Maintain mission capability over the long term: people, finances, environment, services and community trust. | Could we deliver our commitments after a 20% loss of recurring funding? What obligations are we shifting into the future? | Reserve/cash stress and volunteer succession; multi-year sustainability scenarios for large charity. |
| **8. Organisational culture** | Ethical conduct, values, speak-up, protection from harm and board example. | Do staff feel safe challenging a board member? Does our spending reflect our stated values? | Board code, independent complaints route and annual culture conversation; deeper site assurance where complex. |

These short summaries and questions are **independent explanations**, not reproductions of AICD's longer proprietary checklists. A principle is useful when it improves the board's decisions and habits, not when it becomes an annual tick-box exercise.

### [06.14.03] How to map voluntary principles onto binding obligations

A practical map is: Principle 1 supports ACNC Standard 1; Principle 2 supports Standard 2 and applicable entity law; Principle 3 supports Standards 4–5; Principle 4 supports Standard 5 financial/stewardship aspects and External Conduct Standards; Principle 5 supports ACNC reporting and member accountability; Principle 6 strengthens public benefit and trust beyond the minimum law; Principle 7 helps prevent financial distress and loss of service; Principle 8 supports proper conduct and lawful, safe care. **This is an analytical comparison**, not a legislative equivalence. Principle 8 cannot replace the **specific National Redress Scheme duty** in ACNC Standard 6.

### [06.14.04] A six-question annual effectiveness assessment

Invite all directors to provide evidence, not scores alone: (1) What mission outcome improved and how do we know? (2) Which major risk or incident changed the board's behaviour? (3) Which funding or finance decision was altered after independent challenge? (4) How did direct beneficiary feedback reach the board? (5) Which skills, role or behavioural gap has been closed? (6) What is the most important unresolved issue for the next year? Compare responses and commission appropriate independent interviews where needed. Publish a concise member-facing account of improvements without exposing confidential complaints.

[AICD] GOV pp. 41–50, GSH p. 15, RAPP pp. 23–26; 2024 AICD edition cited above.

## [06.15] Financial distress, restructuring, protected property and winding up

### [06.15.01] Recognise deterioration before the bank balance reaches zero

The board should distinguish **accounting deficit**, **operating cash burn**, **balance-sheet deficiency**, **technical grant breach** and **cash-flow insolvency**. These indicate different exposures. An NFP may be profitable but unable to pay payroll before a reimbursement arrives; it may record a deficit while funding an approved program from unrestricted accumulated funds. The operative insolvency test, duty holder and defence depend on legal form. Do not state the Corporations Act insolvent trading provision **s 588G** automatically applies to every incorporated-association committee or CATSI director. [AICD] LEG pp. 47–54; FIN pp. 90–100; LAPP p. 5 historical comparison.

**Early warnings:** forecasts increasingly rely on uncommitted donations; bank balances rise but available unrestricted cash falls; creditor terms stretch; superannuation or tax are delayed; grant acquittals are disputed; high vacancy or turnover reduces service capacity; restricted funding cross-subsidises unrestricted losses; debt covenants tighten; senior finance departures are unexplained; or the board stops receiving timely accounts.

### [06.15.02] A disciplined distress escalation process

**First**, commission a reliable, frequent cash forecast with confirmed restrictions and priority payments. **Second**, preserve accurate accounting records, donor and grant agreements and minutes. **Third**, seek entity-specific legal and insolvency advice early. **Fourth**, test realistic turnaround options against credible funding and service continuity, including member and grantor approvals. **Fifth**, protect beneficiaries, employees, restricted assets and creditors; stop inappropriate discretionary distributions, conflicted decisions and commitments that cannot be met. **Sixth**, decide and record whether an orderly transfer, restructuring, administration, liquidation or other lawful route is required. Charity purpose cannot authorise continued insolvent trading or misappropriation of trust assets.

A company's directors may need to consider Corporations Act insolvent trading and **safe harbour** provisions, including preconditions and professional advice; association legislation and CATSI law have distinct provisions, exceptions and possible personal exposures. The board should not assume a rescue based solely on reputational importance of the charity or prospective grants. [LAW] *Corporations Act 2001* (Cth), ss 95A, 588G, 588GA, 588H (where applicable); applicable association/CATSI provisions; `01_Governance_and_Directors_Duties.md` and `04_Financial_Literacy_Performance_and_Solvency.md`.

### [06.15.03] Restricted money and protected assets in distress

Grants, bequests, gifts held on charitable trust, DGR gift funds, land subject to conditions, security interests and government-purchased capital assets require **asset-by-asset legal analysis**. Amounts reported as 'net assets' cannot be assumed freely available to settle all liabilities. Some grant contracts require return of unspent balances or completion funds, while others allow termination payment adjustments or fund transfers. The board should obtain a **restrictions and ownership schedule** with instrument, legal owner, beneficiaries, permitted use, consent requirement, security, relevant financial statement liability and transfer rules. Ring-fencing in the accounts without confirming legal status is insufficient.

### [06.15.04] Orderly restructuring and mission continuity

Restructuring options include programme closure, revised contracts, consolidation with a compatible charity, transferring services with funder consent, voluntary winding up, or changing legal form. A merger is not justified merely because two charities serve similar people; different client safety systems, donors, cultures and restricted funds can make integration destructive. Test *which beneficiaries would lose service, whether another organisation can legally receive their information and grant obligations, and what funding follows the service*. Volunteers and employees need lawful consultation and continuity plans. A transfer of restricted funds must follow trust/contract rules and may require court, Attorney-General, regulator or donor agreement depending on the instrument.

### [06.15.05] Cancellation of charity status is not dissolution

A charity may voluntarily seek to revoke ACNC registration or face regulatory revocation. That status change can affect eligibility for concessions, public claims, grants and DGR endorsement, but it does **not** by itself extinguish the association, CLG, trust or CATSI corporation. A winding up can require separate corporate/association/CATSI processes, winding-up provisions and final ACNC/ATO/regulator notices. Similarly, a surviving legal entity might continue as a non-charity NFP if its underlying law, activities and restrictions permit. The board must map the sequence to avoid receiving new tax-deductible donations after DGR endorsement ends.

### [06.15.06] Dissolution and surplus-distribution decision tree

1. Identify the legal vehicle and formal winding-up decision authority; obtain independent legal and accounting advice.
2. Verify cash solvency, liabilities, employee entitlements, tax, grants, conditional donations, litigation and indemnities.
3. Separate **owned unrestricted property** from trust/restricted property, secured assets and property held for other parties.
4. Apply constitution/rules, state/territory law, charitable trust and DGR transfer requirements; identify recipients with valid purposes and eligibility.
5. Obtain required member votes, regulators' consent or court direction; do not assume donor silence is consent.
6. Prepare verified transfer deeds, acquittals, notices and final accounts; make the ACNC, ATO, ASIC/state/ORIC and public claims consistent.
7. Preserve records and confidentiality following deregistration; document responsibility for future disputes, client data and restricted funds.

**Board governance lesson:** a 12-month closure forecast and credible beneficiary transfer plan may protect more social value than attempting indefinite rescue with insufficient unrestricted funds. A charity's ethical duty includes acknowledging when it can no longer deliver safely.

## [06.16] Indigenous charity, CATSI corporation and native title distinctions

### [06.16.01] Four separate legal and authority layers

An Indigenous organisation may be **(1)** an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander corporation under the CATSI Act; **(2)** an ACNC-registered charity; **(3)** a DGR-endorsed entity or holder of other tax concessions; **(4)** a registered native title body corporate (RNTBC), also called prescribed body corporate (PBC), with statutory responsibilities for native title. These categories overlap but **do not merge**. For an RNTBC, the *Native Title Act 1993* (Cth) and *Native Title (Prescribed Bodies Corporate) Regulations 1999* can allocate decisions to **affected common law native title holders**, including persons who are **not** corporation members. See `07_Aboriginal_Corporations_and_Indigenous_Governance.md`.

### [06.16.02] ORIC, membership and director duties

[LAW] ORIC registers and regulates CATSI corporations, approves rule book processes and administers reporting and other statutory powers under the CATSI Act. Directors' duties are governed by that Act (including the Part 6-1 duty framework) and other applicable law, not simply by quoting Corporations Act s 180. Membership eligibility and rights follow the rule book and statute, with corporation-level members making many conventional decisions. ACNC registration superimposes charity requirements but does not make ORIC irrelevant, including director identity and annual accounts. [CATSI Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2006A00124/latest/text); [ORIC](https://www.oric.gov.au/).

### [06.16.03] Who can decide native title matters?

ORIC's [current RNTBC decision guidance](https://www.oric.gov.au/for-corporations/decision-making/decision-making-RNTBCs) distinguishes **native title and compensation decisions** from **corporate/operational decisions**. The RNTBC must consult and obtain required consent from *affected common law holders* for specified native title decisions and compensation applications, applying the PBC Regulations and valid rule book processes. Common law holders need not join the corporation to retain these rights. Examples requiring careful classification include Indigenous Land Use Agreements, surrender or other interference with native title rights, compensation applications and prescribed decisions on persons who are not common law holders becoming eligible for membership. The board may implement a decision but cannot substitute its commercial preference for the required holder consent.

**Three-question classification test:** (1) Is this an ordinary corporate management decision (e.g., staff hiring, general budget) or a decision affecting native title rights/compensation? (2) Which common law holders' rights are affected, and who may participate under the applicable legal and authorised community arrangements? (3) What consultation, consent, direction and certificate evidence does the law require? If unclear, **do not execute the agreement** merely because a majority of current corporate members support it. Consult suitably qualified legal advisers and the rightful community decision-makers.

### [06.16.04] Limits of importing ordinary charity governance

Common charity governance ideas, such as 'one member, one vote', an independent chair, majority voting, formal risk matrices or a commercially trained board, may be useful but **cannot override** the CATSI Act, native title rights, authorised decision protocols or local cultural legitimacy. Nor should the board infer that an elder, traditional owner, corporation member and common law native title holder are legally identical classes. The relevant community defines appropriate cultural practice; the researcher must not invent universal customs. [ORIC, common law holder rights](https://www.oric.gov.au/for-corporations/roles-and-responsibilities/native-title-common-law-holders).

Good governance may require **two-way accountability**: directors answer to members under corporation law and ensure affected common law holders have their legally required voice. Decision timetables must allow culturally legitimate consultation and accessible language/interpretation, while still maintaining truthful minutes, contracts, budgets and safeguards. Indigenous governance capacity building is not satisfied by replacing local authority with outside technical experts.

### [06.16.05] Funding, protected money and conflicts in an Indigenous charity

An Indigenous corporation may administer government programmes, grant-funded services, community enterprise proceeds, compensation and native title trust funds. These categories can be governed by **different ownership, decision rights, consent requirements and distribution restrictions**. The board should not combine them into a generic 'community money' account and use surpluses interchangeably. The fact that an initiative would deliver general social benefits does not itself permit native title-related money to finance it. Identify legal title, consent power, trust instrument, financial reporting obligations and bank/investment mandate for each pool.

Conflicts can be especially complex in small communities with dense kinship and business relationships. The goal is not impossible removal of all connected people from community decision-making but **identifying specific interests and duties, using valid culturally and legally appropriate procedures, independent valuations where appropriate and transparent records**. Confidentiality and identity protection must be handled sensitively; a generic public conflict register may expose protected information. Use independent local legal/cultural advice rather than making assumptions about families or customary leadership.

### [06.16.06] CATSI charity board checklist

Before each major decision confirm: CATSI rule book and ORIC obligations; charity registration and restrictions; correct members' resolution; native title/compensation classification; affected common law holder consent where required; cultural communication/translation; budget authority and restricted-fund ownership; conflicts and independent pricing; service safeguarding; annual ORIC reports and any ACNC/ATO overlap; and a decision minute that distinguishes **member, board, common law holder and delegate** authority. For matters involving native title, preserve consultation and certification records, not just an ordinary board resolution. [REGULATOR] ORIC guidance above; [AICD] RISK course pp. 16–18; GOV pp. 46–49; LAPP p. 5 (historical comparison only).

## [06.17] AICD teaching cases, financial exercise and an original WA board simulation

### [06.17.01] AICD teaching hypothetical: Positive Impact Centre Limited

**Source:** AICD[GOV, 2025/26 V1, pp. 103–104]. **Status:** fictional educational case, not a reported corporate failure or judicial finding. Positive Impact Centre (PIC) is a large NFP delivering services in Australia and the Pacific. Its eight-person voluntary board includes six non-executive directors and two executives. After a significant bequest, it expanded and invested in board education, governance review and evaluation. Nonetheless, stakeholders questioned blurred board/executive roles, high board travel and a poor reputation for discretionary conference attendance. Following a Fiji conference, directors sought reimbursement of a restaurant dinner involving directors' spouses, about **A$2,500**, despite a policy requiring family travel costs to be privately met. The chief financial officer (CFO) refused to pay; directors treated that decision as a challenge to authority.

**What the facts support:** the board may have made an imprudent and conflicted expense decision; policy requirements were apparently not reconciled with the expense; an executive who controls payments was placed under pressure; the board may have normalised special treatment for directors. The case does **not** by itself establish a particular statutory contravention, criminal conduct or completed accounting misstatement. Legal characterisation would depend on entity law, constitution, charitable status, actual receipts/policy interpretation and evidence.

**Original board analysis:** The issue is not the quantum of dinner alone. It is the authority chain: can persons who benefit from a decision validly authorise an exception, was the expenditure for organisational purposes, were interests disclosed and managed, and could an employee raise an objection without retaliation? An apparently unanimous board is not automatically an independent decision. The CFO's refusal may be a legitimate internal control rather than 'insubordination'.

**Board response:** suspend payment while eligibility is checked; obtain independent advice if the governing rule is unclear; have non-conflicted directors or members consider the matter using proper processes; protect the CFO from retaliation; examine conference value and travel procurement; review the policy on family expenses, conflicts and exceptions; reassess board-management role boundaries; and publicly/privately repair standards through consistent application. Record a balanced minute without declaring misconduct in advance of investigation.

**Transferable director questions:** Would we defend this reimbursement to a donor? Which policy and delegation authorise it? Are beneficiaries paying for insiders' private benefit? What would management be expected to do if an ordinary employee made the same claim? [Cross-reference] §§06.05, 06.13, 06.14 and `02_Board_Effectiveness_Leadership_and_Culture.md`.

### [06.17.02] AICD teaching hypothetical: Early Gifts Limited

**Source:** AICD[BE, 2025/26 V1, pp. 107–108]. **Status:** fictional NFP CLG board simulation; the African location and political event are elements of the exercise, **not verified contemporary events**. Early Gifts supports young mothers, has overseas programs and receives important funding from mining-linked donors. In the case, a fictional political coup creates reported abuse, safety risks, coercive demands for facilitation payments and conflicting pressure either to remain and serve communities or to withdraw. Staff commitment, donor dependency and partner actions pull in different directions.

**Board dilemma:** the mission has a compelling claim to continue support, but there is no entitlement to expose employees or beneficiaries to uncontrolled violence or to approve corrupt payments as a price of benevolent activity. A donor-funded programme can have a positive impact and an unacceptable risk profile simultaneously. A quick binary 'stay or leave' vote may fail to examine safer alternatives.

**Original decision pathway:** (1) establish credible, independent security, humanitarian, sanctions, anti-bribery and legal information; (2) identify immediate staff/beneficiary protective action and evacuation capability; (3) distinguish voluntary staff choice from employer duty of care; (4) assess external conduct, partners and resource diversion; (5) compare temporary suspension, remote delivery, neutral local partners, staged withdrawal, alternative funding and controlled continuity; (6) document prohibited actions and escalation thresholds; (7) commission a time-limited independent review; (8) communicate responsibly with beneficiaries, workforce and funders. The board should explicitly revisit the decision as facts change.

**Teaching point:** ethical objectives do not dissolve legal constraints. Grant and donation reliance creates pressure for principled compromise at precisely the time independent judgement matters. Cross-reference §§06.04.04, 06.10, 06.12 and `03_Strategy_Risk_and_Organisational_Resilience.md`.

### [06.17.04] Original, explicitly hypothetical: WA community arts association and restricted grant

**Fictional situation, created solely for this handbook:** *Harbour Arts Community Association Inc.* is a volunteer-governed incorporated association based in Western Australia, not currently ACNC-registered. It operates a small theatre and inclusive community workshops. Annual ordinary revenue is **A$310,000**. It has **A$160,000 bank cash**, which includes **A$45,000 unspent municipal project funds** earmarked for accessible stage improvements; operating liabilities of **A$40,000** fall due within 60 days. A new grantor offers **A$280,000 paid in instalments** to create an after-school youth arts programme. Delivery would require a part-time coordinator, new child-safe controls and a **A$60,000 matching contribution**. A committee member's sibling owns the only contractor who has so far quoted to refit the space. The chair wants to sign within 48 hours, believing a large surplus makes this a low-risk expansion. Two members have asked whether activity with children is within the present rules.

**What can be deduced without legal speculation?** The **$160,000 bank balance is not fully discretionary**; $45,000 is already subject to grant conditions, and near-term liabilities consume capacity. The youth program creates safeguarding, payroll and long-term recurrent cost exposure. On a simple annual sum, $310,000 + $280,000 = **$590,000 potential reported receipts** but **not necessarily $590,000 accounting revenue**: accounting recognition, grant conditions, year of receipt and report classification need verification. A Tier 2 assurance obligation may arise if qualifying WA revenue enters the relevant band. The contractor relationship requires declaration and a legally valid conflict process. The purpose and member question must be resolved from actual rules, not the chair's view.

**Board agenda and decision gates (original):**

| Issue | Specific enquiry and evidence | Suggested board disposition |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose and authority** | Current registered objects; youth-workshop scope; any alteration or member resolution required. | Do not bind the entity until legal capacity and any required membership process confirmed. |
| **Cash and restrictions** | Source agreement for the $45k restricted cash; bank reconciliations; 13-week forecast; matching fund source; payroll/tax. | Treat only genuinely available funds as match; demonstrate stress-tested financing. |
| **Grant conditions** | Signed grant conditions, milestones, eligible costs, receipts timing, clawback, procurement standards. | Approve subject to legal and budget conditions rather than a blanket acceptance. |
| **Revenue category/audit** | Recognised revenue forecast under AASB requirements; possible Tier 2 review; auditor availability. | Obtain accountant analysis; update reporting calendar before year-end. |
| **Child safety and volunteers** | Working with Children eligibility, supervision ratios, recruitment, transport, insurance, incident protocol and local requirements. | No delivery until safeguarding controls are evidenced. |
| **Connected contractor** | Declared relationship; legal exclusion rules; independent market pricing and tender exception. | Conflict-aware independent selection or postpone procurement. |
| **Member communication** | Valid meeting notice, affected members, beneficiary consultation, complaint channel. | Publish clear member information and record any votes required by rules. |
| **Exit and ongoing funding** | Cost of closing after grant expiry; worker entitlements; residual obligations; likely sustainable demand. | Set launch and shutdown triggers; prohibit unapproved unfunded continuation. |

**Original staged resolution:** 'The committee endorses further development of the proposed youth programme *in principle* as potentially consistent with the association's objects but does not authorise grant execution, contractor appointment or expenditure beyond the existing investigation delegation. The secretary is instructed to obtain rules/legal advice on objects and member approval, the treasurer to provide a restricted-funds reconciliation and 13-week forecast, and the safeguarding lead to present a verified child-safety plan. The interested committee member disclosed the sibling relationship and will not participate in contractor selection except as the applicable WA Act and rules lawfully permit. A special meeting will consider a final decision against documented conditions.' **This is a hypothetical process illustration, not a legal conclusion that member approval or a particular recusal method is always required.**

**Further scenario:** three months later the first instalment arrives and is coded as unrestricted income, while the committee learns that the grant contract requires unused funds to be refunded. The immediate priorities are to correct the financial classification with accounting advice, reconcile the restricted account, inspect whether any ineligible spending has occurred, prevent further misallocation, notify the grantor where required, assess internal control failures and document the outcome. The board must not retrospectively relabel the spend as 'mission expenditure' to avoid scrutiny.

### [06.17.05] Comparing lessons without treating fictional cases as legal precedents

PIC is mainly a **governing culture, conflicts and board-management** problem; Early Gifts tests **safety, overseas law and ethical mission trade-offs**; Surf Life Saving Queensland provides **real historic financial data requiring interpretation**; Harbour Arts tests **the interaction of WA law, governance authority, restricted money, capacity and safeguarding**. The common principle is disciplined enquiry, not a universal outcome. File `08_Directors_Toolkit_Cases_and_Practical_Applications.md` is the intended destination for full case exercises; this file contains an independently usable analysis of the relevant NFP dimensions.

## [06.18] Annual board work plan, proportional arrangements and practical checklists

### [06.18.01] Annual board calendar: work, decisions and evidence

A board calendar is a **control over what gets decided, reviewed and filed**, not merely a schedule of meetings. The AICD tool emphasises compliance dates, strategy and risk cycles, committees and contingencies; an NFP board must add service outcomes, membership, donor conditions and safeguarding. The below is an **original illustrative calendar** for a June year-end charity; change exact dates for entity/jurisdiction and ACNC exception. [AICD] TOOL pp. 3–11; GOV pp. 59–79.

| When | Principal board work | Evidence and approval gate |
|---|---|---|
| **July–August** | Confirm year-end cash, grants, restricted balances, auditors; refresh declarations and new directors' suitability. | Draft accounts; conflict/eligibility signed declarations; auditor plan; closing finance reconciliation. |
| **September–October** | Review annual results, mission impact, incidents; approve/receive audited or reviewed accounts if required. | Statement sign-off packet, management letter, impact evidence, grant acquittals, member communication. |
| **November–December** | Review fundraising ethics, tax/DGR status, contracts, strategy and reserve policy; prepare standard FY ACNC AIS where relevant. | ATO and ACNC status certificate; cash stress; governance and fundraising compliance map. |

| **February–March** | Deep-review safeguarding, volunteers/staff, CEO performance and succession, stakeholder complaints, audit actions. | Risk/incident dashboard, anonymous feedback, workforce analysis, training evidence, action closures. |
| **April–May** | Strategy and budget workshops, grants and sustainability stress tests, financial-reporting tier forecast. | Mission portfolio review, three-year forecast, funding concentrations, documented risk tolerance. |
| **June and each regular meeting** | Monitor restricted funds, liquidity, progress, grants, safety, litigation, compliance and significant policy changes. | Integrated finance/outcomes dashboard; exception decisions; minutes and actions with owners. |

For a June year-end WA association, the AGM/statutory association dates may occur **before** an extended ACNC AIS due date. Work backwards from the **earliest** applicable deadline rather than assuming a later ACNC extension relieves state obligations automatically.

Large ACNC-registered CLG charity** | Corporations Act as modified for charities, ACNC and sector/funder laws; group structures may add ASIC/other reports. | Integrated three-statement forecast; finance and audit/risk committees; service outcomes by programme; incidents and assurance by site; related parties; reserves and liquidity. | Statutory ACNC audit, consolidated financial/mission report, external audit findings, formal director and CEO reviews, cyber/people assurance, scenario strategy and membership accountability. |
| **4. ACNC-registered CATSI corporation** | CATSI Act/ORIC; ACNC overlay; additionally native title/PBC laws **if RNTBC**. | ORIC and relevant ACNC compliance, funds separated by purpose/legal ownership, conflicts, service and safety indicators, community accountability and culturally authorised consultation schedule. | ORIC annual returns/audits based on correct size, rule book/meeting review, distinct native title consent records, grant/tax confirmation; never substitute board vote for common law holder consent. |

**Two principles apply equally to all four:** the governing body must receive a credible picture of money and risk; and the complexity of controls should follow the **harm at stake and operational exposure**, not simply the size of the board. A small rural child-protection provider may require more stringent safety assurance than a large low-risk investment foundation.

### [06.18.03] Before joining an NFP board: personal due-diligence checklist

An incoming director should ask for **all of**: current governing rules and legal status; ACNC extract and ATO endorsement; member register or its lawful summary; last two years' accounts and audit/review findings; current unrestricted cash and 12-month forecast; top grants and termination rights; details of legal disputes, safeguarding incidents and insurance; conflicts and related-party register; board/chair and CEO performance record; delegations and reserved matters; director indemnity and directors' and officers' cover; fundraising licences and complaints; major property/security restrictions; and any CATSI/native title obligations. Check whether the board is seeking a new person **to govern** or merely to lend credibility and contacts to an existing leadership group. [AICD] TOOL pp. 41–46 and 60–66; LEG pp. 18–25.

For an association without formal reports, request bank statements and a current cash forecast rather than assuming the absence of an audit implies low risk. For a CATSI corporation, meet legitimate community representatives through authorised processes and ask who holds which decision rights. Consider whether cultural/sector competence and available time match the board's real risks.

### [06.18.04] NFP board pack: an integrated dashboard

|---|---|---|---|
| **Purpose and outcomes** | Beneficiaries served; sustained outcome rate; unmet demand; service quality/complaints. | Program lead and independent feedback. | Explain outcome deterioration, client risk or evidence gap. |

| **People and safety** | Serious incident escalation time; mandatory checks; volunteer/staff attrition; safeguarding audit actions. | Safeguarding/people leads. | Immediate protective, reporting and independent-review pathway. |

### [06.18.05] Model records: what 'good governance evidence' looks like

**A conflict entry** records person, relationship and affected decision, when disclosed, statutory/rules basis for exclusion, who remained for quorum, independent evidence, decision and review. **A grant approval note** records the purpose, governing object, contract version, restrictions, budget and 13-week stress, safeguarding, procurement, deadline and authorised signatories. **A safeguarding report** records incident ID, date, immediate protection, reporting obligations checked, independent lead, board notification, evidence preservation and action closure, without publishing unnecessary identity. **A meeting minute** explains the options and major objections, not a verbatim discussion. **An audit action log** names owners, due dates, evidence and verification that a weakness was actually fixed. **An outcome report** records denominators, collection method, uncertainty, adverse findings and changes made.

A board should not retrospectively backfill incomplete minutes or reclassify a restricted gift to fit a desired result. Records should be candid, contemporaneous, accessible to relevant authorised people and securely retained under applicable records laws.

### [06.18.06] Practical director enquiry: twenty questions across a full board year

1. **Purpose:** What measurable change, not just activity volume, did the organisation produce this quarter?
2. **Mission drift:** What would we stop doing if the largest funder withdrew, and why are we doing it now?
3. **Governance:** Which legal entity and exact constitution/rule book empowers this decision?
4. **Membership:** Whose member or holder consent is required and how is it evidenced?
5. **Charity law:** Which ACNC standards actually apply, and what reasonable steps are documented?
6. **Regulators:** Which financial and fundraising reports must go to ACNC, ASIC, ORIC and state regulator, separately?
7. **Conflict:** Who benefits personally or owes another duty, and have they been appropriately excluded?
8. **Restricted cash:** How much of the bank balance is legally available for general purposes?
9. **Solvency:** What is the lowest forecast unrestricted cash point in the next 13 weeks and 12 months?
10. **Funding:** What happens if the largest grant ends or pays three months late?
11. **Contracts:** Does service price cover full safe delivery and the cost of exit?
12. **Grant integrity:** Which acquittals or expenditure rules might trigger repayment?
13. **Control:** Who can create a supplier, change its bank details and approve a payment?
14. **Assurance:** What findings did the auditor, reviewer or programme evaluator raise that remain unresolved?
15. **Safeguarding:** What incident would require notification before our next scheduled meeting?
16. **People:** Who can contradict management or a founder without retaliation?
17. **Culture:** Which complaints were handled differently because we listened to service users?
18. **Strategy:** What decision trigger would cause us to reallocate scarce funds to another programme?
19. **Governance renewal:** Where are our skill, diversity, community legitimacy or succession gaps?
20. **Accountability:** What will be different at the next meeting because of today's board decision?
